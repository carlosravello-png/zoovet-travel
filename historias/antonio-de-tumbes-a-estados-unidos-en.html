Obama flies cargo to the United States (and that's why he's now called Antonio)

A Venezuelan family, a cargo flight in the middle of the 2023 CDC storm, and a name change that made us smile. Antonio, from Tumbes to the United States.

Told by Carlos Ravello · Case coordinated and verified by Dr. Jessica Camacho (DVM · CMVP 12434) · July 9, 2026

Trip file

Pet
Antonio (formerly “Obama”) — mixed-breed dog
Route
Tumbes → Lima (Jorge Chávez · LIM) → Los Angeles (LAX) → Tulsa, Oklahoma (USA)
Mode
Cargo — LATAM Cargo, flight LA2478
Context
New post-COVID CDC rules (2023) mid-rollout and changing frequently
Process
Microchip and rabies vaccine via a trusted vet network · serology · CDC Dog Import form · cargo and customs paperwork
Detail
The cargo flight was delayed a day: due on the 22nd, it arrived on December 23rd
Outcome
Reunited with his owner just in time for Christmas 2023

When I thought about starting this blog, this was one of the first stories that came to mind.

A Venezuelan family that, after a few years in Peru, decides to emigrate to the United States. The first to contact me was Carlos's mom: a patient, polite and, above all, understanding person. And I stress understanding, because right at that time the United States was rolling out the new post-COVID CDC rules, with regulatory changes that not even over there were entirely clear.

I remember the call:

“Pucha, no sabía… no, no la puedo dejar. ¿No puedo hacer más? No quiero dejarla.”
To which I replied, half joking: “Better give him another name; we wouldn't want customs to think the president is coming as cargo.” — Carlos's mom, and my reply (“Man, I didn't know… no, I can't leave him. Isn't there anything else I can do? I don't want to leave him.”)

And just like that, Obama became Antonio.

Antonio was a nervous dog —the kind that really is—, noble and even-tempered, but sad: he'd spent years apart from Carlos, who was already in the United States.

The problems weren't only logistical, they were also regulatory: the parole, the new rules the US itself hadn't yet mastered, the cargo paperwork. The CDC had rolled out a new structure —photos of the teeth, the new microchip-and-vaccine registry, submitting the form— and everything kept changing. It tested us, yes. But nearly ten years of experience back then weren't in vain.

We started by updating the vaccine. Obviously we couldn't travel all the way to Tumbes to vaccinate and microchip —that was counterproductive for everyone—. But for exactly that, over the years, we'd woven a network of trusted vets for these cases: licensed, responsible, impeccable. We knew they respected the cold chain and the standards. So the initial part was secure.

Once the sample was taken —the critical step— the result came, and with it the real start. Because the key, always, is solving the logistics. And here's a gripe we have after thirteen years at this: in Peru it's sometimes harder to fly a pet domestically than to send it abroad. Our mission is to make it happen anyway. We found the airline, we found the flight to Lima.

Then, sorting out the CDC format. Back then, being meticulous and documenting everything was an understatement. The United States adds extra challenges: different date formats, passport numbers with their own format, their own rules. And a detail worth keeping always in mind: as a country with state laws, some airports can add their own nuances to the requirements. That's why we always —always— carry translations and crystal-clear copies of absolutely everything. We also knew exactly which airport Antonio was landing at. A tip I share often: arriving through Miami is more comfortable for a Latino, but not friendlier. If you don't handle that detail, and if you don't speak fluent English, bear it in mind: Americans have a well-known culture of precision and economy of words.

Antonio reached Lima. From there, the transfer to TALMA, the cargo terminal. And the logistics you can't forget: diapers, feeders, water bowls, coloured zip ties —several colours in some cases, only black in others— and paperwork handled by an experienced customs agent who, above all, loves animals and cares about them. That's our case.

Cargo has its own rules, and we always warn clients about it. Carlos —a man with the patience of a saint— got to live it: the cargo plane, subject to its own rules, delayed its flight by a day. That's where having the right person is worth gold: Migdonio, our travel agent, went to bring Antonio his food on the day of the delay. And Carlos had to ask for one more day off work. We know most of our travellers are hard-working people; that's why warning them in time is a duty, not a detail.

From Antonio's real file

And after all the logistical chaos, what matters: the arrival. The flight was due on December 22nd, it was delayed a day, and Antonio landed in the United States on the 23rd —just in time for Christmas— and reunited with Carlos after years apart. Hard to think of a better gift.

The best part is that the story didn't end there. Carlos turned out to be as kind and polite as his mom, and out of it grew a client–clinic relationship that lasts to this day: every now and then I get a message telling me about the adventures —and the complaints— of Obama, now Antonio.
