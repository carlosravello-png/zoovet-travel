Sandra adopted them as puppies, left them with her dad, and one day decided to take them with her

She left Peru many years ago and yet she adopted here: two Peruvian dogs she left in her father’s care. She could have gone on like that for a long time. She decided not to.

Written by Dr Jessica Camacho García (DVM · CMVP 12434) · Reviewed by Carlos Ravello · 29 August 2026 · Published with the express authorisation of Sandra Ferguson to use her name and her photographs

Sandra Ferguson has been away from Peru for many, many years. But she adopted here: two Peruvian puppies.

She left them with her dad to look after. It was the sensible thing: she was a continent away, and that is how things stood at first.

What happened next is what usually happens. The puppies grew, and looking after two growing dogs took far more attention than she could have reckoned on from so far away. She could have gone on like that for a long time. She decided not to, and she wrote to us.

The process started at a distance: her on the other side of the continent, us coordinating from here and our affiliated colleagues moving every step on the ground.

First things first

The rabies vaccine. They already had microchips, and not because of the trip: they were fitted when they were puppies, as part of the breed registration for their litter. That requirement was already met, by chance.

After the vaccine you have to wait a month. It is not a recommendation: a blood sample drawn before that is worthless, and you start again. Once the month was up we drew the sample and sent it to Kansas.

The results came back fast, and good.

And there, with the medical side already settled, the real story began.

The sums

The plan was for Sandra to come and collect them so they could fly with her. But we did the sums, and a return ticket cost more than sending them as cargo.

A dog travelling as cargo is not an accompanied dog at a different price. It is a different file. Now there is customs, the air waybill, the warehouse, and papers nobody asks you for in the cabin.

And in Synaq and Chaska’s case there was one more, because of their breed. To take a dog of a Peruvian breed out of the country you need a permit from the State: the Constancia de Exportación issued by the National Committee for the Protection of the Peruvian Hairless Dog. The airline does not ask for it and we did not invent it; SENASA itself requires it, among the exit requirements.

With that permit in hand we could book the facility. Not before.

Why Washington

A dog leaving a high-risk rabies country cannot enter the United States just anywhere. It comes in through six airports, the only ones with a CDC-registered animal care facility to receive it, and it comes in with a reservation already made. Seven facilities in all, because Los Angeles has two: Atlanta, Los Angeles, Miami, New York, Philadelphia and Washington Dulles. No others.

That is why Synaq and Chaska’s destination was Washington. And that is why the facility is booked before the flight, not after.

We chose Pender Pet Retreat ourselves, because we had worked with them before. And I will name them: they are the best facility we know. Impeccable quality of service and a human warmth you cannot fake.

I know because I know the opposite. We have worked with another facility, at another airport, with people who — in my opinion, and this is mine alone — lack empathy and lack any real calling for this work. They are never available. They do not look for solutions. I am not going to name them, because my bad experience need not be another colleague’s. But you feel the difference on exactly the day you need it.

SUNAQ, with a u

With the facility booked, we went for the flight, and behind that there was a whole team coordinating things the client never sees.

And there something happened that still makes me laugh. Synaq was not called Synaq. It was SUNAQ, with a u. Someone typed a y on some form and the name went into the paperwork like that, and once a name is in the paperwork it does not come out. We told Sandra, expecting the worst. She said it was no problem.

And so, because of one keystroke, SUNAQ has been Synaq ever since.

She is one of the kindest clients we have had. Along with Diana, another client of ours, whose export process is still under way.

The day

We had a technical visit to Lima with other patients — Diana among them — and we built the trip inside that window. That gave us room for the SENASA certificate, customs and the rest of the papers cargo asks for, which do not get sorted over the phone.

We went to their house to collect them. We took them to Talma, the airport warehouse. Water bowls fitted, crates labelled, everything checked.

And there they told us the aircraft had a technical fault and that no pets would be carried on that flight.

Months of work, and a week’s delay all at once. I did not take it well that day. But I would choose it a thousand times over: a flight can be rebooked, a problem with the little ones up there cannot.

The small hours

The following week they really did leave. Lima, a stopover in Panama, Washington.

We were on it hour by hour, right through the small hours, tracking the flight, confirming the stopover went through and that everything was going as it should. You can have done all the previous months right and still not sleep that night.

And there is one more thing that was already settled that night and shows in no photograph: the sample we sent to Kansas. A dog that arrives without a valid titration spends twenty-eight days in quarantine at the facility. With a valid one, they examine the dog, revaccinate it and it goes home. That sheet of paper was the difference between Sandra taking them home that same day and visiting them for a month.

Four and a half hours

Sandra drove from New Jersey to Washington. Four and a half hours to collect two dogs she had adopted as puppies in a country where she no longer lives.

The reunion was beautiful. And it was the same day.

One more thing

Sandra is Peruvian. She could have spoken to us in Spanish perfectly well, and we ran this entire process in English.

That was my decision and I confess it here: every day I want to get a little better at it. So while we were coordinating permits, customs and a facility in Virginia, she was giving me lessons without knowing it.
