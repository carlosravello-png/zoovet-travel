Pacha, from Bolivia to Belgium: four countries and a 90-day wait

They adopted her on the Salar de Uyuni and the paperwork stopped dead. The obstacle was not a regulation: it was that nobody could understand anyone.

Written by Dr. Jessica Camacho García (DVM · CMVP 12434) · Editing: Carlos Ravello · 3 August 2026

Trip summary

Dog
Pacha — large mixed breed, caramel coloured. Shy and wary at first, very affectionate later
Family
Justine and Lucie, a Belgian couple, who adopted her while travelling through Bolivia
Route
Bolivia → Lima, Peru (Boliviana de Aviación flight) → Canada → Belgium
Starting point
The process had been attempted in Bolivia and was stuck: nobody could understand anyone
Framework
Entry into the European Union from a non-listed country: rabies antibody titration of at least 0.5 IU/ml, and a minimum of 90 days between blood sampling and the issuing of the health certificate
Process
SENASAG export permit in Bolivia · ISO microchip and blood sampling in Lima with our affiliated veterinary practices · titration at a designated laboratory · a reserve second sample · export certificate endorsed by the CFIA in Canada
Cabin
Too large to fly in cabin as a pet; she travelled with them accredited as an assistance dog
Duration
Around four months from the first WhatsApp message
Outcome
Pacha arrived in Belgium in perfect condition

Justine and Lucie were travelling through Bolivia when Pacha turned up. A large mixed-breed dog, caramel coloured, one of those who will not let you touch them at first: shy, wary. She started following them everywhere and never stopped. By the time they noticed, they were no longer deciding whether to take her.

They tried right there. They asked around, went to a clinic, looked into how you export a dog from Bolivia. And they came to a halt. It was not an impossible requirement that stopped them: they simply could not make themselves understood. They spoke their language, the vet spoke his, and no procedure moves forward that way.

They wrote to us on WhatsApp from Bolivia. We work in English, so that first conversation was a long one: the entire process, step by step, until they knew exactly what they were getting into.

First she had to get out of Bolivia

Pacha had been vaccinated there, but crossing into Peru required a permit from SENASAG, the Bolivian equivalent of our SENASA. And that permit had to be arranged by a vet in Bolivia who spoke no English, for two owners who spoke no Spanish.

So we placed ourselves in the middle. We called the clinic over there and translated the conversations word for word, in both directions, without summarising or interpreting, until it was clear on the other end which document was needed and what it was for. The permit came through. Pacha flew to Lima with them.

In Lima, with our affiliated veterinary practices, she was fitted with an ISO microchip and her blood sample was taken. That day we drew more serum than the laboratory needed. We always do, not because of this particular animal: thirteen years of practice are not for nothing. Things go wrong. This time they did.

None of this required having the dog in front of us, and that is the point. We work from a written protocol — what is done, in what order, with what margin, and what happens if something fails — and with a network of affiliated veterinary practices that carry out each procedure wherever the animal is. We trust those colleagues: we know how they work, which sample to take, how to store it and how to ship it. We provide the judgement and the responsibility for the case; they provide the hands. That is why we work nationwide without forcing anyone to move city to get started. And why we could take on a dog who was not even in the country.

Why taking a dog into the European Union takes months, not weeks

To enter the European Union from a country Europe does not have on its list — and neither Bolivia nor Peru are on it — vaccinating against rabies is not enough. You have to prove, through a blood test called the rabies antibody titration test, that the vaccine produced real protection: at least 0.5 international units per millilitre.

The sample is taken by an authorised veterinarian at least 30 days after vaccination, and analysed in a laboratory designated by the European Union itself. Then comes the deadline that surprises everyone: between that blood sampling and the date the travel health certificate is issued, at least 90 days must pass.

This is not administrative slowness. It is the period European law requires, and it cannot be shortened by paying more. The only thing you can do is start earlier.

The 90 days, split between two countries

Justine and Lucie could not stay three months in Peru. Their trip was continuing and the next destination was Canada.

So we took the sample in Lima as early as possible, to start the clock here. Pacha would wait out part of the period in Peru and the rest abroad, travelling with them. The wait did not get shorter — that cannot be done. But it stopped being a prison.

The wait, in two countries

The email nobody wants to get

We followed every step of the process as if the dog were here with us: the protocol and the safety measures demand it. Nothing was improvised along the way, it had all been thought through in advance. A client's trip is not optional, and neither are our decisions.

They were already outside Peru when the laboratory's email arrived. There had been a problem with the sample. A new one had to be sent.

Read like that, it sounds like a disaster. A family a continent away, a dog who could not fly back to Lima to have blood drawn again, and a 90-day clock threatening to restart from zero.

I felt nothing. We are prepared for any setback, and that is exactly what the surplus is for. The reserve serum did precisely what it is drawn for: it was sent, and the clock kept running without going back to the start.

Understanding the physiology of timing is part of the job: how long a body takes to produce antibodies after the vaccine, how long serum keeps, and under what conditions it remains valid for analysis.

And once it has served its purpose, it is not kept as a souvenir. It is discarded as hazardous waste: segregated at source, collected by a registered waste operator and accompanied by its manifest, exactly as Peru's Integrated Solid Waste Management Act — Legislative Decree 1278 — and its regulations require. None of this shows up in a photograph, but it is part of the same work.

I felt nothing. We are prepared for any setback. — Dr. Jessica Camacho, the day the laboratory asked for a second sample

The results came back, and they were satisfactory. The paper saying Pacha was protected reached them over there.

Canada put the final stamp on it

The last leg was closed by the CFIA, the Canadian Food Inspection Agency, Canada's equivalent of SENASA. For a pet to leave the country, a licensed veterinarian issues the export certificate and an official CFIA veterinarian endorses it — and that has to happen while the animal is still in Canada, because afterwards it cannot be done. The documents they presented were the ones we had put together from Peru. They were reviewed, endorsed, and from there Pacha flew straight to Belgium.

Managing time is not optional. Seen from the outside, all of this looks like logistical chaos: three countries, three authorities, three calendars that do not speak to each other. We do not see it that way. These are deadlines to be respected, out of respect for each country's animal health system; those dates exist to protect the animals and the people who live there. Knowing them thoroughly is what allows us to offer a way through in a process this critical, instead of simply saying it cannot be done.

A big dog who flew in the cabin

There is still the question of how she travelled. Pacha is large: because of her size she was not allowed in the cabin as a pet, and they did not want to send her alone in the hold. They trained her and had her accredited as an assistance dog, with proper training and certification, to accompany one of them. And she travelled beside her own people.

The dog who trusted nobody at first ended up being the one who accompanies. Nobody planned that part.

Accredited, and in the cabin

Pacha arrived in Belgium in perfect condition. Four months, four countries, two blood samples, three health authorities and a great many conversations translated word for word.

You imagine this job is paperwork and deadlines. Almost always, it is. But every so often the only thing standing between a street dog and a life beside her family, on the other side of the world, is someone sitting down to translate slowly and keeping one spare vial.
