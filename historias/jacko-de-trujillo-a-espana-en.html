Jacko flies cargo to Spain (and the European rules had just changed)

A well-mannered Labrador, an owner waiting in Spain and a European framework that had just come into force. Jacko, from Trujillo to Madrid, by cargo.

Told by Carlos Ravello · Case coordinated and verified by Dr. Jessica Camacho (DVM · CMVP 12434) · 9 July 2026

Trip file

Pet
Jacko — Labrador, male, 6 years old
Route
Trujillo → Lima (Jorge Chávez · LIM) → Madrid-Barajas (MAD), Spain
Mode
Cargo — commercial movement (EU Regulation 2026/848), LATAM Cargo air waybill to Madrid
Context
New European framework just come into force (in effect since 22 April 2026); cargo and airlines still catching up
Handling
Owner in Spain — everything by power of attorney, a 100% professional process
Process
ISO microchip · FAVN (rabies serology) at an EU-designated laboratory · health certificate (valid 120 h in Peru) endorsed by SENASA · clinical exam within 48 h under the commercial route
Detail
No airline for the Trujillo–Lima leg: a private, safe ground transfer
Outcome
Boarded healthy and arrived safely in Madrid, with Karen

Karen had to travel to Spain before Jacko did. And that, at heart, is the whole story: an owner who goes ahead, a dog left waiting at her parents' home in Trujillo, and the task of bringing them back together without anyone having a hard time of it.

Jacko is one of those Labradors who restore your faith in dogs. Big, six years old, well-mannered, a gentleman. He lived with Karen's parents —already elderly— and was no trouble at all: quite the opposite, loving, calm, even-tempered. But Karen missed him, and a dog misses too. So she asked us to handle everything, one hundred percent.

The first problem showed up early, and it's more common than it seems: there was no airline to move Jacko from Trujillo to Lima. An adult Labrador is a big dog, and space for pets that size on domestic flights is scarce. We've said it before after thirteen years in this: sometimes it's harder to move a pet within Peru than to send it abroad. The solution was as simple as it was careful: a private, safe vehicle with a driver who knows how to drive. Jacko rode in the back, spent hours without sleeping —we understand, he was away from his people and feeling the stress— but he behaved wonderfully. The driver ended up charmed by that machine for shedding fur and giving love.

And then came the most complex part. Days before the trip, the European framework for pets entering the EU had just come into force —the new EU Regulation 2026/131 and its sibling for commercial movements, 2026/848, in effect since 22 April 2026— and the cargo system and the airlines were still catching up. The detail that changed everything: because Karen was already in Spain, Jacko was not travelling accompanied by his owner. And under the new rule, a pet travelling alone stops being a “personal trip” and is treated as a commercial movement. In other words, almost like an import, declared value included.

So we had to sit down and study. At Zoovet we understand legality and the sanitary criteria —they exist for a reason— but a six-year-old dog who sleeps in a family's home is not merchandise. It was hours and hours of reading the rule and calling Spain until they explained it to us properly. And the good news arrived: yes, you can declare a symbolic value, as long as it is true. And in Jacko's case it was.

A dog that lives with the family is not merchandise. When Spain confirmed we could declare his real value —the symbolic one, the true one—, we breathed again. — Carlos Ravello

Since Karen was not in Peru, we handled everything with power of attorney and a one-hundred-percent professional process. And here is the crux I want anyone reading this to understand, because it's where most people stumble: the step through SENASA. On the paperwork, the document shifts from exporter to owner, each with their own credentials; that's why the power of attorney has to be flawless. The FAVN —the rabies serology—, the microchip and the certificates must all be crystal clear, without a shadow of doubt. And a critical timing point: in Peru, the health certificate for the SENASA process is valid for 120 hours. That's why everything is brought ready, forms clear, calculating the exact departure time; and if the flight is delayed —they do get delayed— those 120 hours give the margin to cover any contingency.

Jacko arrived in Lima. There he was met by Migdonio, our travel agent, and he deserves a proper introduction: good old Migdonio has been with us for thirteen years and has a genuine love for animals. Having him is, quite simply, peace of mind. He gave Jacko his walk to relieve himself, his good breakfast, and set about calculating the trip. Because once again the plane decided to leave a day later than agreed. No matter: Jacko can stay in the spaces we keep in Lima precisely for these contingencies. And being such a well-behaved dog, literally everyone wanted to spend the afternoon with him. Food, for health reasons, is never improvised: the exact ration, the timing, his water, his pads. All calculated.

Eventually we accompanied him to TALMA, the cargo terminal. And Dr. Jessica Camacho went there in person, for the commitment made to Karen and because she had to watch over his health. It isn't only affection: on the commercial route, the European rule requires the veterinary clinical exam to be done within the 48 hours before the flight. Jessica examined him and confirmed it: a healthy, strong dog, fit to travel.

From Jacko's real file

Jacko boarded well. We followed his LATAM Cargo flight from Lima to Madrid-Barajas and, on the other side, Karen was waiting for him. Nothing extraordinary happened —and that's exactly the point—: a big Labrador, a brand-new rule and an owner thousands of kilometres away, and even so it all ended the way it had to. Jacko arrived safely in Spain, home.

Karen was one of the most careful and grateful clients we've had, even from a distance. And that's the kind of trip we like to tell: not a feat, but a job well done, calmly, so that a good dog can go back to his family.
