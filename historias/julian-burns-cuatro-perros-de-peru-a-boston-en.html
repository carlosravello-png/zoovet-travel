Julian Burns' four dogs: from Peru to a house in Boston

He walked into the clinic with a black dog in his arms and a plan to take her to the United States. He left with four.

Written by Dr. Jessica Camacho García (DVM · CMVP 12434) · Editing: Carlos Ravello · July 10, 2026

Trip at a glance

Dogs
Osita (mother), Travi and Sharkie (her puppies) and Darrow (adopted in Cusco) — four in total
Route
Trujillo and Cusco → Cusco (Alejandro Velasco Astete · CUZ) → Bogotá (El Dorado) → Washington Dulles (IAD) → Boston, United States
Airline
Avianca, connecting in Bogotá. Sharkie in the cabin; Osita, Travi and Darrow in the hold, in IATA kennels
Framework
The United States lists Peru as a high-risk country for dog rabies (CDC): rabies antibody titer and entry only through an airport with a CDC animal care facility (ACF)
Process
ISO microchip · two-dose rabies vaccination · FAVN titer at the KSVDL laboratory (Kansas State University) · CDC Dog Import Form · SENASA export certificate
Handling
End-to-end advisory; samples and vaccines through associated veterinarians in Lima and Cusco; service in English
Outcome
All four arrived in Boston on July 9, 2026

Julian Burns left Peru with four dogs he didn't have when he arrived. This is his story, told by the people who walked it with him from this side — the side of the phone and the exam table.

The first was Osita. A black dog from Trujillo, with a litter of skinny puppies no one was looking after. Julian met her and couldn't get her out of his head. His plan was always the same: see a little of the country — Lima, Cusco — and go home, to Boston. Only now Osita was going home with him.

Before leaving, he tried to find homes for her three puppies here in Peru. He managed it with one. The other two — Travi, cream-colored, and Sharkie, black like her mother — stayed with him; and if they stayed with him, they were coming too.

The puppies in Peru

The first obstacle isn't in any regulation: no one lets you travel with three dogs at once — not an airline, not a transport company. So Julian did the Trujillo-to-Lima leg by road, nine hours by car, just him and the three dogs. We didn't go with them — our job was to prepare every detail so that trip, and the long one that came after, would be possible. In Lima, through our associated veterinarians, Travi and Sharkie's vaccination schedule continued, and Osita got her booster.

Julian still hoped someone in Peru would adopt the two puppies before he went on to Cusco. It didn't happen. And at some point, between one call and the next, he stopped looking: the two were going to Boston, to his family. He asked us to start the paperwork for both. That made three dogs crossing the continent. And then, from Cusco, one more call: he had adopted another one. Darrow, a Cusco dog, tan, so much like Travi and so close in age that they could pass for siblings. The same question as always, this time with the clock against us: can it still be done? It could. Just barely, but it could. And they were four.

Behind every photo there's a reason this takes months and not days. For the United States, Peru is a high-risk country for rabies. In practice, that meant each dog needed its microchip, its rabies vaccination — we use two doses, to leave nothing to chance — and, above all, a blood test, the FAVN titer, which proves the vaccine actually worked. Those samples, drawn by our associates in Lima and Cusco, traveled all the way to the Kansas State University laboratory (KSVDL), one of the few the CDC recognizes. All four came back flawless. That sheet of paper is what saves a dog twenty-eight days of quarantine on arrival. And there's a detail that stops more trips than you'd imagine: a dog leaving a high-risk country can only enter the United States through six airports, the only ones with a CDC facility ready to receive it, and with a reservation made weeks in advance. All four entered through Washington.

The process at Cusco airport

They left Cusco, from Alejandro Velasco Astete airport, on Avianca, connected through Bogotá and from there to Washington Dulles. Sharkie, the smallest, traveled in the cabin, in her bag, under the seat; Osita, Travi and Darrow flew in the hold, each in their kennel. We did the rest from the ground: the end-to-end advisory, the microchip, the up-to-date vaccines, the samples, the translations, the SENASA export certificate and the CDC permit for all four. We explained it all to Julian in English, because we speak his language, and for someone entrusting his dogs to a country that isn't his own, understanding each step isn't a luxury: it's the difference between worrying and sleeping soundly.

I'll tell you something that isn't always said. Julian didn't trust us at first. He asked about everything, came back to the same question in different words, asked us to explain things twice. And I understood completely: he was placing in our hands four dogs he had rescued one by one. That caution didn't bother me; it seemed fair. The lovely part came later. Week by week, answer by answer, that distance shrank, until one day he stopped being the client who double-checks and became someone who thanks you. In the end he told us we were his friends now, and that without us none of this would have been possible. You don't do this work expecting that sentence. But when it comes, it stays.

In the end he told us we were his friends now, and that without us none of this would have been possible. — Julian Burns, on his four dogs' journey to Boston

All four arrived in Boston on July 9. Osita, who was born on some empty lot in Trujillo, now sniffs the grass of a yard with trees. Travi and Darrow run together as if they had always lived in the same house. Sharkie, the one who traveled glued to Julian the whole way, now sleeps on the couch. We see them in the photos he sends — the four on a walk, the four lying down, the four inside their home — and it's hard to believe they're the same skinny dog from the first picture, the same litter huddled under a green mesh. But it's them. All four made it. All four are home.
