She wrote to us in German and we assumed a lot of things. She was nineteen.

She had come alone to northern Peru to teach at a school for children with disabilities. She adopted a mixed-breed dog, called her Alaska, and did everything right. And still the laboratory said no.

Written by Carlos Ravello · Veterinary review: Dr Jessica Camacho García (DVM · CMVP 12434) · 29 August 2026 · Published with Katharina Schomers's express authorisation to use her name and photographs

The email came in German. Precise, orderly, the questions numbered, not a word more than necessary. We reply in whatever language people write to us in, so we answered in German, and for several exchanges we worked with the picture you build without noticing: an adult, serious, who knew exactly what she was asking for.

Here comes a confession, and I am making it — not the company.

Thirteen years of sorting people into boxes

I have spent thirteen years talking to clients and planning their trips. The job leaves you with a curious way of seeing the world and, if I am honest, with mental shortcuts you use without asking permission. Every country is a different world. Sometimes every city inside the country.

A German has to be dealt with using the same surgical precision he applies to reading the rules: a clear explanation, no waffle. Once that is out of the way, they are among the kindest clients and among those who trust you most to get on with it. Russians start with a seriousness out of a film and end up with the best sense of humour I have come across, and with a "you sort it out" that is total trust and also an enormous responsibility. Spaniards arrive with an almost Latin cheerfulness and a manner so direct it throws you at first, until you understand that this is simply how it is. Brazilians with their joy, which does not desert them even at a cargo counter. The French asking, right up to the last day, how every single thing is going to work, in order.

None of this is in a manual. You learn it by doing the job. Thirteen years give you the world, or the world forces you to become a citizen of it. The world is broad and alien, wrote Ciro Alegría. I think that is true, but I also think it becomes more human and warmer when there is an animal involved.

The trouble with shortcuts is that sometimes they take you to the wrong place.

Nineteen

When we collected her details and saw the date of birth, we went quiet for a moment.

Katharina Schomers was nineteen years old.

There was no woman in a suit on the other side of that email. There was a girl who had crossed half the world on her own to do volunteer work in a country that, back home, many people know only through exotic stories or a YouTube video. She came to teach at a school for children with disabilities. She arrived speaking very little Spanish, and she learned the language while working, teaching, in a classroom where communicating is hard even when you share the language.

She was proud of that. She had every right to be.

At some point Alaska turned up. Mixed breed, cinnamon, exactly the size of a dog you can pick up in your arms. And by the time she noticed, Katharina was no longer deciding whether to take her along.

She did everything right, and that is not always enough

Microchip. Rabies vaccination. Blood draw for the antibody titration. In the right order, with vets from the area where she lived, with the dates written down. And something almost nobody does: she started about seven months ahead.

Hold on to that. Everything that follows rests on it.

The laboratory result came back below the threshold. Insufficient antibody load. The European Union requires a minimum of 0.5 international units per millilitre to accept that the vaccine produced real defences, and that number is not negotiable.

The first thing to say is the thing nobody says: that does not mean anyone did anything wrong. It happens to correctly vaccinated dogs, with the right vaccine, given on the right date, by a vet who did the job properly. That is why the test exists. If vaccinating were enough, there would be no analysis. We are not going to name any colleague or any clinic, because there is nothing to name: a low titre is a biological fact, not an accusation.

And then, the other thing

A low result can be solved. It is work, it is time and it is money, but it can be solved.

What was not in the plan was what happened next: a family emergency in Germany that required her presence. All at once, time stopped being a column of dates in a spreadsheet and became something very physical.

That is the moment when young people are young. Nineteen years old, ten thousand kilometres from home, with a dog in her care and a question that would not let her sleep: am I going to be able to leave with her or not?

At some point in those weeks she told me she believed a great deal in God.

I answered the only thing that came to me, and that I also happened to think: then He has put us in your path, and we are going to sort this out.

What we did

After saying a sentence like that, you have to hold it up with dates, not with encouragement. I sat down with Dr Jessica and we put the boring part on the table: timings, costs, contingencies. How long an organism takes to produce defences after a vaccine. How much every week weighs. What happens if it fails again.

The maths worked. With seven months of margin, it worked.

The D1 / D15 / D30 protocol, in three lines

It is the same one we applied in the ATON case, the Weimaraner who arrived at 0.22 IU/ml — 44 % of the European threshold — and came out at ≥3.46. It is published in open access, with verifiable laboratory report numbers.

D1 — primary vaccination with Novibac Rabies. The microchip has to come first. D15 — the booster, fourteen days later. This date is the critical element: it falls in the window where memory B cells are fully primed and the primary response has not yet declined. D30 — serum drawn exactly thirty days after D1, and off to the designated laboratory.

Thirty days of protocol. Then the European ninety-day deadline before the certificate can be signed. Four long months in total. That is why seven months was not excessive caution: it was exactly what was needed.

The second titration came back the way it had to come back.

Lima

We met in person when she came to Lima to collect the paperwork. We handed it to her ourselves, which is how these things are handed over when somebody has spent half a year waiting for them.

She arrived nervous about the flight home. It wore off quickly. From then on it was all laughter: she told us she had gone out to eat Peruvian food, and she told us in excellent Spanish. The same language she barely spoke when she arrived.

Alaska, meanwhile, in her arms, completely calm. One of the calmest and most affectionate dogs who has passed through here.

The day

On the day of the flight an animal becomes, for a few hours, a consignment. It is weighed, it is registered, it is sealed. That sounds ugly and I am not going to dress it up: it is exactly that, and it is why the work beforehand matters so much. The hours in which you can do nothing are the hours you already worked months earlier.

Alaska went into her crate, lay down and waited. We had warned Katharina, because it is the part nobody tells you about: the paperwork ends and the silent waiting begins.

Germany

She arrived well. She wrote to say thank you, which is the part of the job that keeps you doing it.

Two things from the whole case stayed with me. A very calm dog. And a nineteen-year-old citizen of the world, with a faith and a love for her animal that were worth writing down.

What this case leaves behind

Margin is not a luxury. It is what turns a catastrophe into an errand. Katharina's seven months absorbed a failed result, a full thirty-day protocol, a new titration and ninety days of regulatory waiting. Without that margin, the same story is told differently and it does not end with a photograph on a sofa.

A low titre is nobody's fault. It is the reason the test exists. Anyone who reacts in time, with a protocol rather than improvisation, reverses it.

And mine: read the whole file before picturing who is on the other side. The shortcuts thirteen years give you work almost every time, and the day they fail they make you treat a nineteen-year-old girl as if she were somebody else. We are telling it because that is how it went.

In the end everything turned out fine.
