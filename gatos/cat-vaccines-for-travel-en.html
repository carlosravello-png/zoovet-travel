Cat Vaccines for Travel: Rabies Is Not Enough

Written by Dr Jessica Camacho García, Veterinary Surgeon — CMVP 12434. Editorial review: Carlos Ravello. Published 28 August 2026.

What vaccines does my cat really need in order to travel?

Two things get confused here and they have to be kept apart. What the border demands is, in practice, the rabies vaccine: to enter the European Union from certain countries you also need an antibody test with a result equal to or above 0.5 IU/ml and a long wait, whereas the United States does not even ask for proof of rabies vaccination for a cat, although it recommends it. What the cat needs is something else: the three core vaccines in every country in the world are panleukopenia, calicivirus and herpesvirus, and those are not given for customs. The feline leukaemia vaccine is considered core in cats under a year old in areas where the infection is common, and in adults with outdoor access, and it carries a condition that no other one carries: only a cat that has tested negative is vaccinated, and he has to be tested first.

The two mistakes that complicate the most trips: (1) leaving the vaccines until the week of the flight, because there are administrative deadlines and because chronic stress is among the factors that compromise the response to a vaccine; and (2) giving them all on the same day, when a study of nearly half a million cats found that the risk of a reaction rises the more of them are given at the same visit. This article does not give doses: the schedule is set in the consulting room.

When someone comes in because their cat is moving country, the conversation almost always starts with rabies and very often stops there. I understand why: it is the only one that appears on the forms, the one that governs the deadlines, and the one that can bring a trip down if something is done outside its window. But reducing a cat's protection to what a border asks for is starting from the wrong end, and that is what this article is about.

I have set it out the way I set it out in the consulting room. First what any cat needs, whether he travels or not. Then what travelling adds. Then the schedule I work with so as not to pile vaccines on top of one another. And at the end the leukaemia one, which deserves a chapter of its own.

What any cat needs, travelling or not

The vaccination guidelines of the World Small Animal Veterinary Association, in their 2024 edition, define core vaccines in a way that is worth reading slowly, because they mention travel: they are the ones all dogs and cats should receive, taking into account their lifestyle and the places where they live or to which they travel. Geography is inside the definition. It is not an addition of ours.

In the cat, those core vaccines are three in every country in the world, and they protect against feline panleukopenia virus, feline calicivirus and feline herpesvirus type 1, which is the rhinotracheitis one. In practice they are given together, starting in the kitten no earlier than six weeks and repeating every three or four weeks up to sixteen weeks. In the low-risk adult cat, revaccination against panleukopenia is considered at three years and thereafter no more than once every three years; for calicivirus and herpesvirus, those same guidelines allow for revaccinating more often, even yearly, in higher-risk animals.

And here comes the first piece of information that will interest you if you are going to travel, because those guidelines place among the higher-risk animals the cats that go to catteries or to other high-stress, high-exposure settings, and they recommend giving the calicivirus and herpesvirus vaccine one or two weeks before the stay. An international move brings all of those conditions together at once.

Classed as non-core are the vaccines against feline immunodeficiency virus, against Chlamydia felis and against Bordetella bronchiseptica, which are weighed case by case.

The rabies vaccine is not just a customs formality

Rabies has a place of its own in this article for a reason that has nothing to do with paperwork. The 2024 guidelines recommend that, in regions where rabies is endemic, all cats be vaccinated against it in order to protect both animals and people, and they add expressly that this holds even where the law does not require it for cats. The 2020 American feline guidelines take the same line and classify it outright among the cat's core vaccines.

Hold on to that, because the clearest example comes from precisely the country that asks for the least. The United States, according to the information published by its Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, does not require proof of rabies vaccination to import a cat, and even so it recommends that all cats be vaccinated. It does require the animal to arrive looking healthy, it inspects cats at the point of entry, and it can refuse entry if there are signs of a zoonotic disease. And be aware: the destination state can ask for more, and Hawaii and Guam impose quarantine even on animals arriving from the mainland.

Out of that comes the idea that holds up everything else: the rabies deadlines are set by the border, but the vaccine is not given for the border.

Why I give two doses, and how far the backing for that goes

Here I am going to draw a careful line between what is a rule and what is my own judgement, because they are not the same thing and you deserve to know which is which.

In destinations that require proof that the vaccine worked, the cat has to pass an antibody test with a result equal to or above 0.5 international units per millilitre. And that threshold is not always reached. An analysis of more than sixteen thousand samples from dogs and cats found that some animals, already vaccinated, do not reach the required titre. Among the variables analysed were the vaccine used, the interval between vaccination and sampling, sex, age and country of origin, and in cats all of them showed a significant effect on the failure rate.

When the reason for vaccinating is a trip that is going to require that test, I start with the rabies vaccine and give two doses about fifteen days apart, aiming for a higher titre and less risk of an insufficient result that would force a repeat test and a restart of the deadlines.

Now let me tell you what I am leaning on, and also how far that support reaches. The evidence comes from a prospective study of nearly seven thousand Swedish dogs, where the proportion of animals reaching the threshold was 85.7% among those vaccinated once and 94.1% among those vaccinated twice, with a statistically significant difference. Three qualifications take a good deal of the edge off that figure. The first: when the authors adjusted for all the variables at once, the factor that weighed most was not the number of doses but the vaccine brand, and their explicit recommendation is to give two doses in large-breed dogs. The second: that study received partial funding from the two manufacturers of the vaccines being compared, something worth keeping in mind when reading that particular conclusion. And the third, the one that matters most here: the samples were taken between 120 and 360 days after vaccination, whereas the rules we work under allow sampling from 30 days onwards, so the results do not carry over automatically to our situation.

And above all, it is a study in dogs. I do not know of an equivalent piece of work comparing one dose against two in cats. So what I recommend rests on that finding, on the fact that serological failure also happens in cats, and on my own experience in export work. It is not a regulatory requirement, nor a finding demonstrated in the cat, and that is how it should be understood.

And now a point that is not settled today and that can cost you the trip. The rules require the sample to be taken at least 30 days after the primary vaccination and within a valid vaccination series in progress. When two doses are given, it is not expressly established whether that period runs from the first or from the second, and the difference is not a small one: if it had to be counted from the second, a blood draw done 30 days after the first would not be valid, it would have to be repeated and the wait would start again. So, with two doses, the sampling date is set by counting from the last dose given, which is what satisfies both readings, and it is confirmed with the laboratory and with the competent authority before anything is booked.

For the same reason I am not giving you a closed schedule in days here.

The three-month wait is not dead time

When the destination requires proof by laboratory test that the vaccine worked, as is the case for entering the European Union from certain countries of origin, the rules in force — Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/131, which refers to Annex XXI of Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2020/692 — establish that the blood sample must be taken by a veterinarian authorised by the competent authority, in a period that begins at least 30 days after the primary vaccination and ends three months before the date on which the certificate is issued.

That detail lends itself to confusion and I want to make it plain: the three months are not counted back from the date of the flight, but from the date on which the certificate is issued.

What matters for our purposes is that this wait exists and that it is long. And it does not have to be dead time: it is exactly the window in which to complete the rest of the schedule, with room to space the vaccines out instead of piling them up, and with the cat still in his own home, before the disorder of the move begins.

When the destination does not require titration, as with the United States, that wait disappears and the schedule becomes more flexible. There my judgement is likewise to space things out, leaving something in the order of fifteen days between one and the next, rather than settling everything in a single visit. I explain why in the next section.

"Can he have them all today and be done with it?"

I get asked this a lot, usually in those words, to make the trip to the clinic count. It can be done, but there is a reason not to, and it is not just an impression of mine.

A retrospective cohort study looked at the adverse reactions recorded after vaccination in 496,189 cats seen at 329 hospitals, across more than 1.2 million doses. The overall rate was low, 51.6 per ten thousand cats vaccinated, and the most frequent finding was lethargy with or without fever. But they found that the risk of a reaction rose significantly as the number of vaccines given at the same visit went up, peaking in cats around a year old. The authors close by recommending expressly that the number of vaccines given at one time in the cat be limited.

It is not that grouping them is forbidden, or that it carries a high risk. It is that, if there is time to play with, spreading them out comes off better. The feline guidelines themselves take that logic on board when they acknowledge that in young cats an additional visit will be needed for the rabies and leukaemia vaccines.

And there is a second reason, less obvious and to my mind more important when a move is involved. Among the factors that compromise the ability to respond to a vaccine, the feline guidelines list concurrent illness, inadequate nutrition and chronic stress. A cat in the middle of a move, with the house taken apart and the carrier in plain sight, is not at his best for mounting a solid immune response. Bringing the schedule forward is not a matter of diary management but of how well the vaccine itself works.

The leukaemia vaccine carries a condition that no other one does

The 2024 guidelines consider it core in cats under a year old in areas where the infection is common, and also in adults with outdoor access or living with other cats that have it. Outside those cases it becomes an individual decision.

What sets it apart comes beforehand and admits no exception: only cats that have tested negative are vaccinated, and the test is done before the vaccine is given. The reason is twofold, and it is worth knowing. Vaccinating a cat that is already infected does not protect him, because the infection is already established; and on top of that you lose a piece of clinical information that is decisive for managing that animal and for his living alongside other cats. In a relocation, where the cat is often going to be sharing space with others at the destination, knowing his real situation is worth something well beyond the trip.

As for the schedule, the guidelines put the first dose from eight weeks of age and a second one three to four weeks later, with a revaccination a year after the last dose of the initial series and, from then on, yearly revaccination in cats who remain at high risk.

The figure to have in front of you when there is a flight in the diary is how long it takes to protect. The 2020 feline guidelines state that two initial doses three or four weeks apart are required and that protective immunity is expected seven to ten days after the second. Between the first dose and real protection close to a month goes by. This is not something you sort out the week before the trip.

Why we no longer vaccinate at the scruff

I mention this without going on about it, because it surprises people who have been coming to the practice for years: cats today are vaccinated in the distal part of the limbs or in the tail, not between the shoulder blades. The reason is that in the cat there is an uncommon tumour that can appear at the injection site, and in those locations it could be removed with the margin it requires.

It is an infrequent and unpredictable complication, and it is no reason to stop vaccinating. The sensible thing is to keep an eye on the injection site with a simple rule: come and have it looked at if a lump there is still present three months later, if it goes beyond two centimetres, or if it is still growing a month after vaccination.

If you take away one thing

A cat who travels needs two different things that are best not mixed up. He needs to meet what the destination country asks for, which in the case of rabies can mean a vaccine, an antibody test and a long wait, and which varies enormously from one destination to another. And he needs, independently of that, to be protected against panleukopenia, calicivirus and herpesvirus, which are the three core vaccines in every country in the world and which are not given for customs but for him.

What I suggest is using the first need to resolve the second: start with the rabies vaccine and use the wait that the procedure imposes to complete the rest calmly, spaced out, and with the cat still settled at home.

Every animal is a case of its own. Age, weight, state of health, destination and the date the certificate is issued change the planning completely, and no schedule published on a web page replaces individual assessment. What I describe here is the approach I work with; your cat's schedule is decided in the consulting room.
