How to Keep a Cat Calm at the Vet and on a Flight

Written by Dr Jessica Camacho García, Veterinary Surgeon — CMVP 12434. Editorial review: Carlos Ravello. Published 28 August 2026.

Can I give my cat something to keep him calm at the vet and on the flight?

Yes, and it helps to separate two things that get confused all the time. Calming fear and keeping a cat still are not the same thing. The gabapentin used before an appointment is an anxiolytic, not a sedative, and that distinction governs both what gets prescribed and what you can expect: it has support in clinical studies and a legitimate place, but it does not replace careful handling and it is not there to “knock the animal out”. This article gives no doses, because that is decided in consultation.

For a flight the position is two sentences and you have to keep both: you do not sedate, in line with what the veterinary associations and the live-animal transport regulations recommend, but you can and should work on the anxiety with a prescribed anxiolytic, at a dose assessed and tested days before the trip, never on the day itself. And what works best is not the drug: it is getting the cat used to the carrier at home, and a practice organised to receive cats.

Of everything people ask me when they walk in with a cat, this is question number one, and it usually arrives when the cat is already inside the carrier and yowling. How do I get him to stay still. Sometimes it is for a vaccine, sometimes for the microchip, and very often for the one that makes everyone most nervous, which is drawing blood. For some years now it almost always comes with a second half attached: and for the trip, what do I do?

I have spent thirteen years seeing cats and I am going to give you the answer I give in the consulting room, which is not the one people expect. Your cat's calm is not achieved on the day of the appointment. It is achieved beforehand, at home, and a good part of it depends on you and not on me.

Your cat is not defying you

I start here because almost every mistake that comes afterwards is born of misunderstanding this part, and because when I explain it in the consulting room I watch people's faces change.

Your cat does not resist because he has been spoilt. The feline guidelines we follow put it in a way that strikes me as the key to the whole business: the cat evolved with emotions and behaviours it needs in order to survive both in its role as a predator and in its role as prey. It is both things at the same time. And an animal that is also prey does not experience a change of place, strange smells and being held as a passing nuisance, but as a genuine threat.

Once you look at it that way you understand why the cat who sleeps belly-up at home turns into a different animal by the time he gets here. He is not challenging anyone. He is doing exactly what his biology taught him to do.

And there is something else that matters a great deal to me as a clinician, because it affects the result I then have to sign. A cat who arrives overwhelmed does not only have a worse time of it: he gets a worse examination. Those same guidelines point out that appropriate handling makes examinations more efficient and diagnostic tests more reliable. Calm is not a kindly detail of the visit. It is part of the quality of what comes out of that appointment.

What you can do at home, and it works better than almost anything I do

Here is the most effective part of the whole process. It is also the part that gets skipped most.

The carrier cannot appear on the day of the appointment. If your cat only sees it when something unpleasant is about to happen to him, that is precisely what he will learn, and he learns it very fast. Take it out of the cupboard and leave it open in a quiet corner of the house, with a blanket that smells of him, as one more piece of furniture, so he can come and go whenever he feels like it. What we are after is that on the day of the appointment going in is his own decision and not the end of a chase down the hallway.

If you are going to buy one, choose the kind that opens from the top and comes apart into two halves. It looks like a silly detail and it changes my consultation completely, because it lets me lift off the top and examine the cat inside his own space, without dragging him out. Plenty of cats who would be impossible on the table let themselves be checked over this way without any trouble.

The car gets rehearsed too. Short outings that do not end at the clinic, spread out over time, so that getting into the car stops announcing the same thing every time.

And ask about fasting in advance, not on the morning of the appointment. If it is needed, we organise it sensibly, because in the cat, and above all in the overweight cat, going a long stretch without eating is not a minor matter.

The day of the blood draw

Cover the carrier with a light cloth, because the half-dark takes stimuli off him, and strap it down in the car so it cannot shift or tip over. Do not open it on the way, even if he cries. I know how hard that is, but opening the door with the car moving adds risk and does not calm him.

On restraint I want to be clear, because this is where the most harm is done with the best of intentions. Holding a cat down by force and keeping that hold is counterproductive: it increases the struggling, it ruins the sample, and it teaches the cat that next time he has to defend himself before anyone touches him. It is not how we work here, and to my mind it should not be how anyone works.

"Can I give him something before we come in?"

This is the other big question, and it deserves a serious answer, with no promises and no scaremongering. But first I have to explain a distinction that almost nobody explains and that puts everything else in order.

Calming fear and keeping him still are not the same thing

An anxiolytic acts on fear: the animal stays conscious and functional but stops experiencing the situation as a threat. A sedative does something different, which is to reduce the motor response and make the animal easier to hold, without that meaning the cat has stopped being afraid.

The difference is not a textbook nicety. The key review on pre-visit medication warns that studies use the terminology however they please and do not distinguish between a calming, an anxiolytic and a sedative effect, says that those differences matter, and holds that they should be explained to whoever cares for the animal so that person forms a realistic idea of what is going to happen. What you are reading is exactly that.

What gabapentin is and what it is not

It is a gabapentinoid: it binds to the α2δ subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels and reduces the release of excitatory neurotransmitters. The feline guidelines describe it without hedging as an excellent feline anxiolytic, more effective than the other pharmacological and nutraceutical options, with evidence that it reduces distress during transport and examination. They add something I find decisive: that it improves the quality of medical care, because it allows examinations to be completed that in a very frightened cat would be impossible.

The effect we are after is anxiolytic. Sedation, when it appears, is among its adverse effects, along with ataxia — which means walking unsteadily, uncoordinated — salivation and vomiting. And it depends on the dose: in a double-blind, placebo-controlled field trial, fear responses were reduced without measurable sedation, whereas at higher doses sedation was described as a frequent effect. It is the same drug doing different things depending on how it is used, and that is why the dose is adjusted to each cat and is not taken off a table.

And let me tell you in advance what you may see at home, because if I do not you are going to be frightened: in the key randomised trial, all the effects described resolved within the following eight hours. If your cat comes home a little unsteady or drooling, that is what we expect and it passes the same day.

Why I am not going to give you a dose in writing

Nothing above turns gabapentin into something for free use or into a decision to be taken at home.

It is eliminated entirely by the kidney, so before prescribing it I need to know how that particular cat's kidney is working, and the guidelines themselves say to lower the dose in small animals and in those with chronic kidney disease, frailty or debilitation. On top of that, the response varies enormously from one cat to another, so much so that the available studies have not managed to relate it consistently either to the dose or to age.

That is why you will not find a single dose in this article, and it is not an oversight. Whether it is indicated, whether it is advisable and what the regimen should be are decided in the consulting room, looking at the animal, and signed by whoever is looking at him.

One thing about efficacy that is genuinely useful to know: anxiolytics work better if they are given before the animal has become worked up, and at home. Getting ahead of it is not a matter of scheduling, it is part of the effect.

And a warning the researchers themselves make and that I repeat all the time: the drug goes alongside good handling, it does not replace it. A medicated cat, in a noisy room, in a hurry and with too many hands on him, is still a cat receiving poor care.

A detail almost nobody is going to tell you

There is a consequence of all this that is barely written down anywhere and that touches me closely, because I am the one who signs the export certificates.

A prospective, randomised, blinded study published in 2023 asked what happens to the neurological examination of a cat who arrives medicated with gabapentin. They found significant alteration of gait and of postural reactions, which is to say the cat can look as though he has a neurological problem he does not have. The authors expressly warn of the risk of false positives and of mislocalising a lesion that does not exist.

The other half of the finding reassures me as a clinician and is useful to you as well: neither the cranial nerve assessment nor the spinal reflexes were altered, and they remain interpretable even when the cat comes in medicated. And the authors' recommendation is plain common sense: if ataxia or a postural deficit shows up in a cat who received pre-visit medication, the sensible thing is to repeat the assessment on another day, with the drug out of the picture, before concluding anything.

It is one more reason for the person prescribing the medication and the person doing the examination to be working from the same plan and not each on their own.

Why you will not find dogs waiting in our practice

A good part of what upsets a cat happens before anyone touches him, and it happens in the waiting room. Dogs at eye level, voices he does not know, the smell of other animals and a wait with no end in sight, shut in a box.

That is why we see patients by appointment only and in spaces set up for cats. We do not take several patients at once and we do not mix different species at the same time in the same place. Each cat has his own slot and his own room, and we prepare the surroundings before he arrives: no noise, no dogs, no animals waiting around.

We do not do it for convenience, we do it because it changes the result. A cat who arrives without having gone through that anteroom arrives with a margin of tolerance that shows later in the examination, in a cleaner blood draw, and in the next visit not starting off from fear.

And there is something that helps me a great deal and that hardly anyone volunteers: if your cat has a history of reacting badly, tell me beforehand. It is not an awkward confession or anything to be ashamed of. It is clinical information and it changes the whole appointment for me, because I can prepare the room, cut down the number of hands involved, work in pauses and not force anything at any point.

And now the plane, which is a different kettle of fish

Here I want to be very explicit, because there is a great deal of confusing information going around and because this decision does not allow for improvisation.

You do not sedate a cat to fly

I will start with what is not done, because there is no nuance here and we agree with the leading professional position: dogs and cats should not be sedated for air transport.

The reasoning is solid. A sedated animal spends hours with a reduced motor response, in a pressurised cabin, with nobody to check on him and no possibility of intervening halfway. He loses the ability to hold his posture and to react to what is going on around him, at precisely the moment when there is least room to put anything right.

But it does not follow that there is nothing to be done

And this is the point where public information gets most confused. From "do not sedate" a lot of people conclude that nothing can be done, and that is not so. The very policy that advises against sedation draws the distinction clearly and acknowledges that medicating dogs and cats for air transport with an antiemetic or anxiolytic effect may be desirable or even necessary.

They are two different things. To sedate is to reduce the animal's response. To treat anxiety is for the animal to stop travelling terrified while keeping his ability to respond. The aim on a flight is not for the cat to travel motionless, but for him not to travel afraid.

What we do

We work with an anxiolytic, not with a sedative. Gabapentin, on veterinary prescription and with the dose adjusted in advance for that particular cat. No sedatives, under any circumstances.

And I will tell you with the same honesty what we do not know: specific research on anxiolytics in animals travelling by plane is scarce, and the very association that advises against sedating acknowledges that no studies on modern anxiolytics have been published in this context and calls for them to be carried out. Far from being an argument for sitting on our hands, that is precisely why the groundwork with each animal is indispensable.

The flight dose gets tested days beforehand, not on the day itself

This is what I most want you to take away from the whole article.

When a trip is planned, we do the assessment days beforehand. We weigh up how the animal is — kidney function, cardiovascular status, age, frailty — and we test his response at home, in advance and in steps, until we find the dose that in that cat produces the anxiolytic effect we are after without taking him into sedation. That is the exact line we are looking for, and it is an individual line.

That trial run does two things. One is to find the dose. The other seems to me just as important: it lets us see how that particular cat responds in a safe setting, instead of discovering it for the first time at ten thousand metres with nothing anyone can do.

The day of the trip is no time to be finding anything out. By then we have to know how he responds, at what dose and within what span of time.

So for the cat travelling in the cabin the work is done beforehand: real habituation to the carrier, a carrier that meets the airline's dimensions and in which he can turn around and lie down, rehearsals, the clinical assessment, the dose trial and an agreed fasting plan.

What I would ask you not to do

Bringing the carrier out on the day of the appointment, which is the most common avoidable cause of an impossible visit. Medicating on your own, with what was left over from another animal or with what worked for a friend's cat, because the response varies a lot between cats and the kidney has to be assessed first. Using sedatives for a flight. Trying the medication for the first time on the day of the trip. Holding him down by force, which ruins today's sample and the next visit. And leaving it all to the end, because habituation needs weeks and the paperwork for a trip needs a good deal more.

If you keep only one idea

That your cat's calm is not improvised on the day of the appointment. It is built beforehand, at home with the carrier and the journey, and in a practice organised to receive him without putting him in a room full of stimuli.

Pre-visit medication has scientific backing and a legitimate place. It is an anxiolytic and not a sedative, and that difference governs both what gets prescribed and what you can expect. It never substitutes for being treated well.

And when there is a flight ahead, the position is two sentences and you have to keep both: you do not sedate, but you can and should work on the anxiety. With a prescribed anxiolytic and with the dose adjusted and checked days beforehand, never improvised as we go. Starting the consultation weeks before the date is, by a long way, the most effective thing you can do.
