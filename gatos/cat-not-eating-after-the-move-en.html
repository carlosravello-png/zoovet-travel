My Cat Won't Eat After the Move: When to Worry

Written by Dr Jessica Camacho García, Veterinary Surgeon — CMVP 12434. Editorial review: Carlos Ravello. Published 28 August 2026.

How many days can my cat go without eating after a trip?

That he does not eat on the day he arrives is to be expected. My criterion, as a vet, is this: if 48 hours have gone by without his having eaten absolutely anything, the cat has to be assessed within that window, and less time still if he is an overweight cat or over seven or eight years old. The reason is that the cat's liver copes very badly with an interruption in food intake and he can develop hepatic lipidosis, which responds well to treatment if it is caught early. There are signs that do not wait for those 48 hours: a yellowish colour in the gums, the eyes or the ears, vomiting, lethargy or weakness, drooling, and signs of dehydration.

The two mistakes that do the most damage: (1) forcing him to eat, because there is a risk of aspiration pneumonia and of his taking a dislike to food, which drags the problem out for weeks; and (2) changing his food to tempt him with something new, when a change of food is precisely one of the described triggers. The literature does not set a number of days: the 48-hour threshold is clinical judgement, and each animal is assessed in the consulting room.

There is one scene people describe to me over and over, almost always by message and almost always in the small hours. The cat began eating less in the days before the trip, while the house filled up with boxes and the carrier appeared in the middle of the living room. During the flight he ate nothing, which is normal. And then at the destination, in a house he does not know, with different smells and different noises, he still will not go near the bowl. One day goes by, then two, then three. And the person writing to me is thousands of kilometres from their usual vet, in a country whose system they do not know, looking at an untouched portion of food without knowing whether that is normal or whether they ought to be doing something.

This article is for that moment. I am going to tell you first when you have to worry, because that is what you came looking for, and after that the why and the how.

Why in a cat this is not a whim

I will start with what makes the cat different, because everything else comes out of that.

The feline liver has peculiarities it shares neither with the dog nor with us. When a cat stops eating, his body starts sending fat to the liver at a rate that liver is not equipped to process, and the fat ends up building up inside the cells themselves until it compromises how they work. The condition is called hepatic lipidosis, and the standard clinical review describes it as the most common form of liver dysfunction in the cat. It adds something that is not a minor point: although many species can produce a "fatty liver", the cat is one of the few that suffers this particular condition, and it speaks expressly of how singular and how severe the cat's response to anorexia is.

And it finishes with the sentence that explains why I am writing this: if it is recognised early and treated properly, the prognosis is good; if not, the prognosis is serious.

I do not want to frighten anyone. I want nobody to arrive late because they did not know what they were looking at.

When do I have to start worrying?

Here I have to be precise about where what I am going to tell you comes from. The literature does not set a number of days beyond which you should go in: it describes cases with anorexia ranging from several days to several weeks. The threshold I give is my own clinical judgement, and I am presenting it to you as exactly that.

I chose that margin because the first twenty-four hours after a move are to be expected, and because at the same time I do not want the clock to keep running while you wait for an improvement that may never come. If your cat is overweight or is past seven or eight years old, that margin has to be shorter, and the sensible thing is to have it agreed at the pre-travel appointment, written down next to his reference weight, so that when the moment comes you do not have to decide it on your own and from a distance.

And there are things that do not wait for those 48 hours. With any of these, the appointment is immediate:

A yellowish colour in the gums, in the white of the eye or on the inner surface of the ear. Vomiting, above all if it repeats. Marked lethargy or weakness, a cat who does not get up, who does not respond, who stays where you put him. Excessive drooling. And signs of dehydration, such as dry gums or skin that has lost its elasticity.

Of those, three carry demonstrated prognostic weight and are not merely striking: in a study of seventy-one cats, lethargy, weakness and drooling were among the findings on arrival at the consulting room that were significantly associated with a worse outcome.

About the yellowish colour I want to give you one warning, because it is a reliable sign but a late one. The image that illustrates that clinical review is of a cat who arrived with two weeks of anorexia. Waiting to see yellow means arriving late.

What everyone does with the best of intentions, and what you have to not do

This is probably the most useful part of the whole article.

Do not force him to eat. It is the first thing almost anyone tries, and the clinical review expressly advises against it for three reasons: the risk of aspiration pneumonia, the risk of creating food aversion — a particularly serious problem in the cat, because it can drag the loss of appetite out well beyond the episode that started it — and damage to his bond with you, which is precisely what it suits you to keep intact right now.

Now, that sentence does not end there, and it is the second half that makes sense of it. The authors go on to say that the best strategy is to sustain the cat's nutritional intake by routes that do not involve putting food into his mouth, until he starts eating on his own again. Translated: I am not asking you to sit on your hands. I am telling you that the alternative to forcing is not in your house, it is in the consulting room, where there are procedures for feeding him without causing aversion or any risk of aspiration. Not forcing and coming in are, at bottom, the same instruction.

Do not wait to see whether it passes on its own. The review itself points out that when a cat with this condition finally reaches the consulting room, the likeliest thing is that he has already gone several days without eating, and that from there any further delay counts.

Do not change his food to tempt him with something new. It is the most understandable thing in the world and it is counterproductive, for the reason I am about to give you.

And do not give him medication or supplements off your own bat.

It is not that he is losing weight: it is that he stopped eating

This distinction is usually understood exactly the wrong way round, and it has immediate practical consequences.

The same review contrasts two experimental situations that make it perfectly clear. In the first, a group of obese cats had their intake halved under controlled conditions: they lost weight without developing lipidosis. In the second, a group of otherwise healthy obese cats had their food changed, stopped eating as a result of that change, lost between thirty and forty per cent of their weight over six or seven weeks, and did develop the condition.

So what sets the problem off is not that the animal loses weight, but that he cuts off his intake all at once. A cat on a controlled diet is not in the same situation as a cat who has stopped eating.

Out of that comes the piece of advice that pays off most in this whole article: do not change your cat's food in the weeks around the trip. Not before, not during, not on arrival. If they do not sell his brand at the destination, take enough for the first few weeks and make the transition later on, with the animal already settled and eating normally.

One in five cases starts with a fright

That a move sets this off is not a supposition of mine. A retrospective study published in Veterinary Record analysed seventy-one cats diagnosed with hepatic lipidosis between 2004 and 2015 and classified what the primary cause had been in each case. Digestive disease, pancreatitis and cholangiohepatitis added up to forty-four per cent; episodes of stress accounted for fourteen cats, that is to say twenty per cent; and in another twenty-eight per cent no cause was found. One in five got there from a stressful event and not from an illness.

An international move brings several of those circumstances together and, on top of that, chains them: the preparation, the journey, the arrival and the settling in are four anxious situations one after another over very few days, and often with a change of food thrown in.

The authors add an observation that describes well what happens in the consulting room: the cause of a cat's having stopped eating may be an obvious illness, but it may also be an event that is subtle to us and stressful to him, one that only comes out by asking carefully. In a cat who has just been moved that event is not subtle in the least, but hardly anyone mentions it unprompted, because they do not connect it with the loss of appetite. If your cat travelled recently, say so even if nobody asks you.

It also helps to know that not all cats start from the same place. Most of those who develop this condition were overweight before the weight loss, and in that same study advanced age was among the factors associated with a worse outcome, with a median of seven and a half years. A young, thin cat in good condition who does not eat in the first thirty-six hours after landing is not in the same situation as an obese cat of eight or ten years.

The first few days in the new house

That he does not eat on the day he arrives is to be expected. What matters in those first hours is making it easy for him to start eating on his own again.

Set up a single room at the start, not the whole house. A small, quiet, closed-off place, with his carrier left open inside. Hiding is part of the process and it is not a bad sign: a cat with nowhere to hide takes longer to relax, not less.

Offer him his usual food, in his usual bowl if it travelled with you, and well away from the litter tray. A little at a time and often works better than a full bowl that stays untouched and loses its smell.

Water deserves the same attention as food, or more, because dehydration often goes along with these cases and is harder to spot than the loss of appetite.

And watch him without crowding him. Above all, report what he actually eats and not what you think he has eaten. What matters is knowing whether he used the tray, whether he is drinking and whether he moves around the room when nobody is looking.

There is one further factor I want to mention: the feline guidelines on the cat who will not eat point out that previous bad experiences make the problem worse. A cat who had a rough time at the vet's before the trip arrives at the destination with less margin than one who was handled calmly. One more reason to prepare the move without rushing.

What we do before he leaves, and it prevents nearly all of this

If you had to take one practical recommendation away from this article, it would be to bring the cat in for a full appointment several weeks before the date, and not only for the visit the paperwork makes unavoidable.

The two get mixed up a great deal and they are not the same thing. The certification visit has an administrative purpose and is usually done with the trip already upon you. The pre-travel assessment is there to find out what condition that particular animal is in for the move, while there is still room to put something right.

At that appointment I go over the general examination and whatever tests are appropriate for his age and history, I assess his body condition and I write down his weight, which then serves as a reference to compare against on arrival. It is also the moment to rule out something already lowering his appetite without your having noticed, to plan the pre-flight fast individually instead of applying a general rule, and to have it agreed what to do if he stops eating at the destination, so that you do not have to improvise from a distance.

And one more thing, short but important: have a veterinary clinic at the destination tracked down before you leave, with its address and its opening hours. If the problem shows up on the third day after arriving, that is not going to be a good moment to start looking for one.

What to expect if it does happen

I will tell you plainly, because some of what circulates about this condition comes from case series published more than thirty years ago and paints a considerably blacker picture than is warranted today.

It is a treatable condition, and work from recent years shows that most cats who receive adequate nutritional support pull through. That support is the cornerstone of treatment: it is not a matter of giving a drug, but of getting food into the animal by a route that does not depend on his wanting to eat. And bear in mind that those studies were done on animals who had already developed the full condition and needed hospital care, which is to say the scenario this article is trying to help you avoid.

There is one finding from that study of seventy-one cats that corrects a fairly widespread idea: the primary disease that started the condition off turned out not to be associated with the outcome. What was associated with a worse prognosis were certain findings the animal presented with on arrival at the consulting room, among them age, lethargy, weakness and drooling. So what weighs most is not why he stopped eating, but what state he is in when he finally gets seen. Which is exactly where we started.

And I will end with something reassuring that is well documented: in cats who come through the episode, the practically universal observation is that it does not happen again.

If you take away only one thing

A cat who stops eating because of the stress of a move is not off his food and is not being difficult. He is in a situation his metabolism copes with badly and that gets worse as the days go by.

Prevention fits into two moves: that he goes on eating his usual food, and that there are no unnecessary changes around the trip. Monitoring fits into one: reporting what he actually eats.

And if 48 hours go by without his having eaten anything, or any of the warning signs appears before that point, the appointment does not get put off. In this condition, the moment when help is asked for has a decisive influence on how it ends.
