Otitis externa: primary causes, perpetuating factors and why it recurs

Otitis externa is inflammation of the external ear canal. It is rarely an isolated disease: it is almost always the symptom of an underlying cause that must be identified and treated.

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 12 May 2026

Definition based on dermatological clinical practice 2013-2026 · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

Inflammation of the external ear canal · Multifactorial · Strong tendency to recur

Otitis externa is inflammation of the skin lining the external ear canal, the ear flap, or both. It causes itching, head shaking, odour and discharge. Its key feature is that it almost never appears alone: behind it there is a primary cause —often an allergy, a foreign body or a mite— on which bacteria and yeast build up and perpetuate the condition. Treating only the visible infection without addressing the underlying cause is the main reason otitis returns again and again.

Formal technical definition

Otitis externa is defined as inflammation of the epithelium of the external ear canal, distal to the eardrum. In veterinary medicine it is analysed using the model of primary, predisposing, perpetuating and secondary factors (PPPS model): primary causes directly initiate the inflammation; predisposing factors increase the risk without causing it on their own; perpetuating factors sustain the disease even when the initial cause is controlled; and secondary causes are the opportunistic infections that settle on an already inflamed canal. Understanding this architecture is what separates a symptomatic treatment from a resolutive one.

Primary causes

Primary causes are those that trigger the inflammation on their own. The most frequent in dogs and cats are allergic disease —atopic dermatitis and food reactions, in which the ear is often the first territory affected—, foreign bodies (plant seeds and grass awns that lodge in the canal) and ear mites, especially Otodectes cynotis, very common in puppies and cats. Less frequently, keratinization disorders, polyps, canal tumours and autoimmune diseases are involved. Identifying and controlling the primary cause is the only way to resolve otitis in a lasting manner.

Signs and diagnostic sequence

The typical signs are ear itching, repeated head shaking, scratching, a characteristic odour, redness and discharge or exudate in the canal. In painful cases the animal may resist having the ear touched or hold its head tilted. Faced with these signs, the orderly approach is as follows:

  • History and general examinationAllergy history, frequency of episodes, bathing or swimming, and examination of the skin beyond the ear.
  • OtoscopyVisualisation of the canal and eardrum: rules out foreign bodies, assesses the degree of stenosis and confirms whether the eardrum is intact.
  • CytologySample of the canal under the microscope to identify bacteria, Malassezia yeast or mites and guide treatment.
  • Look for the primary causeIf there are recurrences, investigate allergy or another underlying cause. Treating only the symptom guarantees relapse.

Common errors

  • Treating only the symptom. Cleaning and applying a product without identifying the primary cause gives temporary relief, but otitis recurs as soon as treatment stops.
  • Stopping treatment too soon. Interrupting at the first sign of improvement, before the recheck, leaves perpetuating factors active and favours relapse and resistance.
  • Skipping cytology. Without knowing whether bacteria or Malassezia predominate, treatment is chosen blindly.
  • Cleaning with cotton swabs inside the canal. It pushes material deeper, can damage the epithelium or eardrum and worsen the condition.
  • Ignoring the underlying allergy. In dogs with repeated otitis, atopic or food dermatitis is often the true cause; without controlling it, no ear treatment is definitive.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my dog's ear infection keep coming back?
Because otitis externa is almost never an isolated disease: it is the ear's expression of an underlying cause. If only the visible infection (bacteria or yeast) is treated without identifying and controlling the primary cause —often an allergy— the canal becomes inflamed again as soon as treatment stops. Recurrence is the clearest sign that the underlying cause has not been addressed. This is why the correct veterinary approach targets the cause, not just the symptom.
Is otitis externa contagious between pets?
It depends on the cause. When the origin is the mite Otodectes cynotis (common in puppies and cats), it can indeed spread between animals living together. In contrast, otitis caused by allergy and overgrowth of bacteria or Malassezia is not contagious: it reflects an individual problem of the animal itself. This is why identifying the exact cause is so important before assuming there is a risk to other pets in the home.
Can I clean my pet's ears at home?
Gentle, external cleaning of the ear flap can be part of maintenance, but the veterinarian should first confirm that the eardrum is intact and that there is no foreign body or lesion that could worsen with handling. Pushing cotton swabs into the canal or applying products without indication can force material deeper, damage the epithelium or aggravate the condition. If there is redness, pain, odour or discharge, the right step is a consultation before cleaning.
Why does the vet perform an ear cytology?
Cytology is a sample of the canal contents examined under the microscope. It distinguishes which perpetuating factor is present —bacteria, Malassezia yeast or a mixture— and in what proportion. That information guides treatment and serves as a baseline to assess the response at follow-up visits. Without cytology, treatment is chosen blindly and is more likely to fail or for the otitis to recur.
Can otitis externa affect hearing or balance?
Yes, if it becomes chronic or is neglected. Sustained inflammation thickens and narrows the canal, and in some cases progresses to the middle ear (otitis media), which can affect hearing and, occasionally, balance with signs such as head tilt. Treating otitis externa in time and controlling its underlying cause is the best way to prevent it from evolving into harder-to-resolve forms.
