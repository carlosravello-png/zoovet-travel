Non-commercial movement of companion animals

Up to five animals · No change of ownership, no sale · And the 5-day rule

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 11 July 2026

Definition based on documentary practice 2013-2026 · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

Maximum 5 animals

The entire European pet travel system rests on a distinction that is rarely spelled out: the simplified rules —passport, certificate, titration— only apply to private journeys that involve neither sale nor change of ownership. That is what is called non-commercial movement.

It has three concrete limits. One: the maximum is five animals per person. Two: there can be no sale and no transfer of ownership. Three: if the animal travels with a person authorised by the owner instead of with the owner, the journey must take place within 5 days of the owner's journey.

Crossing any of those three limits pulls the animal out of the non-commercial regime and pushes it into the commercial one — which carries different requirements, different documents and different costs.

Formal technical definition

The limit of five. You may travel with up to five dogs, cats or ferrets. To take more than five, two things must be proven at once: that the animals are taking part in a competition, exhibition or sporting event —producing, for instance, the entry document— and that they are more than six months old. One of the two is not enough.

The written declaration. Anyone entering the EU from a third country must attach to the health certificate a written declaration stating that the movement has no commercial purpose. It is not a formality: it is what triggers the simplified regime.

The 5-day rule. This is the part most people ignore. If the animal does not physically travel with its owner but with an authorised person —a relative, a shipper, a friend— it remains a non-commercial movement provided the animal's journey takes place within 5 days of the owner's journey. Outside that window, it stops being one.

That rule exists to shut the door on the obvious fraud: without it, any commercial sale could be dressed up as "my dog is travelling with a friend".

Regulatory context in export

The distinction between commercial and non-commercial is not a matter of semantics: it changes the body involved, the documents, the checks and the cost. An animal under the commercial regime stops being a pet and becomes live merchandise, with everything that drags in behind it.

For anyone exporting from a third country, the critical points are the declaration and the 5-day rule. A dog travelling as accompanied cargo, with the owner flying on a different date, can fall outside the regime without anyone noticing until the point of entry.

And there is one case worth naming plainly, because it is frequent and painful: an international adoption is a change of ownership. However well intentioned, it is not a non-commercial movement. Treating it as one is the shortest route to having the animal held at the border.

Typical timeline from a third country

  • The regime is definedIs there a sale? A change of owner? How many animals? These answers decide the whole file.
  • The 5-day rule is checkedIf the animal does not travel with its owner, both journeys must be synchronised within that window.
  • Written declarationIt is drafted and attached to the health certificate.
  • Point of entryThe authority checks the declaration alongside the rest of the file.

Common errors

  • Treating an international adoption as a non-commercial movement. There is a change of ownership: by definition, it is not one. It is the most frequent mistake and the most expensive.
  • Travelling with six animals without proving a competition. To go beyond five you need two simultaneous conditions: an accredited event and more than six months of age.
  • Sending the dog with a relative three weeks earlier. Outside the 5-day window, it stops being a non-commercial movement.
  • Forgetting the written declaration. Without it, the simplified regime is not triggered, however private the journey may in fact be.
  • Assuming that "I did not charge anything" equals "non-commercial". What defines the regime is the change of ownership, not whether money was involved.

Frequently asked questions

How many pets can I take to the European Union?
Up to five dogs, cats or ferrets per person. To take more than five you must prove two things at once: that they are taking part in a competition, exhibition or sporting event —with their entry document, for example— and that they are more than six months old. Only one of the two conditions is not enough.
I adopted a dog abroad. Is that a non-commercial movement?
No. An adoption involves a change of ownership, and the simplified pet travel rules only apply to private journeys with no change of ownership and no sale. It is the most frequent mistake and the most expensive one: the animal may be held at the point of entry.
My dog travels with a friend and I arrive later. Does that still work?
It works if both journeys take place within a window of 5 days. The rule allows the animal to travel under the responsibility of a person authorised by the owner, but it requires the animal's journey to take place within 5 days of the owner's journey. Outside that window, it stops being a non-commercial movement.
What is the written declaration?
A document attached to the animal's health certificate declaring that its movement has no commercial purpose. It is what triggers the simplified regime, and it is also required when the animal travels with an authorised person instead of with its owner.
I did not charge anything for the dog. Does that make it non-commercial?
Not necessarily. What defines the regime is not whether money changed hands, but whether there is a change of ownership. A dog given away changes owner just as a dog sold does — and in both cases it falls outside the non-commercial regime.
