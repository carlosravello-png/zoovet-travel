Glaucoma: raised intraocular pressure, a sight emergency and treatment in dogs and cats

Glaucoma is a rise in intraocular pressure that damages the optic nerve and retina. It is an ophthalmic emergency: vision can be lost within hours.

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 23 July 2026

Definition based on veterinary clinical practice · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

Raised intraocular pressure · Ophthalmic emergency · Vision is lost fast

Glaucoma is a group of eye diseases characterised by a rise in intraocular pressure (IOP) that progressively damages the optic nerve and the retina. When the pressure rises acutely, vision can be lost within hours and the damage is often irreversible. That is why acute glaucoma is an emergency: it needs immediate veterinary assessment to measure the pressure, lower it and relieve pain.

Formal technical definition

Under normal conditions the eye continuously produces and drains aqueous humour, and that balance keeps intraocular pressure within an approximate range of 10 to 25 mmHg in dogs and cats. Glaucoma occurs when drainage of aqueous humour through the iridocorneal angle is impaired and the pressure rises above that range. Sustained pressure compresses and damages the retinal ganglion cells and the optic nerve fibres; if it is not controlled in time, vision loss progresses to blindness. The extent of the damage depends both on how high the pressure rises and on how long it stays elevated, which is why prompt care matters so much.

Warning signs

Acute glaucoma usually presents in a striking and painful way. The signs to watch for are: a red eye (congestion of the surface blood vessels), pain —which in dogs and cats may appear as lethargy, rubbing the eye, squinting or loss of appetite—, a cloudy or bluish cornea from oedema, an enlarged eye (buphthalmos) in more advanced stages, a dilated pupil that responds poorly to light, and loss of vision, which the animal may show by bumping into objects or seeming disoriented. If any of these appear —especially if they come on suddenly— the pet should be seen immediately.

Why it is an emergency: what happens and what to do

  • Acute signs appearRed, painful eye, bluish cornea, sometimes a dilated pupil. This is the moment to act.
  • Pressure damages the optic nerveEvery hour with a raised IOP increases the risk of irreversible vision loss.
  • Go to the vet immediatelyAcute glaucoma does not wait: it should not be put off until the next morning when signs are clear.
  • Diagnosis: tonometryConfirmed by measuring intraocular pressure; the eye is also examined to point to the cause.
  • Lower the pressure and relieve painTreatment begins to reduce the IOP and, in secondary glaucoma, to treat the underlying cause.

Common mistakes

  • Mistaking the red eye for a trivial conjunctivitis. A red, painful eye with a bluish cornea can be glaucoma; waiting "to see if it improves" risks the sight.
  • Delaying the visit. Vision lost to glaucoma is usually not recovered; time is the factor that weighs most on the outcome.
  • Stopping treatment when the eye "improves". The pressure can rise again; monitoring must continue as directed by the vet.
  • Ignoring the fellow eye in primary glaucoma. The second eye is at risk and usually needs monitoring or preventive measures.
  • Using eye drops without veterinary guidance. Some products can worsen certain glaucomas; ocular medication must be prescribed by the professional.

Diagnosis and approach

The diagnosis relies on measuring intraocular pressure (tonometry), together with examination of the eye to identify signs of damage and possible causes. The approach has two immediate goals: to lower the intraocular pressure and to relieve pain. In secondary glaucoma the underlying disease is also treated (for example uveitis or lens luxation). Depending on the case and the response, the specialist may consider medical or surgical options; in blind and painful eyes, comfort-focused procedures are prioritised. The treatment plan and doses are set by the veterinarian after examination. At Zoovet Travel we assess and treat it in our veterinary ophthalmology service, which is where we handle it.

Frequently asked questions

Is glaucoma an emergency in dogs and cats?
Yes. Acute glaucoma is an ophthalmic emergency. The rise in intraocular pressure damages the optic nerve and retina, and vision can be lost within hours. If you see a red, painful eye, a bluish cornea or an enlarged eye, take your pet to the vet immediately so the pressure can be measured and treated.
How do I know if my pet has glaucoma?
Common signs are a red eye, pain (which may show as lethargy, rubbing the eye or loss of appetite), a cloudy or bluish cornea, an enlarged eye, a dilated pupil that does not respond to light, and loss of vision. If you notice any of these, seek care immediately. The diagnosis is confirmed by measuring the intraocular pressure with tonometry.
Can vision lost to glaucoma be recovered?
Usually not. The optic nerve and retinal damage caused by glaucoma is often irreversible, which is why speed is decisive. Treatment aims to preserve remaining vision, lower the intraocular pressure and relieve pain. The sooner it is treated, the better the chance of keeping useful vision.
What is the difference between primary and secondary glaucoma?
Primary glaucoma has an inherited basis: an abnormal drainage angle in predisposed breeds, and over time it tends to affect both eyes. Secondary glaucoma results from another eye disease such as uveitis, lens luxation, intraocular tumours or bleeding inside the eye; it is the most common form in cats. In secondary glaucoma, besides lowering the pressure, the underlying cause must also be treated.
How is glaucoma treated?
The immediate goal is to lower the intraocular pressure and relieve pain with medication prescribed by the veterinarian. In secondary glaucoma the underlying disease is also treated. Some cases require surgery and, in blind and painful eyes, comfort-focused procedures. The treatment plan and doses are set by the specialist after examination; ocular medication should not be given without veterinary guidance.
