Diabetes mellitus in dogs and cats: signs, diagnosis and control

Diabetes mellitus is one of the most common endocrine disorders in dogs and cats. It is controllable with early diagnosis and consistent follow-up, and many patients lead a normal life.

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 12 May 2026

Definition based on documentary practice 2013-2026 · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

Endocrine disorder · Dogs and cats · Controllable with follow-up

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic disease in which the body cannot properly regulate blood glucose. It results from an insulin deficiency (the pancreas does not produce enough) or insulin resistance (cells respond poorly to it), leading to sustained hyperglycaemia. It is a controllable condition: with treatment and periodic follow-up, many dogs and cats maintain a good quality of life.

Formal technical definition

Diabetes mellitus is an endocrine-metabolic disorder characterised by chronic hyperglycaemia arising from an absolute or relative insulin deficiency, or from an insufficient tissue response to this hormone. Insulin allows glucose to enter cells and be used for energy; when it fails, glucose accumulates in the blood and is ultimately excreted in the urine (glucosuria). In dogs, insulin deficiency from loss of pancreatic beta cells predominates, whereas in cats insulin resistance associated with factors such as excess weight is usually involved. Management belongs to the field of veterinary endocrinology and must always be directed by a veterinarian.

Clinical signs

The most characteristic signs are the triad of polyuria (increased urination), polydipsia (increased thirst and water intake) and polyphagia (increased appetite) accompanied by weight loss despite eating well. Owners often notice the animal drinking and urinating more than usual, or losing weight for no apparent reason. In advanced stages there may be lethargy, a dull coat, cataracts in dogs or hind-limb weakness in cats. The appearance of these signs warrants a veterinary evaluation, because other diseases can produce a similar picture.

Diagnosis and follow-up

  • Observation of signsThe owner notices more thirst, more urination, more appetite with weight loss.
  • Clinical evaluationClinical history and complete physical examination by the veterinarian.
  • Blood glucose and glucosuriaHigh blood glucose together with glucose in the urine guide the diagnosis.
  • FructosamineConfirms that hyperglycaemia is sustained and not a transient stress spike.
  • Treatment and periodic controlRegular follow-up to adjust management and monitor progress.

Common errors

  • Mistaking the signs for ageing. Drinking and urinating a lot or losing weight are not a normal part of getting older; they deserve a veterinary evaluation.
  • Stopping treatment on your own. Suspending or changing therapy without monitoring can destabilise the patient. Any change should be decided by the veterinarian.
  • Skipping periodic check-ups. Diabetes is dynamic: without regular follow-up it is impossible to know whether management is still adequate.
  • Changing diet or routine without supervision. Feeding and timing influence glycaemic control; changes should be coordinated with the veterinarian.
  • Assuming a diabetes diagnosis means a poor quality of life. This is a myth: with proper control, many diabetic dogs and cats lead an active, long life.

Frequently asked questions

Can diabetes mellitus in dogs and cats be cured?
In dogs it is usually a lifelong condition that requires ongoing treatment, but it is controllable with proper follow-up. In cats, some patients can enter remission (no longer needing insulin) when the diagnosis is early and metabolic control is good, although it may recur. In both cases the goal is to control blood glucose and clinical signs, not a guaranteed cure.
What are the first signs of diabetes in dogs and cats?
The classic signs are increased urination (polyuria), increased thirst (polydipsia) and increased appetite (polyphagia) together with weight loss despite eating well. Lethargy or a dull coat may also appear. These signs warrant a veterinary consultation, since other diseases can produce a similar picture.
How is diabetes mellitus diagnosed?
Diagnosis is based on the combination of clinical signs consistent with persistent hyperglycaemia (high blood glucose) and glucosuria (glucose in the urine). Because stress can transiently raise blood glucose, especially in cats, fructosamine is used to confirm that hyperglycaemia is sustained. Interpretation always corresponds to the veterinarian.
Can a diabetic dog or cat lead a normal life?
Yes. With a correct diagnosis, treatment and periodic follow-up, many diabetic dogs and cats live an active, good-quality life for years. The key lies in consistent monitoring, a stable feeding routine and regular veterinary check-ups.
How often should a diabetic patient be monitored?
Periodic control is essential and its frequency is defined by the veterinarian for each case: at the start check-ups are usually more frequent until the patient is stabilised, and are then spaced out with regular reassessments. Follow-up combines home observation of signs (water intake, appetite, weight) with clinical and laboratory evaluations.
