Double rabies vaccination — the Japanese requirement

⚠ Standing rule · Japan · 11/07/2026

Japan does not accept certain microchips, nor RNA vaccines. Those beginning with “900 202” have been rejected since 2023, duplicates exist in seven other series, and the first vaccination must be given at 91 days — not 84. Read the full rule →

Two doses or more · The first at 91 days, not at 84 · RNA vaccines are not accepted

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 11 July 2026

Definition based on documentary practice 2013-2026 · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

2 doses · from 91 days

Almost everyone has one idea in mind: "the dog is vaccinated against rabies". Japan has another: two doses or more, both after the microchip, with exact ages and intervals — and with types of vaccine that count and types that do not.

The first vaccination must be given when the animal is at least 91 days old (the day of birth counts as day 0). Not 12 weeks. Not 84 days. 91. The AQS itself warns of it: in other countries the first dose may be given between 84 and 90 days, but under Japanese regulations a vaccine given before 91 days is invalid.

The second, at least 30 days after the first — counting the first as day 0 — and within its period of efficacy.

And there is one type of vaccine Japan expressly rejects: RNA rabies vaccines, already in use in some countries. Live-virus vaccines are rejected too.

Formal technical definition

Period of efficacy, not expiry date. The AQS draws a distinction almost no owner knows about: what counts is not the expiry date printed on the vial, but the duration of immunity the manufacturer declares for that vaccine. Every deadline is counted against that.

Accepted vaccines: inactivated (killed virus) or recombinant/modified. Rejected vaccines: RNA and live-virus. The AQS explicitly asks that the type of vaccine used be checked — because in some countries RNA vaccines are already being administered, and whoever receives one falls outside the Japanese system without knowing it.

If the period of efficacy expires before arrival in Japan, an additional dose must be given within that period. A vaccine administered after the previous one has lapsed does not count as an additional dose: the AQS forces the owner to start the whole procedure again from the first vaccination. This is the vaccination lapse, in its most expensive form.

And the order is sacred: a rabies vaccine given before the microchip is invalid — although the AQS allows that it may be accepted under certain documented conditions.

Regulatory context in export

This is the requirement that disqualifies files most quietly, because the problem is not what is missing but what was already done wrong, months earlier, at a clinic that had no idea the destination would be Japan.

A puppy vaccinated at 12 weeks anywhere in the world holds a vaccination that is perfectly valid for Europe, for the United States and for almost everywhere else. For Japan, it does not exist. And there is no way to fix it other than revaccinating and restarting the clocks — which, on the Japanese calendar, means another 180 days.

Hence an operational rule that holds for any destination, and that this requirement illustrates better than any other: the first decision of an international move is not the ticket, it is the vaccine. If there is any possibility — however remote — that the destination might be Japan, the first dose goes in at 91 days, not at 84. Waiting a week costs nothing; not having waited costs six months.

Common errors

  • Vaccinating at 84 days (12 weeks). Valid almost everywhere. Invalid in Japan. And it cannot be patched: you must revaccinate and restart the 180-day wait.
  • Giving a single dose. Japan requires two or more, both after the microchip.
  • Using an RNA vaccine. The AQS rejects it outright. Check the type of vaccine before it goes in.
  • Confusing the expiry date with the period of efficacy. What counts is the duration of immunity declared by the manufacturer, not the date on the vial.
  • Giving the booster late. A dose administered after the previous one's period of efficacy has expired does not count as a booster: it forces you to start again from the first vaccination.

Frequently asked questions

My puppy was vaccinated at 12 weeks. Is that valid for Japan?
No. Japan requires the first rabies vaccination to be given when the animal is at least 91 days old (birth counts as day 0). Twelve weeks is 84 days. The AQS warns of this explicitly: other countries allow it between 84 and 90 days, but under Japanese regulations a vaccine given before 91 days is invalid.
Why two vaccinations and not one?
Because that is what the Japanese rule requires for non-designated regions: two or more doses, both after the microchip, at least 30 days apart, with the second within the first one's period of efficacy. It is a requirement in addition to the antibody titre, not a substitute for it.
Does Japan accept RNA rabies vaccines?
No. The AQS expressly warns that RNA rabies vaccines — already in use in some countries outside Japan — are not accepted for the import of dogs and cats. Nor are live-virus vaccines. Only inactivated or recombinant vaccines are admitted. It is worth checking the type of vaccine before it is administered.
What is the vaccine's "period of efficacy"?
It is not the expiry date printed on the vial, but the duration of immunity the manufacturer declares for that vaccine. Every Japanese deadline is counted against that period, not against the product's expiry date.
I missed the booster. Can I give it now?
You can give it, but for Japan it will not count as a booster. The AQS establishes that a vaccine administered after the previous one's period of efficacy has expired is not accepted as an additional dose: the procedure must start again from the first vaccination, with everything that entails — including the 180-day waiting period.
