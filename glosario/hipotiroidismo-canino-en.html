Canine hypothyroidism: signs, diagnosis and the euthyroid sick confounder

Hypothyroidism is the most common thyroid endocrinopathy in dogs. Its signs are non-specific and its diagnosis requires combining several tests so it is not confused with euthyroid sick syndrome.

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 12 May 2026

Definition based on documentary practice 2013-2026 · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

Thyroid hormone deficiency · Common canine endocrinopathy

Canine hypothyroidism is the insufficient production of thyroid hormone by the thyroid gland. The deficiency of T4 (and T3) slows metabolism and produces slow, progressive signs such as lethargy, weight gain and skin and coat changes. The diagnosis relies on veterinary endocrinology, combining total T4, TSH and free T4, always interpreted together with the clinical picture.

Formal technical definition

Hypothyroidism is a clinical syndrome resulting from an insufficient circulating concentration of thyroid hormones, mainly thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3). In dogs the most common form is acquired primary hypothyroidism, due to progressive destruction of thyroid tissue (lymphocytic thyroiditis or idiopathic atrophy), which reduces hormone synthesis. As thyroid hormones fall, negative feedback on the pituitary decreases and TSH usually rises. Because thyroid hormones regulate the basal metabolism of nearly all tissues, their deficiency produces heterogeneous systemic manifestations. The diagnosis is a stepwise confirmation process, not the reading of a single laboratory value.

Clinical signs

The signs of canine hypothyroidism are non-specific and develop gradually, which makes early recognition difficult. The most characteristic are:

  • Lethargy and reduced activity. Lower exercise tolerance, drowsiness and a generally dull appearance; often the first reason for consultation.
  • Weight gain. A tendency to gain weight without dietary changes, due to the drop in basal metabolism.
  • Skin and coat changes. Dry, brittle or dull coat, non-pruritic symmetrical alopecia, hyperpigmentation and slow wound healing.
  • Recurrent skin disease. Predisposition to skin infections (pyoderma) and repeated otitis secondary to the metabolic and cutaneous imbalance.
  • Cold intolerance. Heat-seeking behaviour and a tendency to mild hypothermia in some animals.

None of these signs is exclusive to hypothyroidism: all overlap with other metabolic, dermatological or systemic diseases. The clinical picture therefore guides suspicion, but the diagnosis is confirmed with laboratory tests interpreted together.

Laboratory diagnosis

The diagnosis combines several hormone tests because none alone is conclusive. These are the three reference tests and their general interpretation:

TestWhat it measuresGeneral interpretationRole in diagnosis
Total T4 (total thyroxine)Total circulating thyroid hormone (bound + free)A normal value makes hypothyroidism unlikely; a low value is compatible but not specificInitial screening test
Canine TSH (cTSH)Thyroid-stimulating hormone produced by the pituitaryUsually elevated in primary hypothyroidism; may be normal in a proportion of casesComplements T4 and supports confirmation
Free T4 (fT4)Metabolically active fraction, ideally by equilibrium dialysisMore resistant to interference from concurrent illness; a low value strengthens the diagnosisConfirmatory test

The classic combination that supports the diagnosis is a low total T4 and low free T4 together with an elevated TSH, always in a dog with compatible clinical signs. Reference ranges depend on the laboratory, the method and the patient; interpretation is the veterinarian's responsibility.

The confounder: euthyroid sick syndrome (NTIS)

The main diagnostic error arises from euthyroid sick syndrome (NTIS, non-thyroidal illness syndrome): the drop in total T4 in dogs that do not have hypothyroidism, caused by another concurrent illness, physiological stress or certain drugs (such as glucocorticoids or some anticonvulsants). In these cases the thyroid works normally, but the hormone tests mimic a hypothyroid pattern.

NTIS explains why a low total T4 alone is not enough to diagnose hypothyroidism. A dog with an acute illness may show a low total T4 and be perfectly euthyroid. To reduce this confounder it helps to: first stabilise or rule out concurrent diseases, review any medication that may alter thyroid hormones, and complement total T4 with TSH and free T4, which resist interference better. Interpreting a single value out of context is the fastest route to a wrong diagnosis — and a wrong treatment.

Common errors

  • Diagnosing on a low total T4 alone. An isolated value does not distinguish hypothyroidism from euthyroid sick syndrome. It must be confirmed with TSH and free T4 and with the clinical picture.
  • Ignoring concurrent diseases. An acute or chronic illness can lower T4 without hypothyroidism being present. Those conditions should be stabilised or ruled out before concluding.
  • Not considering drugs. Glucocorticoids, some anticonvulsants and other medications alter thyroid hormones and can mimic hypothyroidism on the tests.
  • Interpreting values without clinical signs. Without compatible manifestations, a borderline result rarely justifies a diagnosis. Tests are read together with the history and physical examination.
  • Confusing non-specific signs with confirmation. Lethargy, weight gain or skin problems guide suspicion, but do not confirm hypothyroidism on their own.

Frequently asked questions

What is canine hypothyroidism?
It is an endocrine disorder in which the thyroid gland produces an insufficient amount of thyroid hormone (T4 and T3). In dogs it is usually primary, due to progressive destruction of thyroid tissue. The hormone deficiency slows metabolism and produces varied clinical signs that develop slowly and progressively.
What are the signs of hypothyroidism in dogs?
The most common are lethargy, a tendency to gain weight without dietary changes, cold intolerance, and skin and coat changes: dry or brittle coat, symmetrical hair loss, poor wound healing and recurrent skin disease. The signs are non-specific and overlap with other conditions, so the diagnosis is never based on the clinical picture alone.
Which tests are used to diagnose hypothyroidism?
The diagnosis combines several tests: total T4 as a screening test, canine TSH to assess the pituitary response, and free T4 (ideally by dialysis) to confirm. No single test is conclusive; the result is interpreted together with the clinical history, physical examination and any other diseases or drugs that may alter the values.
What is euthyroid sick syndrome and why is it confusing?
Euthyroid sick syndrome (NTIS, non-thyroidal illness syndrome) is the drop in total T4 in dogs that do NOT have hypothyroidism, caused by another concurrent illness, stress or certain drugs. It can mimic hypothyroidism on the tests and lead to a wrong diagnosis. That is why concurrent diseases should be ruled out and total T4 complemented with TSH and free T4 before concluding.
Why is monitoring hypothyroidism important?
A correct diagnosis avoids both treating a healthy dog (by confusing NTIS with hypothyroidism) and leaving a truly affected dog untreated. Veterinary follow-up allows confirmation of the diagnosis, adjustment of management over time and reassessment when concurrent diseases appear. Assessment must always be made by a veterinarian; this entry is informational and does not replace a clinical consultation.
