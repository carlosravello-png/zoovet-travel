Hyperadrenocorticism (Cushing's syndrome): what it is, signs, diagnosis and control

Cushing's syndrome is one of the most common endocrine diseases of the adult and older dog. Its chronic cortisol excess shows in the urine, the appetite, the abdomen and, very often, the skin.

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 23 July 2026

Reference clinical definition · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

Chronic cortisol excess · Pituitary and adrenal forms · Adult–senior dog

Hyperadrenocorticism, known as Cushing's syndrome, is an endocrine disorder caused by the body's chronic exposure to excess cortisol. Most spontaneous cases are pituitary-dependent (a pituitary tumour over-stimulates the adrenal glands) and a minority are adrenal-dependent (a tumour of the adrenal gland itself produces cortisol autonomously). Its signs affect thirst, appetite, the abdominal profile and, very frequently, the skin and coat.

Formal technical definition

Canine hyperadrenocorticism is a syndrome arising from sustained hypercortisolism. In its pituitary-dependent form —the most common— an affected pituitary secretes excess ACTH and drives hyperplasia and overactivity of both adrenal glands. In its adrenal-dependent form, a tumour of the adrenal cortex produces cortisol autonomously, with suppression of ACTH. There is also an iatrogenic form, a consequence of prolonged corticosteroid administration, which reproduces the clinical picture without a functional tumour. Sustained cortisol excess alters the metabolism of water, carbohydrates, lipids and proteins, and modulates the immune system, which explains the breadth of its manifestations.

Classic clinical signs

The picture develops gradually, and not every patient shows all signs or with the same intensity. The most characteristic are:

SignTypical manifestation
Polyuria / polydipsiaMarked increase in urine volume and water intake; often the first change the family notices.
PolyphagiaIncreased appetite, sometimes with food-seeking behaviour.
Pendulous abdomenAbdominal distension ("pot-belly") from fat redistribution, liver enlargement and weakness of the abdominal muscles.
Symmetrical alopeciaBilateral, non-inflammatory hair loss over the flanks and trunk, sparing the head and limbs.
Thin, hypotonic skinThin skin with visible vessels, easy bruising and slow wound healing.
PantingPanting at rest, with no heat or effort to justify it.
Recurrent infectionsPyoderma and urinary tract infections that recur due to the immune modulation linked to cortisol excess.

Why it sometimes reaches dermatology first

Although Cushing's is an endocrine disease, its cutaneous signs are often what the owner notices first: a symmetrical alopecia that progresses without itching, skin that thins and injures easily, or pyoderma and infections that keep coming back. It is therefore not unusual for the patient to arrive first at the dermatology consultation, and for it to be there that an underlying endocrine cause is first suspected. Non-inflammatory alopecia or a skin infection that does not respond as expected are signals that justify investigating the hormonal axis.

Our team has published precisely on the dermatological manifestations of hyperadrenocorticism and their role as a gateway to diagnosis: Hyperadrenocorticism (Cushing's) and canine dermatology. This coordination between dermatology and endocrinology is what makes it possible to recognise the full picture rather than stopping at the skin symptom.

Diagnosis

The diagnosis of hyperadrenocorticism is built step by step and always under veterinary judgement. It starts from the clinical history and the identification of compatible signs; it is supported by blood biochemistry and urinalysis, which guide the work-up and help rule out other causes; and it is confirmed with specific adrenal function tests, which exist for this purpose and assess the response of the pituitary–adrenal axis. Abdominal ultrasound adds information on the size and symmetry of the glands and can help differentiate the pituitary form from the adrenal form.

No single test confirms the disease on its own: results are always interpreted together with the clinical picture. In keeping with its conservative approach, this entry names the existence of these adrenal function tests but does not detail doses, sampling times or protocols: that indication belongs to the treating veterinarian.

Control and follow-up

Cushing's syndrome is, in most cases, a disease that is controlled over time rather than resolved in a single visit. The aim of management is to reduce the burden of signs and improve quality of life, and for that a periodic follow-up with clinical and laboratory re-evaluations is essential to tailor the plan to each patient. The iatrogenic form additionally requires a review of previous corticosteroid therapy, always under veterinary supervision.

This entry is informative and conservative in nature: it does not state doses or drug protocols. The specific plan —for diagnosis, treatment and follow-up— is always set by the veterinarian responsible for the case.

Common errors

  • Attributing polyuria/polydipsia to age or heat alone. Increased thirst and urination in an adult dog deserves investigation; it can be the first sign of an endocrinopathy.
  • Treating the skin in isolation. Symmetrical alopecia or recurrent pyoderma with no apparent cause should raise suspicion of an endocrine, not merely dermatological, origin.
  • Reading a single test as a definitive diagnosis. Adrenal function tests are interpreted alongside the clinical picture; no isolated result is enough on its own.
  • Abandoning follow-up. Controlling Cushing's requires periodic re-evaluations; stopping them after the first improvement compromises management.
  • Confusing iatrogenic with spontaneous Cushing's. A history of prolonged corticosteroid therapy completely changes the approach and must always be reviewed under veterinary supervision.

Frequently asked questions

What is Cushing's syndrome in dogs?
It is hyperadrenocorticism: the body's chronic exposure to excess cortisol. There are two main spontaneous forms: the pituitary-dependent form, the most common, in which a pituitary tumour over-stimulates the adrenal glands; and the adrenal-dependent form, in which a tumour of the adrenal gland itself produces cortisol autonomously. There is also an iatrogenic form caused by prolonged corticosteroid administration. It mainly affects adult and older dogs.
What are the classic signs of Cushing's?
The most characteristic are increased thirst and urination (polyuria/polydipsia), increased appetite, a pendulous or «pot-bellied» abdomen, non-inflammatory symmetrical alopecia over the flanks and trunk, thin skin that injures easily, panting at rest, and recurrent skin or urinary tract infections. Not every patient shows all signs or with the same intensity.
Why does Cushing's sometimes reach dermatology first?
Because the cutaneous signs —symmetrical alopecia, thin and hypotonic skin, pyoderma and other recurrent infections— are often what the owner notices first and what prompts the visit. Alopecia or a skin infection that does not respond as expected can be the first clue to an underlying endocrine disease, which is why dermatology and endocrinology work in a coordinated way.
How is hyperadrenocorticism diagnosed?
The diagnosis is built from the clinical history and compatible signs, supported by blood biochemistry and urinalysis, and confirmed with specific adrenal function tests, which exist for this purpose. Imaging (abdominal ultrasound) helps characterise the glands. No single test is sufficient on its own: results are always interpreted together with the clinical picture and under veterinary judgement.
Can Cushing's be controlled?
Yes. In most cases it is a disease that is controlled with follow-up over time rather than resolved in a single visit. Management requires periodic re-evaluations and adjustments under veterinary supervision. This entry does not state doses or drug protocols: the specific plan is always set by the treating veterinarian for each patient.
