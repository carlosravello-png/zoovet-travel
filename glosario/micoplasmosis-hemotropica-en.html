Feline hemotropic mycoplasmosis (feline infectious anemia): transmission, signs and diagnosis

A bacterial infection of the cat's red blood cells that causes hemolytic anemia. It is ruled out alongside FeLV and FIV in the feline evaluation prior to export.

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 12 May 2026

Definition based on documentary practice 2013-2026 · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

Mycoplasma haemofelis · hemolytic anemia · diagnosed by PCR

Feline hemotropic mycoplasmosis, known as feline infectious anemia, is caused by hemotropic bacteria —mainly Mycoplasma haemofelis— that attach to the surface of the red blood cells and cause their destruction, producing hemolytic anemia. In the evaluation of cats for export it is ruled out alongside feline leukemia (FeLV) and feline immunodeficiency (FIV), and its diagnostic test of choice is PCR on blood.

Formal technical definition

The agents of feline hemotropic mycoplasmosis are hemotropic mycoplasmas (hemoplasmas): cell-wall-free bacteria of the genus Mycoplasma that adhere to the membrane of red blood cells. Three main species are recognized in the cat: Mycoplasma haemofelis, the most pathogenic and capable of producing clinical hemolytic anemia; "Candidatus Mycoplasma haemominutum", usually of low pathogenicity; and "Candidatus Mycoplasma turicensis", of variable pathogenicity. The destruction of the parasitized red cells —both direct and immune-mediated— produces the anemia, whose severity depends on the species involved, on possible co-infections and on the animal's immune status.

Context in the feline export evaluation

In the health evaluation prior to cat export, feline infectious anemia is part of the screening panel alongside feline leukemia (FeLV) and feline immunodeficiency (FIV). The reason is twofold: these infections share risk factors and possible transmission routes, and an anemic or immunosuppressed cat may not be fit to travel. Identifying a hemotropic infection before the flight allows the animal to be stabilized and prevents decompensation during transport. This screening is part of the feline export evaluation performed by the clinical team before certifying the animal's fitness.

Evaluation and diagnostic pathway

  • Clinical suspicionLethargy, pale mucous membranes, weakness or loss of appetite point toward anemia.
  • Complete blood countConfirms the anemia and assesses its severity and regenerative nature.
  • PCR on bloodTest of choice: detects and identifies the hemoplasma species with greater sensitivity than the smear.
  • FeLV/FIV screeningAssessed in parallel because they share risk factors and transmission routes.
  • Stabilization before travelThe cat must be clinically stable before its fitness for transport is certified.

Common errors

  • Relying only on the blood smear. Hemoplasmas appear and disappear from the blood intermittently; a negative smear does not rule out infection. PCR is more sensitive.
  • Assuming every positive cat is ill. Some species are subclinical; interpretation must combine PCR, the complete blood count and the clinical picture.
  • Skipping the joint FeLV/FIV screening. They share risk factors and may worsen the picture, so they should be assessed together.
  • Certifying travel with an unstabilized anemic cat. Uncontrolled anemia can decompensate during the flight.
  • Confusing feline infectious anemia with other causes of anemia. A definitive diagnosis requires identifying the agent, not just confirming the anemia.

Frequently asked questions

What is feline hemotropic mycoplasmosis?
It is an infection caused by hemotropic bacteria (hemoplasmas), such as Mycoplasma haemofelis, that attach to the surface of the cat's red blood cells and cause their destruction. The result is hemolytic anemia of variable severity, which is why it is also known as feline infectious anemia.
How is feline hemotropic mycoplasmosis transmitted?
The transmission routes are not fully established, but blood-feeding vectors such as fleas are considered a likely factor, as is transmission through bites and fights between cats. Cats with outdoor access and those living in large groups are at higher risk of exposure.
What signs does it cause in the cat?
The signs derive from the anemia and are nonspecific: lethargy, weakness, pale mucous membranes, loss of appetite and, in marked cases, fever. Some infections are subclinical, especially with the less pathogenic species, and are only detected through laboratory testing.
How is it diagnosed?
The diagnostic test of choice is PCR on a blood sample, which detects and identifies the hemoplasma species with greater sensitivity than a blood smear. Hemoplasmas appear and disappear from the blood intermittently, so a negative smear does not rule out infection; PCR is interpreted together with the complete blood count and the clinical picture.
Why is it assessed alongside FeLV and FIV in cat export?
Feline infectious anemia is included in the screening panel alongside feline leukemia (FeLV) and feline immunodeficiency (FIV) because they share risk factors and transmission routes, and because an anemic or immunosuppressed cat may not be fit to travel. Detecting and stabilizing the infection before the flight reduces the risk of decompensation during transport.
