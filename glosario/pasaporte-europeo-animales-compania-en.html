European pet passport

Valid for life · EU residents only · Useless for entering from a third country

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 11 July 2026

Definition based on documentary practice 2013-2026 · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

Valid for life

It is the document everyone wants and that almost nobody flying out of a third country can hold — not yet, at least.

The European pet passport is mandatory for moving a dog, cat or ferret between European Union countries. It follows a standard model, it is issued by any authorised veterinarian, and it is valid for life as long as the animal's rabies vaccination stays in order.

But it carries one condition that decides everything: it is issued only to owners resident in the European Union. Whoever travels towards the EU from a third country does not travel on a European passport — they travel on a health certificate. The passport comes later, once they are settled there.

Formal technical definition

The passport gathers, in a single booklet with a format common to the 27 countries: the description of the animal, the code of its microchip (or the tattoo, if it was applied before 3 July 2011), its health record —rabies vaccination, titration, echinococcus treatment— and the contact details of the owner and of the veterinarian who issued it.

Lifetime validity comes with fine print. The passport does not expire as a document, but it only works while the health data it contains remain valid. If the rabies vaccine lapses —a lapse— the passport is worthless for travel, even if the booklet is spotless. The document is a container: what expires is what is inside it.

Equivalent territories. To enter the EU or Northern Ireland, a passport issued in Andorra, Switzerland, the Faroe Islands, Gibraltar, Greenland, Iceland, Liechtenstein, Monaco, Norway, San Marino or Vatican City State is also accepted.

And a post-Brexit warning: since 1 January 2021, European pet passports issued to owners resident in Great Britain are no longer valid for travelling with pets from Great Britain to the EU or to Northern Ireland.

Regulatory context in export

For anyone exporting from a third country, the European passport shows up at a very precise moment: after the flight. The animal enters the EU on its health certificate, endorsed by the official authority —SENASA in the Peruvian case— and validated at the travellers' point of entry. Once there, and once the owner can prove residence, a local authorised veterinarian may issue the passport.

From that moment the animal changes regime: it stops needing a health certificate for every journey inside the Union and starts moving on a single lifetime document. It is the difference between processing paperwork and simply holding it.

That jump is worth planning: the passport should ideally be issued before the animal's rabies vaccine expires, so that the European veterinarian can transcribe valid, continuous cover. A passport issued after a lapse drags the lapse along with it.

Typical timeline from a third country

  • MicrochipOr a legible tattoo, if it was applied before 3 July 2011.
  • Rabies vaccinationAlways after the microchip.
  • Entry into the EUOn a health certificate, not on a passport. The passport does not exist yet.
  • Residence establishedAn indispensable condition: only residents in the EU may obtain it.
  • Issuance of the passportAn authorised veterinarian issues it and transcribes the valid health record.
  • Free movementWithin the EU, on a single document and with no certificate per journey.

Common errors

  • Believing the passport works for entering the EU from a third country. It does not. Entry runs on the health certificate; the passport is obtained afterwards, once resident there.
  • Assuming that "valid for life" means nothing ever has to be done again. The booklet does not expire; the vaccine inside it does. With an expired vaccine, the passport authorises no journey.
  • Requesting it without residence in the EU. It is a requirement, not a negotiable formality.
  • Relying on a British passport post-Brexit. Those issued to residents in Great Britain stopped being valid for travelling to the EU on 1 January 2021.
  • Issuing it after a vaccination lapse. The passport does not erase the lapse: it inherits it.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get a European pet passport for my dog from a third country?
No. The European pet passport is issued only to owners resident in the European Union. From a third country, the animal enters with a health certificate endorsed by the official authority —SENASA, in the Peruvian case—. The passport comes afterwards, once the owner is resident in the EU.
Does the European pet passport expire?
The document itself is valid for life, but it only works while the health information it contains remains valid — in particular, the rabies vaccination. If the vaccine lapses, the passport authorises no journey at all even if the booklet is immaculate. The passport is the container; what expires is the content.
Who can issue it?
Any veterinarian authorised by the competent authorities to issue passports. It does not have to be a government official veterinarian: it is enough that the vet is empowered to do so.
Is a passport issued in Switzerland or Norway valid?
Yes. To enter the EU or Northern Ireland, passports issued in Andorra, Switzerland, the Faroe Islands, Gibraltar, Greenland, Iceland, Liechtenstein, Monaco, Norway, San Marino and Vatican City State are also accepted.
What about British passports?
Since 1 January 2021, European pet passports issued to owners resident in Great Britain are no longer valid for travelling with pets from Great Britain to an EU country or to Northern Ireland. It is one of the direct consequences of Brexit on the movement of companion animals.
