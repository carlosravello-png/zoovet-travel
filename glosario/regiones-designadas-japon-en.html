Designated and non-designated regions — Japan

Six territories in the entire world have the fast track. Everyone else, seven months

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 11 July 2026

Definition based on documentary practice 2013-2026 · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

6 designated regions · everywhere else, no

Japan does not sort the world into high risk and low risk. It sorts it into two boxes: the designated regions —those it considers rabies-free— and everything else.

The designated ones are six: Iceland, Australia, New Zealand, Fiji, Hawaii and Guam. That is the whole list. There are no intermediate categories, no exemptions granted for a good sanitary reputation, no partial lists. The United Kingdom is not on it. Switzerland is not on it. Chile is not on it.

Peru —like all of Latin America— is a non-designated region. And that means the full regime: microchip, two rabies vaccinations, an antibody titre test, a 180-day waiting period and 40 days' advance notification. Seven months, at the very least.

Formal technical definition

The logic of the list is geographical before it is sanitary: five of the six designated regions are islands —Iceland, Australia, New Zealand, Fiji, Guam— and the sixth, Hawaii, is one too. Japan, itself a rabies-free archipelago, recognises the territories that are in the same position it is: surrounded by water and free of terrestrial rabies.

This has a counter-intuitive consequence: the Japanese list does not match any other. Not the CDC's list of high-risk countries, and not the European Union's third-country list. A dog leaving France needs no titre test to enter Spain — and does need one, plus a 180-day waiting period, to enter Japan. France is not a designated region.

The time window works as it always does: what counts is where the animal has been, not where it lives today. A dog that has passed through a non-designated region carries that status with it.

Regulatory context in export

For anyone exporting from a non-designated region, this classification boils down to a single sentence: there is no shortcut. There is no intermediate procedure and no fast track earned through good sanitary behaviour. Peru sits in the same box as the rest of the world that is not a rabies-free island.

The operational consequence is a matter of the calendar: whoever is thinking about Japan has to start seven months in advance, and the first decision —giving the first vaccination at 91 days of age rather than at 84— is taken when the animal is a puppy and nobody is thinking about moving abroad yet.

Hence a recommendation we always give and almost nobody follows: if there is any chance that a dog might end up living in Japan, the first vaccination goes in at 91 days. Waiting one extra week costs nothing. Not having waited costs six months.

Typical timeline from a non-designated region

  • Check the originHas the animal been in a designated region? There are only six.
  • If it is a non-designated region…Full regime. That is the case for Peru and for all of Latin America.
  • First vaccination at 91 daysThis is where everything begins. One week earlier invalidates it.
  • Seven months laterThe first possible day of travel. There is no way to shorten it.

Common errors

  • Assuming that "developed country" = designated region. The continental United States, the United Kingdom, Switzerland and the entire European Union are not designated regions for Japan.
  • Confusing the Japanese list with the CDC's or the EU's. They are three different lists, with different criteria and different consequences.
  • Believing that Hawaii and the continental USA are the same thing. Hawaii and Guam are designated regions; the continental United States is not.
  • Thinking there is an intermediate procedure. There is none: either a designated region, or the full regime.
  • Vaccinating the puppy at 84 days "just in case". If that dog ever goes to Japan, that vaccination is worthless. Waiting one week is free.

Frequently asked questions

Which are Japan's designated regions?
Six: Iceland, Australia, New Zealand, Fiji, Hawaii and Guam. They are the only ones Japan considers rabies-free. The entire rest of the world —including Peru, the European Union, the United Kingdom and the continental United States— is a non-designated region.
Is Peru a designated region?
No. Peru, like all of Latin America, is a non-designated region. That means the full regime: microchip, two or more rabies vaccinations, an antibody titre test at a designated laboratory, a 180-day waiting period and 40 days' advance notification. A minimum of seven months.
My dog is coming from the United States. Does that make entry into Japan easier?
It depends which United States. Hawaii and Guam are designated regions; the continental territory is not. A dog coming from California is in exactly the same box as one coming from Lima.
Is there any intermediate procedure?
No. Japan has no graded risk categories like the CDC and no partial lists like the European Union. Either the animal comes from one of the six designated regions, or the full regime applies. There is no middle ground.
Does the Japanese list match the CDC's or the EU's?
No. They are three independent systems. A dog leaving France enters Spain without a titre test — and needs a titre test, two vaccinations and a 180-day waiting period to enter Japan, because France is not a designated region. Each bloc decides on its own.
