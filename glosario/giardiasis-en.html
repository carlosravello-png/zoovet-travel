Giardiasis: intestinal Giardia infection, transmission and diagnosis

Giardiasis is an intestinal infection caused by the protozoan Giardia, spread through the fecal-oral route by cysts. It is a common cause of diarrhea in puppies and a key point in deworming plans.

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 12 May 2026

Definition based on documentary practice 2013-2026 · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

Giardia duodenalis · Intestinal protozoan · Fecal-oral transmission via cysts

Giardiasis is a small-intestine infection caused by the flagellate protozoan Giardia (Giardia duodenalis, also called G. intestinalis or G. lamblia). The parasite is transmitted via the fecal-oral route, by ingesting cysts present in water, food or an environment contaminated with feces. It may present with diarrhea —sometimes intermittent— or subclinically, and is especially relevant in puppies and animals living in groups.

Formal technical definition

Giardia is a flagellate protozoan that colonizes the small intestine. Its cycle has two forms: the trophozoite, which attaches to the intestinal mucosa and multiplies, and the cyst, the resistant form shed in the feces that contaminates the environment. Cysts are immediately infective and can survive for weeks in cool, moist environments, which explains transmission through water and surfaces. Infection is acquired by ingesting a relatively low number of cysts. There are different assemblages (genotypes) of Giardia; some are considered to have zoonotic potential and others appear adapted to specific hosts.

Clinical signs

The most characteristic sign is diarrhea, which may be acute, chronic or intermittent, with pasty or mucous stools and, at times, a markedly foul odor. Many infected animals show no symptoms and act as carriers. When disease is present, it may be accompanied by weight loss or loss of body condition despite a preserved appetite, especially in puppies or animals with other parasitic conditions or concurrent illnesses. Vomiting is less frequent. The severity of the signs does not always match the parasite load, so an animal with few symptoms may still be shedding cysts.

Zoonotic potential

Some genotypes of Giardia can infect both animals and people, so it is regarded as a potential zoonosis. The actual risk of transmission from the pet to the person appears low and depends on the genotype involved, but it is wise to maintain sensible hygiene measures: pick up and dispose of feces, wash your hands and avoid contact with contaminated water or environments. People with a compromised immune system should take extra precautions and consult their doctor. This information does not replace individual veterinary or medical assessment.

Practical approach when suspected

  • Veterinary consultationAssess the signs and rule out other causes of diarrhea before assuming a diagnosis.
  • Stool samplesAntigen detection and flotation; may require serial samples from different days.
  • Veterinarian-directed treatmentThe product and regimen are defined for each case. Do not self-medicate.
  • Hygiene and environmental controlRemove feces, clean and disinfect surfaces and consider bathing the animal.
  • ReassessmentMonitor the clinical response and consider re-testing as directed by the veterinarian.

Common errors

  • Assuming a negative test rules out giardiasis. Cyst shedding is intermittent; a single test may fail to detect the infection.
  • Self-medicating with dewormers without a diagnosis. Not all routine dewormers are effective against Giardia, which is a protozoan.
  • Not cleaning the environment or bathing the animal. Cysts persist on the coat and on surfaces and favor reinfection.
  • Ignoring asymptomatic housemates. In multi-pet households, carriers keep the parasite circulating.
  • Confusing Giardia with "worms". It is a protozoan, not a worm; the diagnostic and therapeutic approach differs.

Frequently asked questions

Can giardiasis spread between pets and people?
Giardia is transmitted via the fecal-oral route, by ingesting cysts present in feces, water or contaminated environments. Between pets, transmission is common when they live together or share spaces. Some genotypes have zoonotic potential, so it is considered a potential zoonosis; the actual risk to people appears low and depends on the genotype. As a sensible precaution, pick up feces, wash your hands and keep the environment clean. This information does not replace individual veterinary or medical assessment.
Why does Giardia diarrhea come and go?
Giardiasis can present with intermittent diarrhea because cyst shedding and parasite activity are not constant. An animal may alternate days of normal stools with episodes of pasty or mucous diarrhea. This intermittency also complicates diagnosis, since a sample taken on a day without cyst shedding may test negative even though the infection persists.
Does a single stool test rule out giardiasis?
Not necessarily. Because cysts are shed intermittently, a single negative test does not rule out infection. When there is clinical suspicion, the veterinarian may combine antigen detection with centrifugation flotation and repeat the study on serial samples from different days to increase the chance of detection.
Is giardiasis more common in puppies?
Yes. Giardiasis is especially common in puppies and young animals, particularly in breeders, pet shops, shelters or high-density environments where infection pressure is higher. In puppies, Giardia diarrhea can affect development and body condition, so early detection and management are important.
Does routine deworming always cover Giardia?
Not always. Many routine dewormers target intestinal worms and are not necessarily effective against Giardia, which is a protozoan. That is why Giardia is addressed within a comprehensive, individualized deworming plan, with prior diagnosis and environmental hygiene measures. The product and approach should be directed by the veterinarian, without self-medication.
