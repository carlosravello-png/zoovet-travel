Advance import notification — Japan (40 days)

It is free, it is one form, and filing it late cancels the trip

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 11 July 2026

Definition based on documentary practice 2013-2026 · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

40 days · free · and indispensable

Of all the requirements for entering Japan, this is the cheapest one and the one that cancels the most trips. It is free, it is a single form, it is sent over the internet — and if it is filed late, the animal does not fly.

The AQS requires that it be notified of the arrival at least 40 days beforehand, at the intended port of entry. On the strength of that notification, the agency reviews the documents and issues an Import Inspection Approval, which is what the airline will ask for at check-in.

The official wording leaves no room: "in principle, a notification filed less than 40 days in advance is not accepted". This is not a planning tip. It is a door that closes.

Formal technical definition

The notification is filed through NACCS, the Japanese customs online system, or by e-mail using the official form — there is one for dogs and another for cats. It is addressed to the AQS at the airport or seaport through which the animal will enter: notifying the head office or any other branch does not count.

What the AQS will not allow you to change afterwards: bringing the arrival date forward, increasing the number of animals, swapping one animal for another, or any modification made after the scheduled arrival date. Changes are requested with a Modification form, and if the original notification was filed through NACCS, the modification must also go through NACCS.

And a logistical warning nobody sees coming: if the AQS detention facility is full, it may be necessary to change the arrival date or the port of entry. In other words: the slot is not guaranteed by notifying — it is guaranteed by notifying in time.

The AQS also recommends attaching copies of certain documents — the antibody test report, among others — to speed up the process. And it warns: if the notification contains unclear points or deficiencies, the Approval may not be issued.

Regulatory context in export

This requirement sits in an odd place on the calendar: the 180-day waiting period is so long that the 40 days of the notification fit inside it with room to spare. There is no reasonable excuse for filing it late — and yet it is the single most common cause of cancelled flights to Japan.

The reason is human, not regulatory: people discover the requirement once the ticket is already bought. By then, if fewer than 40 days remain, no paperwork can save the trip. The ticket can be changed; the rule cannot.

There is one further detail from the AQS itself that sharpens the urgency: because of cases of duplicate microchips, the agency expressly asks that, once the import date has been decided, the notification be filed as soon as possible. If the chip turns out to be a duplicate, it may have to be re-implanted and the process redone — and that is discovered precisely when you notify.

Typical timeline

  • Blood sample collectionThe long wait begins. The 40 days of the notification fit inside it without a squeeze.
  • Date and airport are decidedThe notification goes to the AQS at that port of entry.
  • Advance notification (deadline)Through NACCS or by e-mail. Later than this, it is not accepted.
  • Import Inspection ApprovalThe AQS issues it after reviewing the notification. Print it or keep it digitally.
  • Boarding and arrivalThe Approval is shown at the check-in counter and on arrival.

Common errors

  • Buying the ticket before notifying. That is the order reversed, and it is the origin of almost every cancellation. Notify first; buy afterwards.
  • Notifying the wrong AQS office. It must be the one at the intended port of entry, not the head office.
  • Bringing the flight forward after notifying. Not allowed. Moving the arrival date up is, in principle, an unacceptable change.
  • Adding a second dog at the last minute. Increasing the number of animals is not accepted either.
  • Taking the slot for granted. If the detention facility is full, the date or the airport has to change.

Frequently asked questions

How much does the advance notification cost?
Nothing. It is a free form filed online through NACCS or by e-mail. Its cost is zero, and its consequence, if filed late, is that the animal cannot travel.
What happens if I notify with less than 40 days to go?
The AQS puts it bluntly: "in principle, a notification filed less than 40 days in advance is not accepted". Without an accepted notification there is no Import Inspection Approval, and without that document the airline should not board the animal.
I have already notified and want to bring the flight forward. Can I?
No, in principle. The AQS does not allow the arrival date to be brought forward once the notification is filed, nor the number of animals to be increased, nor one animal to be swapped for another, nor any change made after the scheduled arrival date.
Which office do I notify?
The AQS at the intended port of entry — the specific airport or seaport through which the animal will arrive. Notifying the head office or any other branch does not count.
Does the notification guarantee me a slot?
Not automatically. The AQS warns that, if the detention facility is full, it may be necessary to change the arrival date or even the port of entry. Notifying early is not just about meeting a deadline: it is about securing space.
