High-risk countries for dog rabies — the CDC list

United States · The list that decides whether your dog needs a serology titre, an ACF booking and quarantine

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 11 July 2026

Definition based on documentary practice 2013-2026 · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

The list follows the dog, not the address

The CDC splits the world in two: countries that are high-risk for dog rabies and everyone else. The difference is not academic — it decides how much time, how much money and how much paperwork it costs to take a dog into the United States.

Peru, Colombia, Brazil, Bolivia, Ecuador, Venezuela, Guatemala, Cuba and the Dominican Republic are all on the list. Chile, Argentina, Mexico and Uruguay are not. And the rule does not look at where the dog lives today, but at where it has been in the last 6 months.

If a country does not appear on the list, it is not high-risk. The CDC says so expressly: the absence is the rule, not an oversight.

Formal technical definition

The list is an administrative decision by the CDC based on its own assessment of dog rabies status country by country (Rabies Status: Assessment by Country). It does not necessarily match the classification used by the WOAH or the third-country list of the European Union: each bloc applies its own criteria, and a country can be "safe" for one and "high-risk" for another.

It covers whole countries and also political units: Ceuta and Melilla are listed separately from Spain; China appears with Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan expressly excluded; Israel is listed including the West Bank and Gaza. That granularity matters: a dog that transited through Ceuta is not in the same position as one that stayed in Madrid.

The time window is 6 months. A dog that lived in Lima and moved to Santiago three months ago is still subject to the high-risk requirements. The list chases the animal, not the address.

The CDC updates the list whenever it deems necessary and without individual notice. The version in force at the time of writing is dated 15 April 2026.

Regulatory context in pet export

Being on the list triggers a complete regime. For a foreign-vaccinated dog —which is virtually every dog leaving a high-risk country— the requirements are cumulative:

1. Microchip implanted before the rabies vaccine. 2. A valid rabies vaccine, administered no earlier than 12 weeks (84 days) of age. 3. A minimum age of 6 months on arrival. 4. Rabies serology titre at a CDC-approved laboratory — or 28 days of quarantine. 5. The Certification of Foreign Rabies Vaccination and Microchip form endorsed by an official government veterinarian (in Peru, SENASA). 6. A reservation at an ACF. 7. The CDC Dog Import Form receipt. 8. A healthy appearance on arrival.

And one absolute prohibition: an unvaccinated dog that has been in a high-risk country in the last 6 months cannot enter the United States. No quarantine will fix that.

Typical timeline from a high-risk country

  • The 6-month windowThe 6 months before entry into the US are what count. If the dog was in a listed country during that period, the high-risk regime applies even if it lives somewhere else today.
  • MicrochipAlways before the vaccine that will be used for the count.
  • Minimum age for vaccination12 weeks (84 days). A vaccine given before that is not accepted, with no exception for any brand.
  • Blood drawAt least 30 days after the first valid vaccine.
  • Deadline for the sampleAt least 28 days before entry into the US.
  • Minimum age on entryThe dog must be at least 6 months old on arrival.

Common mistakes

  • Believing the list looks at where the dog lives. It looks at where it has been in the last 6 months. Moving does not erase the history.
  • Assuming that "Latin American country" = high-risk. Chile, Argentina, Mexico and Uruguay are not on the list. Peru, Colombia, Brazil, Bolivia and Ecuador are.
  • Confusing the CDC list with the EU list. They are different lists, with different criteria and different consequences.
  • Vaccinating before 12 weeks of age. For dogs from high-risk countries, the CDC does not accept that vaccine even if the manufacturer authorises it earlier.
  • Travelling with an unvaccinated dog. From a high-risk country, entry is simply prohibited.

Frequently asked questions

Is Peru on the CDC high-risk list?
Yes. Peru appears on the CDC's official list of high-risk countries for dog rabies, verified on 11 July 2026. That means any dog that has been in Peru during the 6 months before entering the United States must comply with the reinforced regime: rabies serology titre or quarantine, a reservation at an ACF, a form endorsed by the national veterinary authority (SENASA in Peru) and a minimum age of 6 months.
My dog now lives in Chile but was in Peru four months ago. Which rules apply?
The high-risk regime applies. The CDC rule looks at the 6 months before entry, not at the dog's current country of residence. Because the dog was in Peru within that window, every reinforced requirement is demanded of it even if it now departs from a country that is not on the list.
What if my country is not on the list?
Then it is not high-risk. The CDC states it explicitly: if a country is not listed, it is not considered high-risk. For those dogs the CDC still recommends the rabies vaccine, but the additional requirements do not apply — no serology titre, no ACF reservation, no quarantine.
Can an unvaccinated dog enter the US from a high-risk country?
No. This is a ban, not a more expensive procedure. A dog that has been in a high-risk country in the last 6 months and is not vaccinated against rabies will not be admitted into the United States. No amount of quarantine can replace the vaccine.
How often does the list change?
The CDC updates it whenever it deems necessary, without individual notice and without a fixed schedule. The version in force is dated 15 April 2026. Check the official list before buying the ticket, not after.
