Leptospirosis: bacterial infection, zoonosis and prevention by vaccination

Leptospirosis is a bacterial disease of dogs —and a zoonosis transmissible to people— linked to contact with the urine of infected animals and with contaminated water.

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 12 May 2026

Definition based on documentary practice 2013-2026 · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

Leptospira spp. · Zoonosis · Urine and contaminated water

Leptospirosis is an infection caused by bacteria of the genus Leptospira. It is a zoonosis: it can be transmitted from animals to people. In dogs it frequently affects the kidney and liver; in humans, the severe form is known as Weil's disease. Infection occurs through contact with the urine of infected animals or with contaminated water, mud and soil. Prevention relies on vaccination as directed by the veterinarian and on hygiene measures.

Formal technical definition

Leptospirosis is an infectious disease caused by pathogenic spirochetes of the genus Leptospira. There are many serogroups and serovars of differing clinical relevance depending on the region. The bacterium is shed in the urine of carrier animals —including dogs, rodents and wildlife— and survives for weeks in warm, damp environments. Infection occurs when the bacterium penetrates through mucous membranes or skin wounds. It is a notifiable and monitored disease in many countries because of its zoonotic nature. This entry is informative and for reference: diagnosis, treatment and the vaccination schedule must always be established by a veterinarian.

Transmission and zoonotic nature

Leptospirosis infection is mainly linked to contact with the urine of infected animals and with contaminated water or environments: puddles, rivers, ponds, mud and waterlogged soil. Drinking or swimming in stagnant water, the presence of rodents, and rain or flooding increase the risk. Because it is a zoonosis, people can also become infected through the same routes, so if a dog is diagnosed it is advisable to reinforce hygiene, wear gloves when handling urine or contaminated areas, and consult a doctor if any symptoms appear. Keeping vaccination up to date and reinforcing environmental measures reduces the risk for the animal and for the household.

Indicative clinical pathway

  • Clinical suspicionFever, lethargy or vomiting, especially with a history of contact with stagnant water or rodents.
  • Veterinary evaluationPhysical exam plus blood and urine tests to assess kidney and liver function.
  • Specific testsSerology (MAT) or PCR to confirm infection. Ordered and interpreted by the veterinarian.
  • Professional managementTreatment and supportive care are directed by the veterinarian. This entry does not include doses.
  • Ongoing preventionVaccination as scheduled by the veterinarian and environmental hygiene to reduce relapse and transmission.

Common errors

  • Believing it only affects animals. Leptospirosis is a zoonosis: people in the household can also become infected through contact with urine or contaminated water.
  • Ignoring access to stagnant water. Puddles, rivers and mud after rain are common sources of infection; it is best to prevent the dog from drinking or bathing in them.
  • Neglecting rodent control. Rodents are important reservoirs; their presence near the home keeps the risk alive.
  • Assuming a single vaccine lasts forever. Immunity is not permanent: it requires boosters according to the schedule set by the veterinarian.
  • Self-medicating or looking up doses online. Diagnosis and treatment must be established by a veterinarian; this entry does not state doses.

Frequently asked questions

Can leptospirosis be passed to people?
Yes. Leptospirosis is a zoonosis: the bacterium Leptospira can be transmitted from animals to people, usually through contact with the urine of infected animals or with contaminated water, mud or soil. In humans the severe form is known as Weil's disease. If a dog is diagnosed, it is important to reinforce hygiene, wear gloves when handling urine, and see a doctor if symptoms appear.
How does my dog catch leptospirosis?
Infection occurs through contact with the urine of infected animals (dogs, rodents and wildlife) or with contaminated water and damp environments: puddles, rivers, mud or waterlogged ground. The bacterium enters through mucous membranes or skin wounds. Drinking or swimming in stagnant water and contact with rodents are common risk factors, especially after rain or flooding.
What are the signs of leptospirosis in dogs?
Signs are variable and non-specific. They may include fever, lethargy, loss of appetite, vomiting, muscle pain and increased or decreased urination due to kidney involvement. In cases with liver damage, jaundice (yellowing of the gums and eyes) may appear. The disease can range from mild to severe. Early veterinary care is decisive when these signs occur.
How is it diagnosed and can it be prevented?
Diagnosis is made by the veterinarian by combining the clinical history, blood and urine tests, and specific tests such as serology (MAT) or PCR. Prevention relies on vaccination according to the schedule the veterinarian sets and on hygiene measures: avoiding stagnant water, controlling rodents, and disinfecting areas contaminated with urine. This entry does not state doses; the protocol is defined by the professional.
Does the leptospirosis vaccine protect for life?
No. The leptospirosis vaccine requires periodic boosters according to the schedule set by the veterinarian, because immunity is not permanent and the vaccines cover a limited number of serogroups. Keeping vaccination up to date and reinforcing hygiene measures reduces the risk both for the animal and for the people in the household.
