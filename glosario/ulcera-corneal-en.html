Corneal ulcer: ophthalmic emergency, signs and diagnosis in dogs and cats

A corneal ulcer is a loss of the corneal epithelium that causes intense pain and can progress within hours. It is one of the most common ophthalmic emergencies in dogs and cats.

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 12 May 2026

Definition based on documentary practice 2013-2026 · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

Ophthalmic emergency · Diagnosis: fluorescein · Risk of perforation

A corneal ulcer is a lesion in which the epithelium covering the cornea is lost, exposing the underlying stroma. It causes marked pain and can deepen rapidly. It is considered an emergency because, without timely assessment and treatment, there is a risk of corneal perforation and vision loss. The reference diagnosis is simple, quick and painless: fluorescein staining. At any suspicion, the recommendation is to see a veterinarian the same day.

Formal technical definition

The cornea is the transparent, avascular layer at the front of the eye, organized into epithelium, stroma, Descemet's membrane and endothelium. A corneal ulcer (or ulcerative keratitis) is a break in the epithelium with exposure of the stroma; by depth it is classified as superficial, stromal (deep), descemetocele —when it reaches Descemet's membrane— and perforated. The loss of epithelium leaves nerve endings exposed, which explains the characteristic pain. Ulcers may follow a simple course (healing expected within a few days) or be complicated by infection, collagenases or factors that prevent re-epithelialization. Assessment of depth and course always falls to a veterinarian.

Why it is an emergency

A corneal ulcer should be treated as an emergency for three reasons. First, pain: the cornea is one of the most densely innervated tissues in the body and an ulcer causes intense discomfort. Second, speed of progression: some infected or enzyme-mediated (collagenolytic) ulcers can deepen within hours and reach a descemetocele or perforation. Third, the risk to vision: a perforated cornea compromises internal eye structures and can lead to permanent visual loss. Therefore, if the eye is held shut, there is pain, or the ocular appearance changes, the correct course of action is immediate veterinary assessment and avoiding self-medication —especially corticosteroid drops, which can worsen an ulcer.

Diagnosis with fluorescein staining

  • History and examinationOnset of signs, pain evaluation, palpebral reflex and appearance of the cornea under good lighting.
  • Fluorescein stainingThe dye is instilled; it binds to the exposed stroma where the epithelium is missing. The ulcerated area appears green under cobalt-blue light.
  • Assessment of depthIt is estimated whether the ulcer is superficial or deep. A descemetocele may not retain the dye at its center: a finding that requires specialized management.
  • Rule out causesForeign bodies, eyelid or eyelash abnormalities, and tear production measurement when dry eye is suspected.
  • Veterinary planTreatment is defined by the veterinarian according to cause, depth and course. This entry does not provide medication or doses.

Common risk factors

  • Ocular trauma. Scratches, contact with vegetation, foreign bodies or blunt injury are a common cause of corneal ulcers in dogs and cats.
  • Dry eye (keratoconjunctivitis sicca). Insufficient tear production reduces corneal protection and lubrication and favors the onset and persistence of ulcers.
  • Brachycephalic conformation. Bulldog, pug, shih tzu, boxer and similar breeds have prominent eyes and incomplete eyelid closure that leave part of the cornea exposed.
  • Eyelid and eyelash abnormalities. Entropion, distichiasis or ectopic cilia continuously rub the cornea and can cause recurrent ulcers.
  • Infections. In cats, feline herpesvirus is a relevant cause of corneal ulcers; bacterial superinfection can complicate any ulcer.

Frequently asked questions

Is a corneal ulcer a veterinary emergency?
Yes. A corneal ulcer should be assessed the same day. The cornea can deepen within hours, and a simple superficial ulcer can turn into a deep ulcer with a risk of perforation and vision loss. If the eye is held shut, there is evident pain, or the eye's appearance changes, seek veterinary care immediately and do not apply eye drops on your own, especially corticosteroid drops, which can worsen the lesion.
How is a corneal ulcer diagnosed?
The reference method is fluorescein staining: a dye is instilled that binds to the exposed corneal stroma where the epithelium is missing, and the ulcerated area appears green under cobalt-blue light. The examination is completed with assessment of the palpebral reflex, pain evaluation and, depending on the case, measurement of tear production and ruling out foreign bodies. Diagnosis and interpretation always fall to a veterinarian.
What signs suggest a possible corneal ulcer?
The most common signs are blepharospasm (the animal keeps the eye closed or half-closed), heavy tearing, a red eye, photophobia (discomfort in light), rubbing the face with a paw or against surfaces and, in some cases, a cloudy or bluish area on the cornea. Any of these signs warrants a veterinary check.
Why do brachycephalic dogs get more corneal ulcers?
Brachycephalic breeds (bulldog, pug, shih tzu, boxer, among others) have shallow orbits, more prominent eyes and, frequently, incomplete eyelid closure that leaves part of the cornea exposed. This reduces natural protection and lubrication and increases the risk of trauma and corneal ulcers. For this reason they require closer ophthalmic monitoring.
Can a corneal ulcer cause vision loss?
Yes, if untreated. An ulcer that progresses in depth can reach Descemet's membrane and perforate the cornea, compromising internal eye structures. Even ulcers that heal may leave residual opacity if they affected deep layers. Early veterinary assessment and treatment are decisive for preserving vision.
