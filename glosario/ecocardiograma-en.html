Echocardiography: what it evaluates, when it is indicated and how it differs from the ECG

Echocardiography is an ultrasound of the heart, non-invasive and painless, that evaluates cardiac structure and function. It is a diagnostic procedure, not a disease.

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 12 May 2026

Definition based on documentary practice 2013-2026 · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

Cardiac ultrasound · Non-invasive · Painless · No radiation

Echocardiography is an ultrasound of the heart: it uses ultrasound waves to produce real-time images of the organ as it beats. It allows assessment of cardiac structure (chambers, walls and valves) and function (contractility and, with Doppler, blood flow). It is a diagnostic procedure, non-invasive and painless, not a disease. It differs from the electrocardiogram (ECG), which records the electrical activity of the heart rather than its image.

Formal technical definition

Echocardiography (or cardiac ultrasound) is an imaging study that uses high-frequency ultrasound to depict the heart in motion. It combines several modes: two-dimensional (2D) mode shows the anatomy in real time; M-mode measures dimensions and motion over time; and Doppler (color, pulsed and continuous) evaluates the direction and velocity of blood flow across the valves and chambers. From these images, the veterinarian assesses the size and wall thickness, contractility (systolic function), the condition of the valves and the presence of pericardial fluid. It does not use ionizing radiation and, in most patients, is performed with the animal awake and under gentle restraint.

What it evaluates and when it is indicated

Echocardiography is indicated mainly when a murmur is detected on auscultation, in the presence of clinical signs such as cough, fatigue, exercise intolerance, fainting or breathing difficulty of possible cardiac origin, and in the follow-up of an already diagnosed heart condition. It is also requested as a pre-surgical evaluation —to estimate anesthetic risk— and as a pre-flight evaluation in animals with a cardiac history, advanced age or predisposed breeds, when it is necessary to assess whether they can safely tolerate air transport. In every case, the indication and interpretation belong to the attending veterinarian; echocardiography provides information but does not replace the complete clinical examination.

How the study is performed

  • PreparationA chest area is clipped or wetted to improve contact. No prolonged fasting is required unless specifically indicated.
  • PositioningThe animal is placed in recumbency, usually awake and under gentle restraint. Sedation is used only if strictly necessary.
  • Image acquisitionGel is applied and the transducer is moved to obtain 2D and M-mode views of the heart.
  • Doppler studyBlood flow across valves and chambers is evaluated with color, pulsed and continuous Doppler.
  • ReportThe veterinarian integrates the findings with the clinical examination and issues the cardiology report.

Common confusions

  • Confusing echocardiography with the electrocardiogram (ECG). Echocardiography is ultrasound imaging of the structure and motion of the heart; the ECG records electrical activity and rhythm. They are complementary, not interchangeable.
  • Believing it is painful or that it always requires general anesthesia. It is a non-invasive, painless procedure. Most dogs and cats tolerate it awake; light sedation is used only if the patient is uncooperative.
  • Thinking it replaces the clinical examination. Echocardiography is a diagnostic tool that complements —not replaces— auscultation and the veterinarian's overall assessment.
  • Assuming a normal result rules out all disease. Some abnormalities are intermittent or affect rhythm (the domain of the ECG). A normal echocardiography must be interpreted within the full clinical context.
  • Treating it as an export formality. It is not a standard zoosanitary requirement; it is a clinical evaluation that may be requested before a flight or surgery to estimate cardiac risk.

Frequently asked questions

Is an echocardiography the same as an electrocardiogram (ECG)?
No. They are complementary but distinct studies. The echocardiography is an ultrasound image that shows the structure of the heart (chambers, walls, valves) and its function (contractility, flow with Doppler). The electrocardiogram (ECG) records the electrical activity of the heart and is used mainly to assess rhythm and arrhythmias. One shows anatomy and motion; the other shows the electrical signal. In many cases the veterinarian combines both.
Does an echocardiography hurt or require anesthesia for my pet?
It is not painful. It is a non-invasive procedure that uses ultrasound, the same principle as an abdominal ultrasound. Most dogs and cats tolerate it awake, in lateral recumbency and with gentle restraint. Light sedation is used only if the animal is very stressed or uncooperative, at the veterinarian's discretion. It does not use radiation.
What exactly does an echocardiography evaluate?
It evaluates the structure and function of the heart: the size and thickness of the chambers and walls, the condition and motion of the valves, contractility (systolic function) and, through Doppler, the direction and velocity of blood flow. It also detects fluid in the pericardium and helps characterize murmurs and suspected heart disease. Interpretation always belongs to a veterinarian.
When is an echocardiography indicated?
It is indicated when a murmur is heard on auscultation, in the presence of signs such as cough, fatigue, fainting or breathing difficulty of possible cardiac origin, in the follow-up of a known heart condition, and as a pre-surgical or pre-flight evaluation when cardiac risk needs to be assessed. The decision to perform it belongs to the attending veterinarian on a case-by-case basis.
Is an echocardiography a requirement to export my pet?
In general it is not a standard zoosanitary export requirement; official requirements revolve around vaccination, serology and certification. However, it may be requested as a pre-flight clinical evaluation in animals with a murmur, advanced age or a cardiac history, to assess whether they can safely tolerate air transport. It is a diagnostic tool, not a documentary formality.
