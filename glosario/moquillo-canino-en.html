Canine distemper: signs, phases and prevention through vaccination

Canine distemper is a systemic, highly contagious viral infection caused by a Morbillivirus (CDV). It affects several body systems at once, is especially severe in puppies, and its prevention relies on core vaccination.

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 12 May 2026

Definition based on veterinary clinical literature and vaccination guidelines · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

Morbillivirus (CDV) · highly contagious · preventable by core vaccine

Canine distemper is an infectious disease caused by the canine distemper virus (CDV), a Morbillivirus of the family Paramyxoviridae. It is highly contagious and simultaneously affects the respiratory, digestive, integumentary and nervous systems. There is no specific antiviral treatment: management is supportive and the prognosis worsens once neurological signs appear. Prevention relies on core vaccination, considered essential for all dogs.

Formal technical definition

The canine distemper virus (CDV) belongs to the genus Morbillivirus, family Paramyxoviridae, related to the human measles virus. It is transmitted mainly through aerosols and respiratory secretions between dogs, and can also affect various wild carnivores. After infection, the virus spreads via the lymphatic and blood routes (viraemia) to the respiratory, gastrointestinal and urinary epithelia, and to the central nervous system. The immunosuppression it induces favours secondary bacterial infections that worsen the clinical picture. The disease is especially severe in puppies and unvaccinated dogs, in which mortality can be high.

Multisystemic clinical signs

Distemper produces signs in several systems at once, with variable intensity. In the early phase, fever, lethargy, ocular and nasal discharge and conjunctivitis are frequent. At the respiratory level, coughing and breathing difficulty appear, often complicated by secondary bacterial pneumonia. At the digestive level, anorexia, vomiting and diarrhoea are common and may cause dehydration. Weeks later, neurological signs may arise —myoclonus, incoordination, behavioural changes and seizures— which usually indicate a worse prognosis. Hyperkeratosis of the footpads and nose (hard pad disease) may also be seen and, in puppies infected during dental development, enamel hypoplasia.

Phases of the infection

  • Aerosol transmissionContact with respiratory secretions from an infected dog. The virus enters via the respiratory route.
  • Initial replication and viraemiaThe virus multiplies in lymphoid tissue and spreads through the body; initial fever and immunosuppression may occur.
  • Systemic phaseRespiratory, digestive and ocular signs. Immunosuppression favours secondary bacterial infections.
  • Neurological phaseWeeks later, nervous signs may appear, even after an apparent improvement; they usually indicate a worse prognosis.
  • Recovery or sequelaeSome dogs recover; others are left with neurological sequelae or persistent myoclonus.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis relies on combining the clinical and vaccination history, compatible multisystemic signs and laboratory tests. Viral genome detection techniques (RT-PCR) are used on respiratory, conjunctival, blood or urine samples, along with serology. The context —age, vaccination status and contact with sick dogs— is key to interpreting the results. Diagnosis and management must always remain in the hands of the treating veterinarian.

Prevention through vaccination

Prevention of distemper relies on core vaccination, considered essential for all dogs by the leading veterinary guidelines. The CDV vaccine is part of the basic canine vaccination plans and is administered according to the protocol and boosters defined by the veterinarian. Keeping the canine population's vaccination up to date reduces circulation of the virus. You can learn how we organise vaccination plans in our vaccination service. This content is informational, does not replace veterinary evaluation and does not indicate specific doses.

Common errors

  • Considering a puppy fully protected before the vaccination plan is complete. Protection builds over the course the veterinarian indicates, not with a single vaccine contact.
  • Mistaking distemper for a simple cold. The early respiratory signs may seem mild, but distemper is a serious systemic disease that requires veterinary assessment.
  • Exposing unvaccinated puppies to dogs or risky environments. Contact before the primary vaccination course is complete increases the risk of infection.
  • Interpreting improvement of respiratory or digestive signs as definitive cure. Neurological signs may appear weeks after an apparent recovery.
  • Self-medicating. There is no specific over-the-counter antiviral; supportive management must be directed by the veterinarian.

Frequently asked questions

What is canine distemper and how is it transmitted?
Canine distemper is a systemic viral infection caused by the canine distemper virus (CDV), a Morbillivirus. It is highly contagious and spreads mainly through aerosols and respiratory secretions between dogs. It affects several body systems at once, which is why its signs are so varied.
What are the first signs of distemper?
Early on, fever, lethargy, ocular and nasal discharge, coughing and, frequently, digestive signs such as vomiting or diarrhoea usually appear. These signs may seem mild at first, but they correspond to a serious disease, so a veterinarian should be consulted as soon as they appear.
Why is distemper so severe in puppies?
Puppies and unvaccinated dogs have an immune system that is less prepared against CDV. In them the disease tends to be more severe, with a higher risk of respiratory complications, dehydration, neurological signs and mortality. That is why early vaccination according to the veterinarian's plan is so important.
Is there a cure for distemper?
There is no specific antiviral treatment against CDV. Management is supportive —hydration, control of secondary infections and of the signs— and must be directed by the veterinarian. The prognosis worsens when neurological signs appear, which may leave lasting sequelae.
How is canine distemper prevented?
Prevention is based on core vaccination against CDV, considered essential for all dogs. The protocol and boosters are defined by the veterinarian. Avoiding contact of unvaccinated puppies with dogs or risky environments until the primary vaccination course is complete also helps prevent infection.
