Cataract in dogs and cats: causes, diagnosis and options

A cataract is a clouding of the lens that progressively reduces vision. Telling it apart from nuclear sclerosis and detecting it early is key to deciding management.

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 12 May 2026

Clinical definition for informational purposes · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

Clouding of the lens · Reduces vision · Not the same as nuclear sclerosis

A cataract is the loss of transparency of the lens, the natural lens that focuses light inside the eye. When the lens clouds over, light no longer reaches the retina properly and vision decreases. It may be a small spot or affect the whole lens, and its progression varies with the cause. It should not be confused with nuclear sclerosis, a normal age-related change that does not block vision. When the pupil looks whitish, assessment by a veterinary ophthalmologist is the right step.

Formal technical definition

The lens is a transparent, avascular structure suspended behind the iris that focuses the image onto the retina. A cataract is defined as any opacity of the lens or its capsule, regardless of size. As it loses transparency, it scatters and blocks light, which reduces visual acuity and, in mature cataracts, can lead to blindness of the affected eye. By extent it is classified as incipient, immature, mature and hypermature; by age of onset, as congenital, juvenile or senile. Diagnosis and treatment indication are the remit of the veterinary ophthalmologist. Learn more through our veterinary ophthalmology service.

Main causes

Cataracts have several causes, and in many patients they combine. The most frequent in small-animal practice are:

Hereditary and congenital

This is the most common cause in dogs. Many breeds have a genetic predisposition to develop cataracts at various ages. Some cataracts are present from birth (congenital), whether or not associated with other ocular abnormalities.

Senile (age-related)

With ageing, the lens may develop opacities. It is important not to confuse a true senile cataract with nuclear sclerosis, which is a normal physiological change in the older animal.

Diabetic (metabolic)

In dogs, diabetes mellitus is a very relevant cause: most diabetic dogs develop cataracts, sometimes with rapid onset. In cats they are far less frequent by this route.

Cataract vs. nuclear sclerosis: do not confuse them

Both give the eye a greyish or cloudy appearance and are easily mistaken, yet they are different things. This is the key difference:

NaturePathological opacity of the lensNormal age-related physiological hardening
AppearanceOpaque white, may block the view of the fundusBluish-grey tone, translucent
Effect on visionReduces vision; may cause blindnessMinimal or no impact on vision
Typical ageAny age depending on the causeOlder animals (usually > 6-8 years)
ManagementOphthalmic assessment; sometimes surgeryNo treatment needed; monitoring only

Signs and stages of a cataract

The signs families notice most are a pupil that looks white or cloudy and behavioural changes from vision loss: the animal trips, bumps into furniture, hesitates on stairs or in low light. Progression usually follows these stages:

  • IncipientA small opacity affecting little of the lens surface. Vision is still functional; it is detected on examination.
  • ImmatureThe opacity advances but part of the fundus can still be seen. Vision begins to be compromised.
  • MatureThe lens is completely opaque; no useful light reaches the retina. There is usually blindness of the affected eye.
  • HypermatureThe lens resorbs or degrades; it may be accompanied by intraocular inflammation requiring control.

Common errors

  • Confusing a cataract with nuclear sclerosis. The cloudy look of an older animal's eye is often nuclear sclerosis, not a cataract. Only an ophthalmic examination tells them apart with certainty.
  • Believing drops cure a cataract. No eye drop or supplement reverses a cataract once formed. Claiming otherwise creates false expectations and delays consultation.
  • Not checking the diabetic dog's eyes. Every diabetic dog should have ophthalmic monitoring, because diabetic cataracts can appear fast and become complicated.
  • Assuming a cataract always means irreversible blindness. Many cataracts are surgical candidates if the retina is healthy; early assessment widens the options.
  • Waiting too long. A mature or hypermature cataract can inflame the eye (uveitis) and worsen the prognosis; timely evaluation matters.

Frequently asked questions

What exactly is a cataract in dogs and cats?
A cataract is a clouding of the lens, the natural lens inside the eye. When the lens loses its transparency, light no longer reaches the retina properly and vision decreases progressively. It may affect a small portion of the lens or make it completely opaque. The degree of vision loss depends on how much of the lens surface is clouded.
Is a cataract the same as nuclear sclerosis?
No, and it is a very common confusion. Nuclear sclerosis is a normal age-related hardening of the lens that gives the eye a bluish-grey tone, but it lets light through and does not significantly reduce vision. A cataract does block light and does compromise vision. To the naked eye they can look identical; only an ophthalmic examination can tell them apart with certainty.
Why did my diabetic dog develop cataracts so quickly?
In dogs, diabetes alters sugar metabolism within the lens, and most diabetic dogs develop cataracts, sometimes within a few weeks or months of diagnosis. That is why every diabetic dog should have metabolic control and an ophthalmic evaluation. Managing the diabetes and assessing the eye must go hand in hand.
Can cataracts be cured with drops or eye drops?
There is no eye drop or medical treatment that reverses a cataract once it has formed. The only treatment that can restore vision is surgery (phacoemulsification), when the case is a candidate and the retina is healthy. The decision to operate is made by a veterinary ophthalmologist after a full examination. This entry is informational and does not replace clinical assessment.
How is a cataract diagnosed?
Diagnosis is made through a complete ophthalmic examination, usually with pupil dilation, a slit lamp and fundus evaluation. The ophthalmologist confirms that the opacity is in the lens (and not another cause), determines its maturity and assesses whether the retina is functional, which defines whether the patient could benefit from surgery.
