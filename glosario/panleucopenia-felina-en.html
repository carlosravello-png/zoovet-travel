Feline panleukopenia: contagion, signs, severity and prevention by vaccination

Feline panleukopenia is a highly contagious viral infection caused by feline parvovirus, especially severe in kittens. Prevention relies on core vaccination and hygiene.

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 12 May 2026

Definition based on documentary practice 2013-2026 · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

Parvovirus felino · muy contagioso y resistente · grave en gatitos

Feline panleukopenia is a highly contagious systemic viral infection of cats, caused by feline parvovirus (FPV), a virus that is extremely resistant in the environment. It is characterised by fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, dehydration and a marked drop in white blood cells (leukopenia). It is especially dangerous in unvaccinated kittens. Prevention relies on core vaccination, hygiene and isolation of sick animals.

Formal technical definition

The causative agent is the feline panleukopenia virus (FPV), a parvovirus of the family Parvoviridae, closely related to canine parvovirus. It is transmitted faecal-orally and through contact with secretions, objects, footwear or contaminated hands; the virus can persist in the environment for months and resists many common disinfectants. Once inside the body, it attacks rapidly dividing tissues —bone marrow, intestinal lining and, in pregnant queens, the nervous system of the fetus—, which explains the characteristic leukopenia and the gastrointestinal picture. Its control depends on immunisation of the feline population and strict biosecurity measures.

Contagion and transmission

Feline parvovirus is shed in large amounts in the faeces of infected cats and contaminates the environment for a long time. Transmission occurs by direct faecal-oral route, but also indirectly through food bowls, litter trays, clothing, footwear and hands. Because of its strong resistance, an environment where a sick cat lived may remain infectious for months. Unvaccinated cats of any age are susceptible, although kittens and immunocompromised animals face the highest risk. Isolation of cases, cleaning with disinfectants effective against parvovirus and vaccination are the pillars of control. For animals about to travel, it is advisable to review their health and vaccination status in a pre-travel feline evaluation.

Clinical course and prevention

  • Exposure to the virusContact with faeces, objects or environments contaminated by feline parvovirus.
  • IncubationUsual period before the first signs appear, according to clinical sources.
  • Gastrointestinal signs and leukopeniaFever, vomiting, diarrhoea, dehydration and a drop in white blood cells.
  • Veterinary managementSupportive treatment and clinical decisions always rest with the treating veterinarian.
  • Core vaccinationImmunisation against feline parvovirus and hygiene are the basis of prevention.

Common errors

  • Believing an indoor cat does not need the vaccine. Feline parvovirus can enter the home on footwear, clothing and objects because of its strong environmental resistance.
  • Assuming any disinfectant kills the virus. Parvovirus resists many common cleaning products; disinfectants specifically effective against parvovirus are required.
  • Confusing panleukopenia with simple indigestion. Vomiting and diarrhoea with lethargy in a kitten are an emergency, not a passing upset.
  • Introducing a new cat without knowing its vaccination status. Adding an animal of unknown origin without initial isolation can expose the rest of the household.
  • Interrupting a kitten's vaccination plan before completing it. The protection from the primary course depends on completing the schedule set by the veterinarian.

Frequently asked questions

What is feline panleukopenia?
It is a highly contagious systemic viral infection of cats caused by feline parvovirus (FPV). The virus attacks rapidly dividing tissues, such as the bone marrow and intestinal lining, causing a marked drop in white blood cells (leukopenia) and severe gastrointestinal signs. It is especially dangerous in kittens and unvaccinated cats.
How is feline panleukopenia transmitted?
Transmission is mainly faecal-oral: infected cats shed large amounts of virus in their faeces. It also spreads indirectly through food bowls, litter trays, clothing, footwear and contaminated hands. Feline parvovirus is highly resistant and can persist in the environment for months, so a space where a sick cat lived may remain infectious for a long time.
Why is it so severe in kittens?
Kittens have an immature immune system and fewer reserves against dehydration. The combination of fever, vomiting, diarrhoea and leukopenia can worsen their condition within hours. That is why lethargy, refusal to eat or diarrhoea in a kitten warrants an urgent veterinary consultation rather than waiting at home.
Can feline panleukopenia be prevented?
Yes. Prevention is based on core vaccination against feline parvovirus, considered essential for all cats, together with hygiene measures, isolation of cases and cleaning with disinfectants effective against parvovirus. The schedule and number of doses are determined by the veterinarian according to the animal's age and condition; this glossary does not state doses.
Does my indoor cat also need the vaccine?
Yes. Even if it never goes outside, feline parvovirus can enter the home on footwear, clothing and contaminated objects because of its strong environmental resistance. For that reason the panleukopenia vaccine is considered core even for cats that live strictly indoors. The final decision always rests with the treating veterinarian.
