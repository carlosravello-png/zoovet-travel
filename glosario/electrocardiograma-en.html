Electrocardiography (ECG): what it measures, what it detects and when it is indicated

The electrocardiogram is a non-invasive diagnostic test that records the electrical activity of the heart. It is an assessment procedure, not a disease.

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dra. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 12 May 2026

Definition based on documentary practice 2013-2026 · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

Diagnostic test · electrical activity of the heart · non-invasive

The electrocardiogram (ECG, also called EKG or the heart's "electrical trace") is the graphic recording of the heart's electrical activity captured through electrodes on the body surface. It assesses the rhythm, the rate and the conduction of the cardiac impulse. It is a diagnostic procedure —not a disease— and is used to detect and characterize arrhythmias. It does not directly measure the structure of the heart: for that, echocardiography is used.

Formal technical definition

The electrocardiogram records, over time, the potential differences generated by depolarization and repolarization of the myocardium. Each heartbeat produces a characteristic sequence: the P wave (atrial depolarization), the QRS complex (ventricular depolarization) and the T wave (ventricular repolarization). From this trace, the heart rate, the regularity of the rhythm, the origin of the impulse and the conduction times are analyzed. In veterinary practice the recording is usually obtained with limb leads, with the patient in lateral recumbency and in a quiet environment to minimize artifacts. The ECG is a clinical tool for cardiological interpretation, not a certificate or a documentary export requirement.

What the ECG detects

The value of the electrocardiogram lies in the analysis of rhythm and conduction. It is the test of choice to document and classify arrhythmias, which often cannot be characterized by auscultation alone.

FindingWhat the ECG provides
ArrhythmiasIdentifies and classifies abnormal rhythms: premature complexes, tachycardias, atrial fibrillation, blocks.
Conduction disturbancesDetects atrioventricular blocks and other disorders of the conduction system.
Heart rateQuantifies the number of beats per minute and their regularity.
Underlying rhythmDetermines whether the impulse arises in the sinus node or in an abnormal focus.

When it is indicated

The indication is always established by the attending veterinarian after the clinical examination. The ECG is requested, among other situations, in the following contexts:

Clinical situationReason
Syncope or faintingRule out arrhythmias as a cause of transient loss of consciousness.
Arrhythmia or murmur on auscultationCharacterize an irregular rhythm detected on examination.
Heart disease follow-upMonitor the evolution of the rhythm in an already diseased heart.
Pre-surgical assessmentEvaluate anesthetic risk before surgery in selected patients.
Pre-flight assessmentPart of the fitness-to-fly evaluation in cardiac at-risk patients.

Difference from echocardiography

It is a common mistake to confuse the electrocardiogram with the echocardiogram. They are not the same test and do not measure the same thing, and in cardiological practice they complement each other.

AspectElectrocardiogram (ECG)Echocardiogram
What it measuresThe heart's electrical activityThe heart's structure and movement (imaging)
TechnologySurface electrodesUltrasound
Mainly assessesRhythm, rate and conductionChambers, valves and contractility
Detects wellArrhythmias and blocksStructural and functional lesions

In many patients the cardiologist orders both tests: the ECG documents the rhythm and the echocardiogram shows the structure. One does not replace the other. You can see how we integrate both assessments in our ultrasound and cardiology service.

Common interpretation errors

  • Believing a normal ECG rules out all heart disease. A normal trace reflects the rhythm at that moment; it does not rule out structural lesions or intermittent arrhythmias.
  • Confusing the ECG with the echocardiogram. The ECG measures electricity; the echocardiogram measures structure. They are complementary, not interchangeable.
  • Interpreting a trace full of artifacts. Trembling, panting or patient movement introduce artifacts that can mimic abnormalities. Recording technique matters.
  • Expecting the ECG to assess the strength of contraction. Contractility and pump function are assessed with imaging, not with the electrical trace.
  • Using the ECG as if it were an export requirement. It is not a zoosanitary certificate; it is a clinical tool that, in at-risk patients, can support the fitness-to-fly assessment.

Frequently asked questions

Is an electrocardiogram painful or does it require anesthesia?
No. The electrocardiogram is a non-invasive, painless test. Contact electrodes are placed on the skin, usually on the four limbs, and the recording takes only a few minutes. In the vast majority of cases it requires no sedation or anesthesia; the animal only needs to stay relatively still to reduce motion artifacts.
What is the difference between an electrocardiogram and an echocardiogram?
The electrocardiogram records the electrical activity of the heart and assesses rhythm, rate and conduction. The echocardiogram is an ultrasound that shows the structure and movement of the heart (chambers, valves, contractility). They do not compete: they are complementary tests. An ECG can detect an arrhythmia that the echocardiogram does not fully explain, and an echocardiogram can show a structural lesion the ECG cannot see. Cardiologists usually combine them.
When is an electrocardiogram recommended for my pet?
It is indicated for fainting or syncope episodes, unexplained weakness, when an irregular rhythm or a murmur is heard on auscultation, in the follow-up of a known heart disease, and as part of the assessment before surgery or a long-distance flight in at-risk patients. The final decision always rests with the attending veterinarian after the clinical examination.
Can an electrocardiogram be requested to travel with or export my pet?
The ECG is not a standard documentary export requirement, unlike the rabies vaccine or serology. However, it can be part of the fitness-to-fly assessment when the animal has a cardiac history, is elderly or is a higher-risk brachycephalic breed. It is a clinical tool, not a zoosanitary certificate.
Does a normal electrocardiogram mean the heart is healthy?
Not necessarily. A normal ECG indicates that, at the moment of recording, the electrical activity and rhythm were normal. It does not rule out structural diseases (for example, valvular insufficiency or a cardiomyopathy) or arrhythmias that appear intermittently. That is why, depending on the case, it is complemented with echocardiography, radiography or a prolonged Holter recording.
