Lapse in vaccination coverage — the mistake that resets every clock

A booster given one day late turns the vaccine into a primary vaccination and voids the titration test

Author: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo Clinical review: Dr. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, CMVP 12434 Published: 11 July 2026

Definition based on documentary practice 2013-2026 · Zoovet Travel, Trujillo, Peru

Quick definition

One day late = start over

It is the quietest trap in the whole file, because it does not spring at the airport or in the laboratory: it springs in the diary. A lapse in vaccination coverage is what happens when the rabies booster is given after the previous vaccine has expired.

And the consequence is wildly out of proportion to the oversight: that vaccine stops being a booster and starts counting as a primary vaccination. With that, the rabies antibody titration test already done and already paid for stops counting, however perfect the result was.

Everything has to start again: new vaccine, wait 30 days, new blood draw, new laboratory, new wait. In the European Union that means three more months. All because of a booster given late.

Formal technical definition

The mechanism is the same in both major regulatory blocs, even if each words it differently.

United States (CDC): titration results are valid for the life of the dog — but only as long as vaccination coverage is not interrupted (and as long as the laboratory remains approved). If there is a lapse, the dog must receive a new rabies vaccine and a new titration test, with the sample drawn at least 30 days after the booster.

European Union: boosters must be administered before the previous vaccine expires. Otherwise, the next vaccination is treated as a primary vaccination: 30 days must pass before the titration test can be run, and then 3 months from the blood draw before the animal can travel.

The public-health logic behind it is coherent: what an antibody titre certifies is not a moment, but continuous protection. A gap in coverage breaks that continuity, and the document that attested to it loses its foundation.

One nuance that does help: the CDC accepts that a dog with a history of several valid vaccines given after the microchip may have its sample drawn at any time after a booster — provided its first vaccine was administered at least 30 days before the draw and there have been no lapses. Continuity is, once again, the condition.

Regulatory context in export

For anyone exporting, a lapse is not a theoretical concept: it is the single most frequent reason files have to be rebuilt from scratch. And it has a dangerous property — it gives no warning. Nobody calls to say the vaccine expired yesterday.

That is why tracking the rabies vaccine's expiry date is not the owner's chore but part of the file itself. It is also one of the reasons the CDC asks, in its travel checklist, that you confirm 60 days or more before departure that the dog's vaccine will not expire during the trip.

And there is a cruel asymmetry: the longer the destination's calendar, the more expensive the fall. On a trip to the United States, a lapse costs 30 days and a new titration test. On a trip to the European Union, it costs four months.

Typical timeline

  • Valid rabies vaccineWith the microchip already in place. It carries a specific expiry date.
  • Titration testSatisfactory result. In principle, valid for life.
  • The vaccine expiresThis is the critical point. The booster must be given before this date, not after.
  • Booster on timeCoverage continues. The titration test still counts. Nothing else needs doing.
  • Late booster = lapseThe vaccine counts as a primary vaccination. The titration test is void. The whole calendar goes back to zero.

Common errors

  • Trusting your memory. The rabies vaccine's expiry date should be written into the file and checked before any travel decision, not recalled.
  • Giving the booster "a few days later" because the vet had no appointment. A few days later is a lapse. The rule allows no tolerance.
  • Assuming a high titre protects you from a lapse. It does not: the numerical value is irrelevant if coverage was broken. What is certified is continuity, not the number.
  • Not checking that the vaccine stays valid throughout the trip. The CDC asks for this expressly in its checklist: the vaccine must not expire while the dog is travelling.
  • Buying the ticket without checking the vaccine date. A lapse discovered late cannot be fixed: it can only be paid for.

Frequently asked questions

What exactly is a lapse in vaccination coverage?
It is the interruption of rabies coverage that occurs when the booster is given after the previous vaccine has expired. It makes no difference whether it is one day or one month: if the booster is late, there is a lapse.
Does a lapse void my rabies antibody titration test?
Yes. Both the CDC and the European Union establish that the lifetime validity of the titration test is conditional on there being no lapses in vaccination. If there is one, the titration stops counting and must be repeated with a sample drawn at least 30 days after the new vaccine.
I gave the booster three days late. Does that matter?
Yes. The rule allows no margin of tolerance: three days late is a lapse. The new vaccine counts as a primary vaccination, you must wait 30 days to run a new titration test and —if the destination is the European Union— another 3 months from that new blood draw before travelling.
Does a very high antibody titre help at all?
Not for this. The numerical value is irrelevant if vaccination coverage was broken. What the rule certifies is not a high figure at a single moment, but continuous protection over time. Once continuity is broken, the number does not rebuild it.
How do I avoid a lapse?
By writing the rabies vaccine's expiry date into the file and checking it before any travel decision. The CDC recommends confirming, 60 days or more before the trip, that the vaccine will not expire while the dog is in transit. It is the one requirement in the file that gives no warning at all when it is breached.
