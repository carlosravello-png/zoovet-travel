Traveling to the US Without FAVN: the 3 Blocking Points, Real Costs and Why Nobody Tells You Until It's Too Late

⚠ Regulatory alert · 11 July 2026

The CDC removed laboratories from its approved list. A rabies titre is valid for life only as long as the laboratory that performed it remains approved by the CDC. If it is dropped, the test stops counting — even if correct and fully paid for. Read the full alert →

This is not a guide to what the regulations say. It is an analysis of what actually happens when an owner arrives at the airport — or is already in the US — with a dog from a high-risk country and no valid antibody titer. The data comes from documented forum cases, Final Rule 89 FR 38450 and 13 years of operational pet export management from Peru.

Clinical authorship: Dr. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, DVM (ORCID 0009-0002-6837-5311 · CMVP 12434 · Wikidata Q138881218) · Operational section: Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo, Commercial Director · FAVN Repository DOI 10.5281/zenodo.19797479
⚠

Peru is classified as a High-Risk Country (DMRVV) on the CDC list. Every dog entering the US from Peru needs an antibody titer ≥0.5 IU/mL and an ACF reservation. Without both: the process is blocked. This classification also applies to Bolivia, Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador, Venezuela and 20+ other countries.

Blocking sequence

The 3 points where the process is blocked — in order

There is not a single checkpoint. There are three distinct moments in the chain where missing documentation stops the journey. The worst of the three, financially, is the third. The most avoidable, logistically, is the first.

  • 1

    Airline check-in counter — Lima (or city of origin)

    When presenting the animal for boarding, the agent checks that the CDC Dog Import Form is completed and the ACF reservation confirmed. If either is missing or the airport does not match the reservation: boarding is denied. The cost at this point is zero in quarantine, but the flight is lost.

    Consequence: missed flight · no quarantine cost · checked luggage may be a complication
  • 2

    Transit layover — connecting airports

    If the flight has a layover at an airport that checks pet documentation in transit, paperwork may be reviewed before reaching the US. Mexico City (MEX), Bogotá (BOG) and some Central American airports conduct intermittent checks. This checkpoint is the least predictable.

    Consequence: animal held in transit · cost of boarding at transit origin · owner travels on alone or waits
  • 3

    US port of entry — CDC/CBP inspection

    This is the definitive and most expensive checkpoint. At the 6 airports with approved ACFs, CBP (backed by CDC) reviews documentation before the animal can leave the arrivals area. Invalid documentation, missing or expired FAVN, microchip that doesn't match: the dog is transferred to the ACF. The 28-day quarantine begins.

    Consequence: mandatory 28-day quarantine · USD 900–5,000+ · USDA re-vaccination · no early release
Why checkpoint 1 is the easiest to prevent: The airline has the legal obligation to deny boarding. If an agent misses the check in error, the animal still arrives in the US — but the full quarantine cost falls on the owner. The airline does not refund the flight ticket in these cases.

The 6 approved ACFs: facilities and real quarantine costs

Only 6 airports in the US have a CDC-approved Animal Care Facility (ACF) to receive dogs from high-risk countries. The owner must reserve at the ACF for the arrival airport before flying. The airport cannot be changed once the process has begun — the CDC Dog Import Form is for a single trip.

Documented errors

The most common errors in cases from Peru and Colombia

Documented in specialist forums (2024–2026) and in Zoovet Travel's operational experience. These are not hypothetical — they are repeating patterns.

Error #1 — Most frequent

Microchip implanted after the rabies vaccine

The CDC requires that the microchip precede the valid vaccine. If the vaccine was given before the chip, that dose does not count. The protocol must be restarted from scratch: new chip, new vaccine, new 30-day wait, new blood draw, new result. In practice: a delay of at least 2–3 months.

Error #2 — Blood draw timing

Blood drawn before 30 days post-vaccination

The FAVN sample must be taken at least 30 days after the valid vaccine. Even one day's difference invalidates the result. The laboratory processes the sample and returns a positive result — but CDC rejects the certificate because the date does not meet the chronological requirement. The process must be repeated from blood draw.

Error #3 — Official documentation

CDC vaccination certificate without SENASA endorsement

The vaccination certificate in CDC format must be signed and endorsed by an official SENASA veterinarian — the signature of the private vet who administered the vaccine is not sufficient. This endorsement converts the document into an official Peruvian state certificate recognised by the FDA/CBP. Many owners do not know this step exists until they are at the airport.

Error #4 — Form and logistics

Wrong airport on the CDC form compared to the purchased flight

The CDC Dog Import Form is valid for a single trip and a single arrival airport. A last-minute itinerary change — a different connecting flight that lands at a different airport than the one reserved — invalidates the ACF reservation and the form. CBP can order immediate return on the next flight, at the owner's or airline's expense.

Error #5 — Expired vaccine in transit

Rabies vaccine expires during travel or at destination

Some vaccines are valid for 1 year, others for 3. If the vaccine expires on the arrival date or in the immediately following days, CBP may consider the document invalid. The solution is to check the expiry date with enough lead time to re-vaccinate, wait 30 days, and take a new sample if the previous FAVN's validity has also expired.

Legal framework

The federal legal basis behind the quarantine

42 CFR § 71.51 — Control of imported dogs
Code of Federal Regulations, Title 42 — Public Health

The regulation establishes that dogs from countries with a risk of canine rabies may only be admitted to the US if they meet vaccination requirements and, where applicable, verified antibody titer requirements. The CDC has the authority to hold, redirect to quarantine or return any animal that does not meet requirements at the border inspection.
Final Rule 89 FR 38450 — August 2024
Federal Register, Vol. 89, No. 154 — CDC Dog Import

The 2024 final rule tightened controls specifically for dogs from high-risk countries for Dog-Maintained Rabies Virus Variant (DMRVV). Key operational changes: (a) ACF reservation became mandatory before boarding, not just recommended; (b) the CDC Dog Import Form must be completed before travel and cannot be modified once started; (c) airlines are responsible for verifying documentation at boarding and may face penalties if they board animals without the requirements met. Background: the suspension of imports from Egypt (2019–2020) and a suspected rabies importation case that generated public health response costs exceeding USD 400,000.

Primary sources: CDC Dog Importation · 42 CFR § 71.51 (eCFR) · FR 89 FR 38450

Operational position

Why Zoovet won't start a case without the complete protocol in place

Our policy is not bureaucracy — it's owner protection

In 13 years of managing international pet exports from Peru, we have seen what happens when any step of the protocol is skipped. There is no good version of that scenario. The minimum cost of an ACF quarantine — USD 900 at the cheapest airport — already exceeds the total cost of the complete protocol managed from the start. The emotional cost has no price.

Our position is simple: we do not start the certification process if the animal does not meet the prior requirements. This means a microchip implanted before the vaccine, a valid vaccine with a verifiable date, a blood draw taken on the correct day, and a FAVN result ≥0.5 IU/mL from the approved laboratory. If any of these points is not met, we correct it before starting the process — not after.

We have declined to start cases with owners who insisted on "trying anyway." We do not do it because the animal pays the price — literally.

Frequently asked questions

Questions owners ask before and after the problem

What exactly happens if my dog arrives in the US without a valid FAVN titer?

The dog is detained at CBP/CDC inspection and transferred to the assigned ACF. Mandatory 28-day quarantine. Full cost borne by the owner: USD 1,400–5,000+ depending on airport and animal size. The dog is re-vaccinated with a USDA-approved vaccine. There is no way to shorten the quarantine or release the animal before day 28.

At which exact point can I be stopped? Can it happen before leaving Peru?

Three checkpoints: (1) airline check-in counter in Lima — boarding denied if the CDC Form or ACF reservation is missing; (2) transit layover airports — intermittent checks at some Latin American airports; (3) CDC/CBP inspection at the US arrival airport — the most expensive of all.

What does ACF quarantine really cost?

Between USD 900 and USD 5,000+ depending on the airport. ATL, PHL and IAD are the most affordable (~900–1,400). LAX and MIA are mid-range (~1,400–2,500). JFK is the most expensive (~3,900–5,000+). Cost includes veterinary exam, re-vaccination and 28-day boarding. Extra charges for size, overnight hours and weekends.

What is the CDC Dog Import Form and why is it different from the FAVN?

Two different things. The CDC Dog Import Form is the online form completed before travel, declaring FAVN status and reserving the ACF if needed. The FAVN is the laboratory result proving immunity. Peru is a high-risk country: both are needed. Having FAVN without the form, or the form without FAVN, is not sufficient.

Will the airline warn me if I'm missing documentation?

Not reliably. The airline should verify and deny boarding, but verification errors occur. If the agent misses a problem and the animal arrives in the US with invalid documentation, the quarantine cost is the owner's — not the airline's.

Why won't Zoovet start a case without the complete FAVN protocol?

Because quarantine cost always exceeds the cost of the full protocol. In 13 years we have seen every possible error. None ends without additional cost. Our policy: if the animal doesn't have a microchip prior to the vaccine, a valid vaccine, a blood draw on the correct day, and a result ≥0.5 IU/mL, we do not start the process. No exception.
