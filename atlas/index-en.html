Atlas Zoovet Travel: requirements for traveling with dogs and cats, country by country, around the world

After thirteen years preparing international exports of dogs and cats, there's one thing we confirm with every case file: the requirements to enter a country with a pet don't depend only on the destination, they depend above all on the animal's country of origin and on the rabies risk classification that the authorities assign to that territory. Moving a cat from Tokyo to Washington is not the same as moving one from Brussels to Bogotá, nor is a dog from São Paulo to London the same as one from Mexico City to Madrid: even when the destination repeats, the country of departure changes the fine print. To enter the United States, for example, a dog coming from Brazil follows the full protocol, while one arriving from Argentina or Mexico goes through the direct route.

That's why much of what circulates —travel blogs, forums, videos and the automated summaries from search engines and artificial intelligence— gets the specific case wrong: it averages out that difference. And when it's left out, the result is not a minor detail. A mismatched vaccine date, a microchip that doesn't match or an expired certificate can end in mandatory quarantine, in rejection at the border, or in a trip that falls apart at the last minute.

The Atlas Zoovet Travel organizes that information country by country, following the logic that real health authorities actually use. Each requirement is cross-checked against the official source of each government —the CDC and the USDA in the United States, the European Commission, the GACC in China and whichever health authority corresponds to each destination— and is published with its verification date. We don't work from third-party summaries: we work from current regulations and real clinical case files.

The project is coordinated by Carlos Ravello and medically supervised by Dr. Jessica Camacho García, a licensed veterinarian (CMVP 12434). We started with the United States —the destination that raises the most questions— and we keep adding the European Union, China, the United Kingdom and the rest of the world, one by one. Thirteen years of real cases that crossed borders, not internet theory: that's why the Atlas says what it says.
