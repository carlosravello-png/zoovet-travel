Requirements to enter the United States with your pet (2026): the rule changes depending on your country

To enter the United States with your pet, the first thing that defines everything is the country it leaves from: the US classifies each country by canine rabies risk. If your country is low risk (direct entry), your dog needs an ISO microchip, a rabies vaccine, to be at least 6 months old and the free CDC Dog Import Form. If your country is high risk (full protocol) —like Peru, Brazil, Colombia or India—, a rabies antibody titration at a CDC-approved laboratory is also required or, failing that, 28 days of quarantine, and entry is allowed only through 6 airports with an ACF facility. On top of this, for countries affected by the screwworm (South America and Mexico), an official veterinary certificate of inspection within the 5 days before travel is required. Cats have a simpler process: no rabies test is required, they just need to arrive healthy.

First step: locate your country

The United States does not treat every country the same. Find yours: the table tells you whether direct entry or the full protocol applies to you. It is the starting point of your entire timeline.

Important: "direct entry" does not mean "no requirements". Every dog, wherever it comes from, needs an ISO microchip, a current rabies vaccine and the CDC form. What changes is whether you are also required to have the serological titration, the quarantine and the restricted airports.

If your country is FULL PROTOCOL (high rabies risk)

This applies equally to all high-risk countries —Peru, Brazil, Colombia, India, the Philippines, China and the rest—: the procedure is the same, what changes is the authority that signs your papers in your country. For a dog with a vaccine issued outside the US, the path is:

  1. ISO microchip (11784/11785), implanted before the rabies vaccine. If it is done the other way around, the vaccine is not valid.
  2. Rabies vaccine: first dose at 12 weeks (84 days) at the earliest, and administered at least 28 days before entering.
  3. Valid rabies serological titration (FAVN/RNATT) from a CDC-approved laboratory. What matters is that it is valid at the time of travel: once approved, it usually does not have to be repeated for each trip as long as your dog keeps its rabies vaccination up to date. No valid titration → 28 days of quarantine at an ACF facility. The validity and the exact sample-collection timing are confirmed by the laboratory and your agency or veterinarian.
  4. CDC Dog Import Form (free, online, one per dog).
  5. Rabies vaccine and microchip certification: the CDC form for dogs with a foreign vaccine, completed by your veterinarian and endorsed by the official authority of your country (in Peru, SENASA; in Colombia, ICA; in Bolivia, SENASAG; in Ecuador, AGROCALIDAD).
  6. Reservation at an ACF facility — the step most often forgotten and the one that stops the most trips. With at least 30 days' notice: you gather your CDC documents, send them to the ACF at the arrival airport, the ACF reviews them and issues your reservation; that confirmation travels with the dog and is shown to the airline before boarding and to CBP on arrival. It includes a mandatory exam + revaccination; and the 28 days of quarantine if there is no valid titration. Without a reservation, the dog does not board.
  7. Entry by air only to one of the 6 airports with an ACF (see below).

Dogs vaccinated in the United States that traveled to a high-risk country have a different, simpler route (they do not require an ACF if they meet the conditions). This section covers the most common case: a dog with a foreign vaccine.

The 6 authorized airports (ACF facility)

A dog from a high-risk country can only enter through these 6 airports, the only ones with an animal care facility registered by the CDC:

  • Atlanta (ATL) · Los Angeles (LAX) · Miami (MIA) · New York (JFK) — it must be JFK, not Newark or LaGuardia · Philadelphia (PHL) · Washington Dulles (IAD).

CDC-approved laboratories for the titration

The blood sample must be processed at a laboratory on the current official CDC list, which changes (it was updated in January 2026): confirm it before sending. In Peru there is no CDC-approved laboratory, so the sample leaves the country. Approved in the US: Kansas State University (KSVDL), Auburn University and University of Missouri; there are others in several countries. Two rules that matter: the result must arrive directly from the approved laboratory (not from a third party that forwards it), and it is advisable to take the sample well in advance, because processing takes weeks. Always check the official CDC list.

If your country is DIRECT ENTRY (low rabies risk)

If your country is not on the high-risk list —Argentina, Chile, Uruguay, Paraguay, Mexico, Canada, all of Western Europe, Japan, Korea, Australia, etc.—, the path is much shorter, but it is not "nothing". Your dog needs:

  • ISO microchip, implanted before any rabies vaccine.
  • Current rabies vaccine (strongly recommended; the CDC advises it even though it does not require it by law for low risk).
  • To be at least 6 months old and arrive healthy.
  • CDC Dog Import Form (free, mandatory for all dogs).

No serological titration, no quarantine and through any airport. That is the whole difference from the full protocol.

Screwworm (USDA-APHIS): a second requirement almost no one coordinates

Note — Screwworm (USDA-APHIS): in addition to rabies, dogs coming from countries affected by the New World screwworm —endemic in South America and, since November 22, 2024, also Mexico— require a certificate from an official veterinarian in the country of origin confirming that the dog was inspected and is free of the pest within the 5 days before shipment. Cats do not require this certificate, but they may be inspected on arrival and rejected if they show signs. Check the official list of affected countries from APHIS. Verified at USDA-APHIS on Jun 24, 2026.

This matters especially from South America: a Peru→US dog needs both things at once —the rabies protocol (CDC) and the screwworm certificate (APHIS)—, two separate requirements from two different agencies. It is exactly the kind of thing that an owner on their own does not coordinate and that a veterinary team does solve.

What about cats?

For cats the process is much simpler: the CDC does not require proof of rabies vaccination or the dog form. The cat just needs to arrive healthy and may be inspected on entry (and rejected if there is evidence of disease). Two caveats: Hawaii and Guam impose a local quarantine on all cats, and if the cat comes from a screwworm-affected area it may be inspected on arrival. The airline may ask for a microchip and other requirements of its own.

Frequently asked questions

Does my country enter easily or under the full protocol to the US?
It depends on whether your country is on the CDC's high-risk list for canine rabies. If it is (Peru, Brazil, Colombia, India, the Philippines, China and most of Africa and Asia), the full protocol applies. If it is not (Argentina, Chile, Uruguay, Paraguay, Mexico, Canada, Western Europe, Japan, Korea, Australia), it is direct entry. Look it up in the table above.
Which airport must I enter through with a high-risk dog?
Only through the 6 airports with an ACF facility: ATL, LAX, MIA, JFK (not Newark or LaGuardia), PHL and IAD. The dog must arrive by air at the airport where it has its reservation.
What happens if I do not have a valid titration?
The dog can enter, but it completes 28 days of quarantine at an ACF facility, with a prior reservation and costs borne by the owner. With a valid titration the quarantine is avoided.
Which laboratory do I send the sample to if there is none approved in my country?
To a CDC-approved laboratory outside the country. In Peru there is none; approved in the US: Kansas State (KSVDL), Auburn and Missouri.
Do these rules apply to cats?
No. The rabies protocol and the CDC form are only for dogs. Cats only need to arrive healthy; Hawaii and Guam impose a local quarantine.
Do I need the screwworm certificate?
Yes, if your country is affected (South America and, since Nov 2024, Mexico). It is for dogs, signed by an official veterinarian, inspection within 5 days before travel. Cats do not require it.
How much does the CDC Dog Import Form cost?
It is free. It is completed online and a receipt is sent by email. Mandatory for all dogs, one per dog. For high-risk countries the receipt is tied to the arrival date and airport; for low-risk countries it is valid for multiple entries from the same country for six months.
