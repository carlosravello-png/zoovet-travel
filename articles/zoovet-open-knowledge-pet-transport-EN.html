Why Countries Detain Your Pet at the Airport

Zoovet Travel has deposited its technical knowledge in verifiable open-access repositories. This article explains why that matters — for veterinarians, logistics agents, and families worldwide.

From the Veterinary Perspective Dr. Jessica Ysabel Camacho García · Veterinarian and Animal Scientist · CMVP 12434 · ORCID 0009-0002-6837-5311

Rabies commands the most attention because it is the most visible — it has a name, a protocol, and the weight of a century of international public health. But rabies is only the threshold. What is truly being managed at every veterinary border is something broader: which organisms travel inside an animal that, clinically, appears perfectly healthy.

Leishmania infantum is an infection I know well from clinical practice. It is a zoonotic disease with a chronic course, potentially fatal in dogs, transmitted by phlebotomine sandflies. Technically, in a country without competent vectors, it should not become established. But transplacental and venereal transmission is documented — meaning that a positive animal can introduce the parasite into a population where the vector does not yet exist, and where no local veterinarian has it in their standard differential diagnosis. Long incubation periods ensure the animal arrives asymptomatic. The owner does not know. The customs officer has no way of knowing.

Dirofilaria immitis, Brucella canis, and a growing list of exotic ticks with their respective pathogens complete the picture. A study published in Animals (2021) documented the risk that ticks imported on companion animals may colonise native tick populations — not the animal, the tick — establishing transmission cycles that previously did not exist in that territory. When that occurs, the problem is no longer about the animal that crossed the border. It belongs to the local fauna.

That is the framework from which I assess a patient before an international trip. It is not bureaucracy. It is the correct question: what does this animal carry that the destination ecosystem does not know? The WOAH establishes official disease-free statuses precisely because countries need an international reference to determine their risk level with respect to each country of origin. When that status is not backed by verifiable documentation at the source, the containment system begins operating on assumptions — and assumptions, in veterinary epidemiology, have known consequences.

The customs officer who detains an animal is not overreacting. They are executing the only control available to them: the documents. Because when documents fail, what fails is not the procedure. It is the only record linking that animal to its clinical history, its origin, its exposure vector, and its serological status. And without that record, no one — not the destination veterinarian, not the sanitary authority, not the owner — can evaluate the real risk.

From the Logistics Perspective Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo · Regulatory Affairs & Knowledge Systems · ORCID 0009-0007-5631-7436 · carlosravello.com

A pet transport agent does not diagnose. They do not touch the animal. They work with documents — and those documents are the only barrier between a healthy dog and a thirty-day quarantine, or between a confident passenger and a post-exposure prophylaxis protocol.

The numbers are concrete. The CDC recorded a 52% increase in falsified rabies vaccination certificates in 2020, and an additional 21% in the first months of 2021. Of the approximately 200 annual border rejections in the United States, between 60 and 70 percent have a documentary origin: incomplete paperwork, dates that do not add up, issuers without official authorisation. Not sick animals detected on examination. Documents that do not withstand a data cross-check.

Documented case — CDC 2015: A dog imported from Egypt with a falsified certificate developed rabies four days after arrival. Eighteen people received post-exposure prophylaxis. Seven dogs served six months in quarantine. The CDC estimates the average cost of disease control from a single imported dog with rabies at USD 270,000 — not counting the damage to the importer's record or the restrictions that follow.

In 2019, another case from the same origin: 26 dogs, one with confirmed rabies three weeks after arrival, 44 people in PEP, 25 animals in four-to-six-month quarantine. In Europe, nine cases of rabies detected in France since 2001 with origins in illegal importation or deficient documentation. Between 2012 and 2024, ten dogs and one cat arrived in European Union territory incubating the virus. Average frequency: one animal with imported rabies in the EU every year.

Australia charges between AUD 2,000 and more for the mandatory ten-day quarantine at the Mickleham Post Entry Quarantine Facility. If documentation arrives with errors — an identity check improperly completed before the RNATT test, or a form outside the regulatory window — the period extends to thirty days. There is no expedited appeal. The animal waits. The owner pays.

Why the Zoovet Travel Technical Series exists: There is no centralised, structured, and verifiable reference that allows a customs officer, a pet transport broker, or an airport veterinarian in transit to compare in real time what the destination country requires, what each term in the certificate means, and whether that term has a recognised equivalent in the country of origin. That absence has documented consequences. The series exists to close that gap — from the real operations of a specialised clinic, for the world.

What no international authority has resolved is simple: terminology is not standardised. Certificate formats are not either. Requirements are updated without a uniform publication protocol. And that is precisely why the Zoovet Travel Technical Series exists — osf.io/8dq2p — an editorial series hosted on OSF, open access, built from the real operations of a clinic specialised in international pet export, to serve as a technical reference for veterinarians, logistics agents, researchers, and owners. Because the information that determines whether an animal crosses a border or not should be findable, readable, and verifiable before the airport.
