Scoping review: attachment bond, separation distress and management

Dog–owner separation in cargo-hold air transport: the emotional impact on both, and evidence-based management

Yes — and it is worth saying before anything else: the cargo hold is pressurised and climate-controlled, and it is safe for the overwhelming majority of healthy dogs. In 2025 the US DOT recorded 99.99% of animals arriving with no incident out of 117,861 transported. This technical note starts from that figure in order to sort out something almost nobody puts together: the risk owners fear and the risk the numbers show are not the same risk. The real stressor is not the pressurised compartment but the dog's separation from his attachment figure — and how that separation is handled. What travels in the cargo hold is not a dog inside a crate: it is one half of a bond.

Jessica Ysabel Camacho García, DVM — CMVP 12434  |  Carlos E. Ravello Joo, Psychology student — Zoovet Travel, Peru  |  July 2026  |  DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.21424713
Scope and limitations statement — required reading This article is a descriptive, educational review. Its purpose is to explain the general emotional and physiological mechanisms of separation during air transport. It does not establish probabilities of harm for any individual animal, does not predict individual outcomes and does not replace the assessment of a veterinarian with direct knowledge of the animal, its history and its current condition. Any decision about fitness to fly or about managing an animal's anxiety rests exclusively with the attending veterinarian.

Scientific evidence with physiological measurements taken inside a cargo hold during a real commercial flight is scarce (detailed in Section 8). Wherever a statement comes from road-transport studies, from different clinical settings or from human research, this is stated explicitly and presented as an extrapolation, not as established fact in the air-transport context. No specific pharmacological recommendations are made.
What you will find here Is it safe for your dog to travel in the cargo hold? The official data say yes: 99.99% of animals arrive without a single incident. But «safe» is not the same as «calm» — and that is where almost everyone ends up solving the wrong problem.

This article explains, with veterinary evidence verified one source at a time, what really happens to your dog when he is taken from you to fly, why your own nerves travel with him — it has been measured: human fear speeds up a dog's heart — and what actually works to get him there in good shape. Including why sedating him, the most common idea of all, is also the worst one.

«Nothing will happen to him down there, will it?»
He travels safely, and that is not an opinion. US law requires airlines to report every animal death or injury to the DOT: in 2025, out of 117,861 animals transported in the United States, there were 3 deaths — 99.99% arrived with no incident at all. The hold is pressurised and climate-controlled; it is not the airless chamber many people picture. That said, «arriving safely» is not «feeling nothing»: what does happen, and has been measured, is a surge of stress — cortisol rises and the heart speeds up, above all during loading and unloading (Bergeron et al., 2002). That is the mismatch running through this whole note: the danger the owner fears (the hold) is not the one that really weighs (the separation). And how much it weighs depends on your dog — his heart, his age, his temperament — which your vet decides by looking at him, not an article looking at all of them.

Section 1 The dog–owner bond is an attachment system, not a feeling

Before talking about the trip, it is worth talking about what the trip breaks. When your dog looks up to find you, he is not looking for whoever fills his bowl. For a quarter of a century ethology has been showing something more uncomfortable and more beautiful: what ties him to you is attachment, built from the same parts as a small child's bond with his mother.

Topál and his team (1998) were the first to put dogs through the Strange Situation Test, the procedure Ainsworth had used to study attachment in infants. The result was unmistakable: after each separation the dog runs to find his person before anything else, and he only explores and plays calmly when she is nearby. Prato-Previde (2003) confirmed it as a full-blown attachment bond. And Palmer and Custance (2008), refining the test so that the order of the episodes could not distort it, put their finger on the key piece: the secure base effect. The owner is the solid point from which the dog dares to take on the world, and the one he returns to when the world frightens him.

If you are his secure base, putting him alone into a crate that travels in the belly of an aircraft is not a piece of logistics: it is pulling the ground out from under his paws at precisely the moment when everything around him feels new and threatening.

That is what turns «my dog needs me» into something more than an anxious owner's line. It is the starting point for everything that follows.

Section 2 What «separation» means on a cargo-hold trip

Two blows land together in the hold, and it is worth keeping them apart: you take away his secure base and, at the same time, you place him in a hostile environment. Each one weighs differently, and neither tells the whole story on its own.

The first is being left without you at the worst possible moment. The second is the compartment itself: even pressurised and temperature-controlled, there is loud noise, confinement, vibration, air so dry it parches the mucous membranes, and no trace of day or night. On long flights, on top of all that, his internal clock is thrown out. The physics of all this I cover in the hypobaric physiology and jet lag articles of this series; what matters here is how it adds to the emotional load.

And it adds differently depending on the dog. One with a healthy bond and no separation anxiety is mostly fighting the environment. One who already suffers every time you leave him at home is fighting, on top of that, a ghost he brought with him. They board the same aircraft, but they do not live the same flight.

Section 3 What happens to the dog

3.1 Separation distress as a clinical entity

Leaving a dog without his attachment figure can set off a picture that behavioural medicine knows all too well. Ogata (2016), reviewing what is known about separation anxiety, describes a mix of signs running from behaviour to the body: whining, endless pacing, drooling, soiling indoors, panting, and those escape attempts that sometimes end in injuries the dog inflicts on himself. Flannigan and Dodman (2001) identified who is most at risk. And Lenkei (2021) added a nuance that changes how you handle it: not all of them suffer the same way — in some fear dominates, in others the frustration of not being able to follow you.

That this matters is a matter of numbers: between 14% and more than half of all dogs show signs of separation-related problems. Translated to the airport counter, it means a good share of those who fly are already leaving home with that crack open.

3.2 The physiological correlates: the only evidence from a real flight

Here is the finding that answers «will something happen to him?» most honestly. Bergeron and his team (2002) did something nobody has repeated since: they measured dogs for real, inside the cargo hold, on a commercial flight. In beagles, salivary cortisol rose clearly, and their hearts sped up above all during loading and unloading — less so in steady flight — while they spent the trip still and switched off, in that immobility that is, at bottom, a way of surrendering to fear. Earlier, Leadon and Mullins (1991) had already seen the same stress response in greyhounds, also on a real flight.

Behind those two studies there is solid ground. Herbel (2020) showed that cortisol rises on every road trip and — note this — without habituation, no matter how often it is repeated. Beerda (1997, 1998) had catalogued how cortisol, heart rate and behaviour shift in the face of a fright. And Jahn (2023), surveying owners who had already flown with their dog, supplies the map of the three moments of the trip that I use in Section 5.

A necessary piece of honesty Almost all the physiology of travel stress comes from the road, not from the air. I bring it in here because the stress mechanism is the same, but the cargo hold adds altitude, noise and separation that those studies did not measure. No figure from a car journey should be read as an exact account of what happens at ten thousand metres.

Section 4 What happens to the owner — and why that matters to the dog

This is the point that almost always gets forgotten, and it is precisely the heart of the matter. The owner handing his dog over at the counter is under strain too — and his strain does not stay with him. It spreads.

Sundman (2019) demonstrated it in a way that is hard to shake off: she measured cortisol in the hair of 58 dog–owner pairs and found their stress levels move together. Stressed owner, stressed dog; calm owner, calm dog. They are not two separate stories: they are hooked to each other, and the hook was stronger still in females. Konok (2015) supplied the other side: the owner's attachment style and personality predict how much the dog suffers when left alone.

Put bluntly: the owner who says goodbye in pieces, crying over the crate, is not calming his dog — he is handing him his own fear. Looking after the owner's nerves is not a kindly gesture; it is part of treating the patient.

And that turns the second question on its head. «How do I keep him calm?» begins, in large part, with «how do I show up myself?».

Section 5 The time window: anticipation, transit and reunion

The trip is not a single moment: it is three, and each asks something different of the pair. Jahn (2023) laid them out like this:

  • Before boarding. Your dog reads you: he notices the suitcase, the broken routine, your rush. And you are stacking up nerves. This is where most can be done, because this is the part you can prepare in advance.
  • In the air. Without you now, and with all the noise of the hold on top. The peaks — heart rate, cortisol — cluster around loading and unloading, not in the calm of the cruise (Bergeron et al., 2002).
  • The reunion. Getting you back, he gets his regulator back. In Jahn's survey (2023), most dogs coped and recovered well. The body usually settles quickly, although we still know little about post-flight recovery.

Seeing the trip in three stages avoids the usual mistake: pouring all the effort into the day of the flight, when what really pays off is done weeks beforehand.

Section 6 Physiological correlates of distress: why the body goes along with the emotion

That fear is «only psychological» is a comfortable myth. In reality it pulls two levers in the body, which is why the owner sees what he sees. The first, the startle lever, releases adrenaline and noradrenaline within seconds: the heart races, blood pressure rises. The second, slower one, releases cortisol, which sustains the alert state and then helps bring it down. Fear and a racing heart are the same thing, seen from inside and from outside.

6.1 Cardiovascular correlate

When that alert state is sustained, it leaves a measurable trace. Wormald (2017) found that anxious dogs have lower heart rate variability — a sign that the nervous system's brake is working less well — and it was not down to cortisol or to how they behaved: it was the heart. Soghyani (2025) saw, on ultrasound, that anxiety also shows up in cardiac function. In a dog with a healthy heart, all of this passes and fades. In a dog with heart disease, the same fright lands on a margin that was already narrow (I come back to this in Section 7).

6.2 Respiratory correlate

Panting is two things at once: a sign of stress and the dog's air conditioning. When he pants without pause, the blood can drift towards alkalosis; Albers (1975) described this, though in dogs that were hot rather than frightened, so the finding has to be carried across to emotional ground with care. What does weigh in the cargo hold is how this crosses with the thin air of altitude and with flat faces — and that I take up in the risk section.

Section 7 When the cargo hold is not advisable: risk stratification

They are dogs whose margin for absorbing separation plus cargo hold is narrower, and in whom, for that reason, the veterinarian's judgement weighs more than any general rule. This is not a list of prohibitions: it is a list of who needs to be looked at more carefully.

Section 8 «How do I keep him calm?» — what actually has backing

The short answer, before the detail
There is no magic pill; there is a process. What works: preparing your dog weeks in advance so that the crate becomes his refuge and not his sentence, bringing your own nerves down, leaning on what has evidence behind it (pheromone, and an anxiolytic that does not knock him out, when the vet prescribes it) — and forgetting the old-school sedative, which neither helps nor is harmless.

8.1 Turning the crate into a portable secure base

If what breaks is the secure base, the move is to send a spare one with him: the crate. But it only works if he learned it beforehand as a refuge, not as a cage. That is built over weeks of patience — meals eaten inside, naps, the door left open, treats that appear on their own — not the night before. Mariti (2012) backs this with numbers: dogs used to travelling since puppyhood have far fewer problems than those who start as adults. And a worn T-shirt of yours tucked into the crate does more than it looks: it is your scent telling him you did not abandon him.

8.2 Pharmacological and non-pharmacological support with evidence behind it

Some aids really do have backing. The appeasing pheromone (DAP) — the same signal a mother uses to settle her puppies — improved the different profiles of dogs with travel-related problems in the work of Gandia Estellés and Mills (2006). And Frank (2006), in a solid double-blind trial, showed something that matters: clomipramine brought travel cortisol down without putting the dog to sleep. That is the key — you can take away the anguish without switching the animal off. Which drug, how much and when is for the attending veterinarian to decide, always within what the airline allows.

8.3 Why NOT to sedate (the most common mistake)

«I'll knock him out so he doesn't suffer» is precisely what not to do. On Bergeron's real flight (2002), acepromazine changed nothing: not the body's stress, not the behavioural stress. It did not calm him — it just left him groggy, with the same fear inside. And it charges a high price: a sedative can depress his breathing and blood pressure, take away the panting he uses to regulate his temperature, and rob him of his balance when the crate moves. All of that gets worse with altitude, and in a flat-faced dog it can become critical. This is why veterinary associations and air transport rules advise against routine sedation, and many airlines will not even accept a sedated animal. The specific sedation policy and the official positions currently in force should be confirmed with the attending veterinarian and with the airline before every trip.

8.4 The owner's role

And the hardest part, because it is about you: if your stress and his are hooked together (Sundman et al., 2019), the goodbye matters. Short, calm, no drama. Not because you don't love him, but because you do — a steady «see you soon» weighs less on him than a tearful farewell.

Section 9 State of the evidence and declared gaps

Saying what is not known is also part of rigour. This is what we are still missing:

  • Almost everything comes from the road, not the air. In terms of measurements taken on a real flight, we essentially have Bergeron (2002) and Leadon and Mullins (1991). The rest is inferred by physiological logic, not because anyone measured it inside a cargo hold.
  • Nobody has measured live during the flight. There is no oxygen saturation, no continuous heart rate, no serial cortisol taken from a dog while he crosses the ocean in the hold.
  • On cats, almost nothing. There are practically no studies of cats on real flights; the one serious source (Jahn and DePorter, 2023) admits it. Everything I say here about cats is extrapolation, and I flag it as such.
  • On the cardiac dog in flight, nothing. Nobody has followed dogs with confirmed heart disease through an air journey.

Section 10 Conclusion

Here it is, in one sentence, what this note set out to sort: the risk the owner fears and the risk the data show are not the same. The numbers are stubborn — 99.99% of animals with no incident (US DOT, 2025); the pressurised compartment is not the main problem. The problem is separation from the attachment figure, and how it is handled.

The cargo hold does not move a dog: it cuts a pair in two. From him it takes away his solid ground at the worst possible moment and piles on noise and confinement; in you it leaves an anguish that, far from staying put, travels all the way to him. That is why «will something happen to him?» is answered with data — he travels safely — and «how do I calm him?» is answered with a process, not a pill: making the crate a refuge, leaning on what works, not sedating, and looking after your own nerves too. Bringing transport data, animal welfare, stress physiology and attachment theory together in a single read is precisely what this note contributes. And what makes it the right decision for your dog is not this text: it is your vet, looking at him.
