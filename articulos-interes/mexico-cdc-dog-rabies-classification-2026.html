Is Mexico High-Risk for Dog Rabies? CDC Classification 2026 and USA Entry Requirements

The direct answer: Mexico is not on the CDC high-risk countries list for dog rabies in 2026. This means no FAVN titer test, no quarantine, and entry through any US port — including land borders. But there is one requirement most owners miss: the USDA APHIS screwworm inspection.

Section 1How the CDC classifies countries for dog rabies risk

The CDC does not publish a list of "safe" or "approved" countries for pet travel. It operates on a simpler binary: there is a list of high-risk countries for dog-mediated rabies, and everything not on that list is implicitly low-risk or dog-rabies-free for import purposes. The CDC states this explicitly on its importation page: "If a country or political unit is not listed below, it is not considered high risk for importing dog rabies into the United States."

A country earns a high-risk designation when there is documented circulation of dog-mediated rabies virus variants — that is, rabies transmitted through dogs within a domestic dog population, not just wildlife spillover. The distinction matters because the CDC's concern is specifically about dog-to-dog transmission chains being imported into the United States, where dog-mediated rabies was eliminated decades ago.

The high-risk list currently includes countries across Africa, Asia, the Middle East, and parts of Latin America. The last published update to this list was May 31, 2024. Countries that appear on it face significantly stricter import requirements: a valid rabies vaccine administered at an approved laboratory, the FAVN titer test (a blood test confirming immune response), a waiting period after the titer, and arrival restricted to designated airports only. These are the requirements that generate months of preparation time and the specialist consultations you may have already encountered.

The countries that do not appear on the list — including Mexico — bypass all of that. The documentation requirement is minimal by comparison, and the timeline collapses from months to days.

Section 2Is Mexico high-risk or low-risk for dog rabies according to the CDC in 2026?

No — Mexico is not high-risk. Mexico is not on the CDC high-risk countries list for dog rabies as of 2026. Under the CDC's binary classification system, Mexico is a low-risk country for dog rabies: no FAVN titer test (RNATT) required, no mandatory quarantine, and no airport restriction. Dogs may enter the United States from Mexico through any port of entry, including all land border crossings. The CDC high-risk countries list was last published on May 31, 2024; Mexico's low-risk status is confirmed unchanged through March 2026.

What does this classification mean in practice? It means that a dog entering the United States from Mexico is not required to undergo rabies titer testing (FAVN/RNATT), is not subject to mandatory quarantine, and can enter through any US port of entry including all land border crossings — a significant operational advantage for people making frequent cross-border trips or relocating by road.

It also means that the documentation package is straightforward: an ISO-compatible microchip, a completed CDC Dog Import Form receipt, and one additional certificate that most sources overlook entirely — the USDA APHIS screwworm inspection. That last requirement is where well-prepared owners still get stopped.

One important caveat applies regardless of Mexico's classification: if the dog has visited any CDC high-risk country within the 6 months prior to US entry, the high-risk requirements apply to that animal even if it is departing from Mexico. The classification follows the animal's recent travel history, not only the country it is physically leaving from. This is particularly relevant for dogs that have recently traveled internationally through countries in Latin America that do appear on the high-risk list.

Section 3Complete entry requirements: dogs from Mexico to the USA in 2026

The following requirements apply to a dog that has been in Mexico and/or other low-risk countries only during the 6 months prior to US entry. All requirements are federal (CDC and USDA APHIS); individual US states may have additional requirements that must be verified separately.

Section 4The screwworm certificate: the detail that stops owners at the border

Of all the requirements in the table above, the USDA APHIS screwworm inspection is the one most frequently missed — and the one with the hardest deadline. The 5-day window before travel is a strict regulatory requirement, not a guideline. A certificate issued 6 days before departure is non-compliant. A certificate issued on the correct day but by a veterinarian who is not official or USDA-accredited is also non-compliant.

The screwworm requirement exists because Cochliomyia hominivorax, the New World screwworm fly, is a regulated pest that the United States has eradicated from its territory. The USDA maintains a buffer zone program in Central America and Mexico to prevent re-introduction. Dogs traveling from Mexico — and several other Latin American countries — must be physically inspected for screwworm infestation before crossing. This is separate from any CDC documentation and is enforced by USDA APHIS at the border.

⚠ Time-critical requirement
The screwworm inspection must be performed by an official or USDA-accredited veterinarian and documented within 5 calendar days before the date of entry into the United States. If your travel dates change, the certificate may need to be reissued. Plan the inspection appointment as close to travel as operationally possible, but allow enough buffer for unexpected schedule changes.

For dogs traveling frequently between Mexico and the United States, the CDC Dog Import Form receipt resolves the CDC documentation requirement for up to 6 months of repeated entries. The screwworm certificate, however, must be renewed each time the dog crosses the border, because each inspection is specific to a point in time. This is the operationally demanding part of routine Mexico–US pet travel at scale.

The USDA APHIS official source for screwworm requirements, including the list of affected countries and inspection certificate requirements, is: aphis.usda.gov/pet-travel/another-country-to-us-import/dogs. Always verify this page directly before any trip, as the list of affected countries and procedural requirements can be updated independently of the CDC rules.

Section 5What to confirm before traveling with a dog from Mexico to the USA

The classification of Mexico as low-risk simplifies the process considerably relative to high-risk origins. But simplification is not the same as elimination of requirements. There are five things that must be confirmed before any trip:

1. Travel history of the dog in the past 6 months. If the dog has been in any CDC high-risk country during this period, the low-risk route does not apply. The full high-risk documentation package — including FAVN titer test, waiting period, and designated port entry — becomes required regardless of the current departure country being Mexico.

2. Microchip readability and ISO compatibility. The microchip must be readable with a standard ISO scanner at the border. Microchip numbers on health documents must match exactly. A transposed digit in any certificate creates a documentation chain break that can result in detention at the port of entry.

3. CDC Dog Import Form status. If a valid receipt is already held from a previous trip, verify that the 6-month window and departure country have not changed. If the form needs to be completed, do so at cdc.gov/importation well before travel.

4. Screwworm inspection appointment. Identify an official or USDA-accredited veterinarian in your area of Mexico who can issue the screwworm certificate. Book the appointment within the 5-day window before your travel date. Confirm that the certificate format meets APHIS requirements.

5. Airline policies and state requirements. Federal CDC clearance does not guarantee airline acceptance or compliance with destination state law. Verify breed restrictions, cabin vs. cargo policies for your specific route, and destination state requirements for rabies vaccination and licensing before purchasing tickets.

The most common reason a well-documented trip fails at the US border is not missing a major requirement — it is a detail problem: an ISO microchip number mistyped in one document, a screwworm certificate dated one day outside the window, or a CDC form that was completed with the wrong departure country. The documentation chain is only as strong as its least accurate entry.

Frequently asked questions

The most common questions about Mexico's CDC classification and dog entry requirements for the USA.

Is Mexico high-risk for dog rabies according to the CDC in 2026?

No. Mexico is not on the CDC high-risk countries list for dog rabies in 2026. Under the CDC's binary classification system, any country not explicitly listed as high-risk is considered low-risk or dog-rabies-free for US import purposes. Mexico's low-risk status has remained unchanged through March 2026. Source: cdc.gov/importation/dogs/high-risk-countries.html

What documents are required to bring a dog from Mexico to the USA in 2026?

Since Mexico is low-risk, the core federal requirements are: (1) dog at least 6 months old, (2) ISO-compatible microchip, (3) appear healthy upon arrival, (4) CDC Dog Import Form receipt obtained before travel. Additionally, USDA APHIS requires a screwworm inspection certificate from an official veterinarian within 5 days before travel. No FAVN titer test and no quarantine required.

Is rabies vaccination mandatory to enter the USA from Mexico with a dog?

Rabies vaccination is strongly recommended by the CDC but is not federally mandatory for entry from low-risk countries like Mexico under current 2026 rules. However, most US states require rabies vaccination for resident dogs, and it becomes federally required if the dog has visited any high-risk country in the previous 6 months. Always confirm destination state law.

What is the screwworm inspection and why is it required from Mexico?

The New World screwworm (Cochliomyia hominivorax) is an eradicated pest in the United States. Because Mexico is an affected country, USDA APHIS requires that dogs be inspected by an official or accredited veterinarian and certified free of screwworm within 5 days before US entry. This is a USDA requirement separate from CDC documentation and must be renewed each crossing. Source: aphis.usda.gov

Can I enter the USA with my dog from Mexico through a land border crossing?

Yes. Because Mexico is classified as low-risk, there are no port-of-entry restrictions. Dogs from Mexico may enter through any US port including all land border crossings. This is different from high-risk country entries, which are restricted to specific designated airports.

What if my dog traveled to a high-risk country in the last 6 months but is now in Mexico?

The CDC high-risk requirements follow the dog's recent travel history, not just its current departure country. If the dog has been in any CDC high-risk country within the 6 months before US entry, the full high-risk documentation package applies — including FAVN titer test, waiting period, and restricted port of entry — regardless of departing from Mexico. Consult a specialist before booking.

Is Mexico a low-risk country for dog rabies according to the CDC?

Yes. Mexico is classified as a low-risk country for dog rabies by the CDC. It does not appear on the official CDC high-risk countries list (last updated May 31, 2024; confirmed unchanged through March 2026). Under the CDC binary system, all countries not on the high-risk list are treated as low-risk or dog-rabies-free for US import purposes. This means no RNATT, no quarantine, and no airport restriction.

Is Mexico on the CDC list of high-risk countries for dog rabies?

Are health requirements the same for all destination countries?

No. Each country applies its own requirements: vaccinations, laboratory tests, official certifications and waiting periods that in many cases cannot be shortened. What is valid for the USA may be insufficient or incompatible with another destination. Always evaluate your specific case before starting any export process.

Do all airlines accept pets on international flights?

No. Policies vary by airline, route, aircraft type and season, and change frequently. Brachycephalic breeds face additional restrictions on most international carriers. Before purchasing your ticket, verify compatibility for your exact route and pet.
