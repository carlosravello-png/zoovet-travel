Dogs and Cats in the Airplane Cargo Hold: What IATA, the AVMA, and the Official Data Say

This isn't what we think. It's what U.S. federal regulation says, what IATA says, what the American Veterinary Medical Association says, and what the government's own data show. We gathered it, verified it, and translated it. Here it is, with every source linked.

By Carlos Ravello · Veterinary review: Dr. Jessica Camacho García (DVM · CMVP 12434) · Sources verified on 16 July 2026

Direct answer: your pet doesn't travel with the suitcases. It travels in a compartment reserved for live animals, pressurized to the same level as the cabin (required by U.S. regulation 9 CFR §3.16) and climate-controlled between 7.2 °C and 29.5 °C. IATA itself writes that dogs and cats "actually travel better this way, because it's quieter and they get to rest in a dark environment." And the numbers from the US DOT back it up: in 2025, of 117,861 animals transported in the U.S., 99.997% arrived without a single death, and 99.99% with no incident at all.At Zoovet Travel we check every official source, one by one, so you can decide with data — not fear.

Where the fear comes from

Almost everyone who writes to us starts with the same sentence: "I heard it's rough down there." We understand. It's hard to put a member of your family somewhere you can't see. But the image almost everyone brings to mind —the dog squeezed between suitcases, in a dark box, shaking— doesn't describe how animals actually travel. It describes how we imagine it.

We're not going to ask you to just take our word for it. We're going to show you what the sources that actually govern this say: United States aviation regulation, the International Air Transport Association (IATA), the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA), and the reports of the U.S. Department of Transportation (US DOT). Every figure on this page has its source linked. If something has no source, we don't write it.

What the airplane's "cargo hold" actually is

First, a distinction that changes everything: there isn't just one cargo hold. An airplane has several cargo compartments, and live animals don't travel in the general-cargo one. They travel in a designated space that the law requires to meet three conditions.

Pressure

United States federal regulation 9 CFR §3.16, which sets the conditions for transporting dogs and cats, requires that the animal cargo space be pressurized in flight, except when the aircraft is flying below 8,000 feet. In practical terms: your pet breathes the same air pressure you do in your seat. The physiology of that pressurization and of oxygen in flight we analyze in depth, with a DOI, in our technical study on hypobaric physiology in air transport. How much pressure, exactly? Aircraft design standards (FAA 14 CFR §25.841 and EASA CS-25) set the cabin pressure altitude —and that of the live-animal compartment, which shares the same pressurization system— to no more than 2,438 meters (8,000 feet), and in real operation it usually sits between 1,500 and 2,438 m. It is a mild hypoxia that a healthy animal compensates without difficulty. The pressurized air is very dry, though (10–20% relative humidity): that is why access to water and prior hydration matter more than they seem.

Temperature

The same regulation prohibits exposing dogs and cats to temperatures above 29.5 °C (85 °F) or below 7.2 °C (45 °F) for more than four hours, and to no more than 45 minutes during transfers to and from the aircraft. That's why, when the forecast at any point along your route falls outside that range, airlines won't fly animals: they apply seasonal embargoes and would rather cancel than take the risk.

Securing and separation from luggage

The carrier isn't stacked with anything. It's secured with nets and straps so it can't move even in turbulence, in a section reserved solely for live animals, separate from general cargo. It's checked in separately, handled by staff trained in live-animal handling, loaded last and unloaded first. The crew knows before takeoff that there are animals on board, so they can manage the airflow to that area throughout the flight.

And regarding the noise and darkness that frighten people so much, it's worth reading what IATA itself —the world's top authority on air transport, not an airline trying to sell a seat— writes in its official guide for pet owners:

"Some airlines will transport them as excess baggage in a heated, ventilated cargo hold. Don't worry — dogs and cats actually travel better this way, because it's quieter and they get to rest in a dark environment." — IATA, Traveler's Pet Corner, Container Requirement CR1, Live Animals Regulations 2026 edition (IATA's own guidance; read the original at the link above). Verified on 16 July 2026.

The numbers, and where they come from

Fear gets answered with data, and here the data comes from government, not from an industry association. The U.S. Department of Transportation (US DOT) legally requires airlines to report every death, injury, or loss of an animal, and publishes the annual totals through the Bureau of Transportation Statistics (BTS).

Official data · US DOT · calendar year 2025

In 2025, U.S. airlines transported 117,861 animals. There were 3 deaths, 5 injuries, and 1 loss recorded: the DOT's own official rate was 0.76 incidents per 10,000 animals. In other words, 99.997% arrived without a single death, and 99.99% with no incident at all. That's fewer deaths than in 2024 (10 out of 161,335): the numbers keep improving year over year.

Source: US DOT, Air Travel Consumer Report, annual animal table for calendar year 2025 (February 2026 report, p. 56, mandatory report under 14 CFR Part 235). Verified on 16 July 2026.

And there's a comparison that helps put it in perspective. Olympic competition horses —half-ton animals worth millions— fly halfway around the world in that same type of climate-controlled compartment, under the same IATA Live Animals Regulations. For the Tokyo 2020 Games, 247 horses traveled by air, many of them via Liège on an Emirates Boeing 777 freighter. A groom who accompanied the Belgian horses described it to The Horse magazine: "for most of them, flying is very calm… they get far less agitated than in a truck. Mine slept through most of the trip." If the system is safe for a show-jumping horse, it's safe for a dog in its crate.

A note on the sources: the incident figures come from the U.S. because it is one of the few countries that legally requires every incident to be published —there is no equivalent global registry—, so it is the most complete public snapshot available. And the welfare standard airlines apply worldwide, the IATA Live Animals Regulations, is the same in Lima, Madrid or Frankfurt: global by design, not American.

The real risks, and who studied them

Clinical section signed by Dr. Jessica Y. Camacho García (Veterinarian · CMVP 12434 · ORCID 0009-0002-6837-5311) and Dr. Víctor J. Camacho Paz (Veterinarian · CMVP 3103 · ORCID 0009-0005-8096-9317).

There is risk. It would be dishonest to say otherwise, and it's also measured. There are three of them, they're narrow, and they're documented by veterinary authorities, not by us.

1. Flat-faced (brachycephalic) breeds

Bulldogs, pugs, Boston terriers, boxers, Persian cats. Their anatomy breathes worse under stress and heat, and that happens on the ground too, not just in the air. The AVMA (American Veterinary Medical Association) puts a number on it: "over the past five years, roughly half of the 122 flight-associated dog deaths involved these short-faced breeds." The US DOT itself confirmed this back in 2010, and a peer-reviewed study published through the NIH (Jahn et al., 2023) examines how well dogs tolerate flying. That's why many airlines won't accept brachycephalic breeds during hot months. In our own clinical research we documented exactly why physics works against them: we explain it in the French bulldog entry and we develop it, with a DOI, in our study on the air transport of brachycephalic dogs (BOAS syndrome, cabin hypoxia and overheating). There we quantify it: BOAS is a continuum of severity, not a yes-or-no, and a brachycephalic dog's airway resistance is significantly higher than a normal-nosed dog's even at rest (Liu et al., 2016, by whole-body barometric plethysmography). That is why the right question is not whether your dog can fly, but with what BOAS severity, on which airline and after what pre-flight veterinary assessment —and why the global trend is to ban them from the hold and allow them only in the cabin under weight limits.

2. Time on the tarmac

The only moment your pet is exposed to outside air is during the minutes of loading and unloading on the airport tarmac. That's the real window of risk —not the flight itself— and it's exactly the one the regulation limits: 9 CFR caps exposure during transfers to and from the aircraft at 45 minutes. That's why a good agent calculates the flight time and the day's temperature, not just the date.

3. Sedation

It's the most dangerous mistake, and it's usually made with good intentions. Don't sedate your pet, and we're not the ones saying it. Three veterinary authorities say it at once. The AVMA: "consult your veterinarian before giving any tranquilizer or sedative; they can increase the risk of heart or respiratory problems." The Australian Veterinary Association (AVA): "sedation is not recommended." And IATA, which explains the physiological reason why: tranquilizers lower blood pressure, an effect that also occurs naturally at altitude, and they take away the animal's ability to maintain a safe posture. A sedated dog on a flight is in more danger, not less.

What actually decides the outcome

The scare almost never comes from the cargo hold. It comes from a carrier that's the wrong size, an animal that wasn't acclimated, or a sedative nobody should have given. Why the stress of travel is settled in the body —and not in the hold— we review, with a DOI, in our study of the gut–brain axis during transport. All three of those are controlled by whoever prepares the trip, not by the airplane.

Which airlines hold the IATA CEIV Live Animals certification

Complying with the IATA Live Animals Regulations (LAR) is the baseline: nearly every major airline does. Above that there's an audited, exclusive tier, CEIV Live Animals (Center of Excellence for Independent Validators), which IATA launched in 2018 and which verifies on-site that a company meets the highest standard of animal welfare.

Why we made this list

Nobody paid us for this. No airline sponsors this list. We put it together because, in more than thirteen years sending dogs and cats around the world, this is the question we've seen cause a family the most anguish: "what if something happens to them down there?" It's also one of the hardest to answer: there's very little clear, organized public information about which airlines actually meet the highest standard.

So we sat down and organized it ourselves. We went airline by airline, checking each one against its official announcement and the IATA One Source registry, and noted what we found, with the date. When a company doesn't have it, we say so anyway —even if it's one we like. We'd rather be useful than look good.

Before you board, demand this

You don't need to trust blindly. These are the exact questions an informed owner asks before handing over their pet. Copy them and use them with your agent or the airline.

  • Does the airline comply with the IATA Live Animals Regulations? (The baseline. Every IATA member airline should.)
  • Does it also hold the IATA CEIV Live Animals certification? (The audited gold standard. Check the table above.)
  • What's the maximum weight allowed in the hold, and which breeds are restricted on my exact route?
  • Is there a seasonal temperature embargo on my travel dates?
  • Do they confirm my pet will travel in the pressurized, climate-controlled compartment, not in general cargo?

If whoever's helping you can't answer these five, you already know who not to fly with. A serious professional answers them without hesitation.

Frequently asked questions

Is the airplane cargo hold pressurized for pets?

Yes. U.S. regulation 9 CFR §3.16 requires the animal cargo space to be pressurized in flight, except below 8,000 feet. Your pet breathes the same pressure as the passenger cabin.

Do dogs and cats get too cold or too hot in the cargo hold?

No, not within regulation. 9 CFR prohibits exposing them to more than 29.5 °C or less than 7.2 °C for more than 4 hours. When the forecast falls outside that range at any point along the route, airlines apply embargoes and won't fly animals.

Is it dangerous for my dog to travel in the cargo hold?

Official US DOT data say no: in 2025, of 117,861 animals transported in the U.S. there were 3 deaths; 99.997% arrived without a single death and 99.99% with no incident at all. According to the AVMA, roughly half of the recorded deaths involve flat-faced breeds, not the conditions of the hold.

Does my pet travel together with the luggage?

No. It travels in a section reserved for live animals, with the carrier strapped down, separate from the luggage. It's loaded separately, unloaded first, and the crew knows it's on board.

Should I sedate my pet for the flight?

No. The AVMA, the Australian Veterinary Association and IATA advise against it: sedatives lower blood pressure —an effect compounded by altitude— and take away the animal's ability to self-regulate and maintain a safe posture.
