What it is and what it's for

Deworming means eliminating and preventing the parasites that affect your pet: the internal ones (intestinal) and the external ones (fleas and ticks). It's not a generic pill for everyone: the plan depends on age, weight, lifestyle and the trip's destination.

What we do

We assess which parasites need to be covered, apply the appropriate internal and external antiparasitic treatment, and document it, because several destinations require proof. We follow international parasite-control standards.1 The technical basis is in our scientific series on antiparasitic interventions.

What we cover and why it matters (in plain terms)

  • Internal parasites — intestinal worms (nematodes and cestodes). Some are zoonotic, such as Toxocara (which can affect people) and Echinococcus.
  • External parasites — fleas and ticks, which also transmit other diseases to your pet.
  • Anti-echinococcus treatment (Echinococcus multilocularis) — several destinations require this treatment documented within a strict time window before entry. Outside that window, it doesn't count.2

Many parasites spread to people. Deworming properly —and on time— protects your pet, your family and your trip's compliance.

When to do it

It depends on the plan and the destination. Watch out for the anti-echinococcus treatment: it has a precise time window before entry into the country, neither before nor after. Write to us with the destination and the date and we'll fit it into the calendar.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I deworm my pet?

It depends on their age, lifestyle and exposure. Puppies and kittens need more frequent deworming, and pets that go out to the countryside or live with children need a specific plan. We follow ESCCAP's international standards to define it case by case. The technical basis is in our technical series on antiparasitic interventions, published in three languages.

What is the anti-echinococcus treatment and why is it required for travel?

It is a specific antiparasitic treatment against echinococcus (Echinococcus multilocularis), a zoonotic worm. Several destinations require it documented within a strict time window before entry; if it is administered outside that window, it is not valid for the trip.
