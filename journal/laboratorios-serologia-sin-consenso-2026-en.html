Standing rule · Rabies serology

There is no single list of rabies serology laboratories. Every authority keeps its own, and they do not match

A laboratory approved by the United States may not be designated by Japan, nor by the European Union, nor even by its own country. Nobody warns the owner, and laboratories do not publish which countries their report is good for. Verified on 13 July 2026 against the four official sources.

By Carlos Ravello · Veterinary review: Dr Jessica Camacho García (DVM · CMVP 12434) · Published 13 July 2026 · Sources verified that same day

⚠ Before the blood is drawn, check three things

1. The list of the authority of the destination country — not the one from the country where the laboratory sits, and not your own. It is the only one that decides.

2. The date of that list. All of them carry one. If the list you are looking at is more than a month old, look again.

3. If the trip has several destinations or a layover with a change of regime, the laboratory has to be on the list of all of them, not just one.

The fact, in one sentence: there is no single international list of rabies serology laboratories. Each health authority keeps its own, with its own criteria and its own dates of listing and delisting, with no mechanism to synchronise them. The same laboratory can be approved on one list, expired on another and revoked on a third. All of them are official. All of them are published. And they do not say the same thing.

The assumption almost everyone makes

When a veterinarian sends a blood sample to a rabies serology laboratory, the implicit assumption is that an "accredited" laboratory is accredited for all purposes. It is not.

There is no single international list. Each health authority keeps its own register of designated laboratories, with its own criteria, its own dates of listing and delisting, and no mechanism to synchronise them. A laboratory can be on one country's list and not on another's. It can expire on one and remain current on the other. And nobody is required to tell you.

This is not new, nor is it an irregularity: it is how the system works today. What almost nobody explains is the practical consequence.

What the official lists say, today

On 13 July 2026 we checked the lists published by four authorities: the United States, Japan, the European Union and the United Kingdom.

United States — CDC. Table updated 12 January 2026; the page is dated 16 January 2026. 70 approved laboratories in 42 countries, counted on the table itself.

Japan — Animal Quarantine Service (AQS/MAFF). List updated 24 February 2026 («更新日：2026年2月24日»). Around 35 designated laboratories in 26 countries, two of them with a cancellation date recorded.

European Union — DG SANTE. Register of laboratories designated in non-EU countries, with a date of approval and, where one exists, an expiry date for each.

United Kingdom — DEFRA. List updated 3 February 2026. One single laboratory.

All four are official. All four are published. And they do not say the same thing.

The clearest example: one laboratory, four different statuses

Biobest Laboratories Ltd., in the United Kingdom, is a reference laboratory with more than twenty years in rabies serology. As of today, this is what each authority says about it.

Japan has revoked it. Its entry on the AQS list reads «Cancellation date: Jun. 25, 2025» and adds that the designation was withdrawn «due to violations of the designation standards». The notice asks anyone holding certificates issued between 27 November 2024 and 25 June 2025 to contact the quarantine office at their airport of arrival. MAFF itself states that it is working with the British authorities to confirm the situation.

The United States still accepts it. It appears in Table 1 of the CDC —currently approved laboratories—, under the United Kingdom, alongside the Animal and Plant Health Agency. It is not in Table 2, the one for laboratories that have been removed.

We make no assessment of the laboratory's conduct: we report what each authority publishes on its own page. And what they publish is not compatible.

What we still had to check, and have now checked

When we published the alert on Biobest, on 11 July, we wrote in our own table that the European Union was a figure "not verified by us as of the date of publication". We were not going to print something we had not read.

We have now read it. The European register says, for Biobest Laboratories Ltd.: «Date of approval: 14 November 2001 — Expiry date: 9 February 2026». Its designation in Europe has been expired since February.

And in the United Kingdom, where the laboratory is based, it is not there either

The United Kingdom publishes its own list of laboratories approved for rabies serology. We consulted it on 13 July 2026: it is updated to 3 February 2026 and holds one single laboratory, the Animal and Plant Health Agency. Biobest is not on it.

In the change history of that same page, dated 3 February 2026, it reads: «Removed Biotest from the list of rabies testing laboratories — it is now closed for any further work.» The spelling is the British body's, not ours, and for that reason we do not claim that "Biotest" and "Biobest" are the same laboratory: the page does not say so. What it does say, and anyone can check it, is that today the British list has one name on it and that name is not Biobest. Six days after that removal, Biobest's European designation expired.

Japan revoked it. The European Union let it expire. The United Kingdom does not list it. The United States keeps it among its approved laboratories. Four authorities, four answers, all of them published.

It is not an isolated case

The mismatch runs in both directions and affects several laboratories.

Removed by the CDC on 12 January 2026, but current in the European register: the Pasteur Institute of Brazil, Komipharm International (KRSL) in South Korea, and the Animal Disease Diagnostic Center of the Guangdong Academy of Agricultural Sciences, in China. In the European register all three appear with a date of approval and no expiry date.

Expired in the European Union since 30 April 2024, but still approved by the CDC: the Pasteur Institute of Cambodia.

And the underlying asymmetry, the one that bites hardest in practice: there are 16 countries where the CDC approves laboratories and Japan designates none — Cambodia, Canada, Chile, China, Croatia, the United Arab Emirates, Slovakia, Slovenia, the Philippines, Greece, Israel, Romania, Serbia, Sweden, Thailand and Ukraine. The starkest contrast is China: the CDC approves eight Chinese laboratories; Japan, none.

The disagreement reaches down to the technique. Japan warns on its own page that micro-RFFIT is not an accepted measurement method: «micro-RFFIT (micro rapid fluorescent focus inhibition test) is not approved as a measurement method». Neither the CDC nor the European Commission says anything about that method on their respective lists. They do not only disagree on which laboratory counts: they disagree on which test counts.

Why this is a real problem, not a bureaucratic one

The system assumes that someone —the owner, the vet, the agent— will know how to cross-check four official lists, in four languages, before a blood sample is drawn. In practice that does not happen, and for two very concrete reasons.

The first: laboratories do not publish which countries their report is good for. It is information that does not appear on their website, nor on their request form, nor in the result they issue. A laboratory can issue an impeccable report, technically correct, and that report can be worthless for the destination your dog is going to. The laboratory has done nothing wrong: it simply has no obligation to tell you where its report is valid.

The second: when an authority removes a laboratory, the removal does not travel. There is no notice to the owner, nor to the vet who sent the sample, nor to the other authorities. The withdrawal is published on an official page that almost nobody re-reads. And the result has already been paid for, already been signed, and it looks valid.

The cost of this gap is not borne by the authorities or by the laboratories. It is borne by the owner, at the airport counter, when there is no room left to fix it.

What we do not know

New Zealand: we could not open the official page that sets out its criterion, so we write nothing about it. It remains pending.

The European Commission page does not state an update date. It carries the approval and the expiry for each laboratory, but nowhere does it say when the set as a whole was reviewed. On 13 July 2026, when we consulted it, that was what there was.

What to do, in concrete terms

Before the blood is drawn, and not after, check three things.

First, the list of the authority of the destination country, not the one from the country where the laboratory sits, and not your own. It is the only one that decides. If the destination is Japan, the list that rules is the AQS one. If it is the United States, the CDC's. If it is the European Union, DG SANTE's.

Second, the date of that list. All of them carry an update date. If the one you are looking at is more than a month old, look again.

Third, if the trip has several destinations or a layover with a change of regime, the laboratory has to be on the list of every one of them, not just one.

And one warning that covers all the rest: do not assume that a large, well-known laboratory with decades of history is on every list. The Biobest case proves exactly the opposite.
