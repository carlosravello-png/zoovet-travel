Standing rule · AQS Japan

Japan does not accept certain microchips, nor RNA vaccines. It has not accepted them for years

This is not a new rule. It is a standing rule nobody explains. The Animal Quarantine Service stopped accepting microchips beginning with "900 202" in April 2023, warned in December 2025 about duplicate chips, and rejects RNA rabies vaccines. Your dog can be microchipped, vaccinated and entirely in order — and still not comply.

By Carlos Ravello · Veterinary review: Dr. Jessica Camacho García (DVM · CMVP 12434) · Published 11 July 2026 · Sources verified that same day

⚠ Four Japanese rules your file may be breaching today

1. Microchips whose number begins with "900 202" are not accepted as a means of identification. AQS notice of 7 April 2023.

2. Different chips carrying the same number have been detected. The AQS names the series beginning with 900113, 900115, 900128, 900201, 900215, 900217 and 900263. It may be necessary to re-implant the chip and redo the whole process. Notice of 19 December 2025.

3. RNA rabies vaccines and live-virus rabies vaccines are not accepted.

4. The first rabies vaccination must be given at 91 days of age or later. Not 84. Not "twelve weeks".

Why this is an alert and not a curiosity: none of these four rules is new. All of them are published. And that is precisely what makes them dangerous — a dog can spend years microchipped and vaccinated, with every document in order, and comply with none of them. The file does not fail on the day of the flight. It failed on the day the chip went in.

The number your reader displays

An ISO microchip has fifteen digits. The first three identify the country or the manufacturer. Any reader shows them on screen, alongside the protocol — FDX-B — before anyone signs anything.

Those three digits are the first check on the Japanese file, and the check costs nothing: if the number begins with 900 202, the AQS does not accept it. If it begins with 900113, 900115, 900128, 900201, 900215, 900217 or 900263, there is a risk that the same number has been implanted in another animal.

The AQS is explicit about what happens then: it may be necessary to implant a new microchip and redo the required procedures. On the Japanese calendar, "redo the procedures" means going back to the first vaccination: 91 days of age, second dose 30 days later, a new titration and a 180-day wait. A year of the animal's life, over three digits.

Which is why the agency itself adds a recommendation that sounds bureaucratic and is not: once the import date has been decided, submit the notification as early as possible. The problem surfaces at notification — and the sooner it surfaces, the cheaper it is.

The vaccine your vet may be about to give

The AQS accepts only inactivated (killed-virus) or recombinant rabies vaccines. And it states, in so many words, that RNA rabies vaccines —already in use in some countries— are not accepted for the import of dogs and cats into Japan. Nor are live-virus vaccines.

This is not a laboratory technicality. It is an operational instruction: ask which type of vaccine is going to be given, before it is given. Afterwards there is no fix — only revaccination and another full calendar.

The 91 days

The rule that disqualifies files most quietly is not a rejection: it is a difference of seven days.

Almost everywhere in the world the first rabies vaccination is given at 12 weeks — 84 days. Japan requires 91 days, counting the day of birth as day 0. The AQS says so itself: in some countries the first dose may be given between 84 and 90 days, but under Japanese regulations a vaccine given before 91 days of age is invalid.

A puppy vaccinated at 12 weeks holds a vaccination that is perfectly valid for Europe, for the United States and for almost any destination. For Japan it does not exist. And it cannot be patched: the dog must be revaccinated and the whole calendar restarted.

Hence the one piece of advice that genuinely saves time, and that has to be acted on while the animal is a puppy and nobody is yet thinking about moving abroad: if there is any chance that dog will one day live in Japan, give the first vaccination at 91 days. Waiting a week costs nothing. Not having waited costs six months.

And one rule that works in your favour

Not everything Japan buries in its footnotes is bad news. There is a favourable exception that almost nobody uses, because almost nobody knows about it.

The rabies antibody test is valid for two years from the date of the blood draw. When those two years run out, the 180-day wait does not have to be repeated — a new result is enough, provided three conditions are met: that vaccination coverage has been maintained continuously since the first dose, that the new blood draw is at least 180 days after the first one, and that the new titre is ≥ 0.5 IU/ml. The AQS underlines it: the test does not have to be repeated every two years.

Why nobody told you

These four rules have been published for between seven months and three years. They sit on the official AQS page, in English, inside boxes headed Precautions, at the foot of the tables for each step.

There is no conspiracy. There is something more banal and harder to fix: these are low-salience notices. They carry no headline, they repeat no keywords, they occupy no block of their own. No search engine weights them. No AI that searches finds them. They surface only if somebody sits down and reads the whole page, top to bottom, knowing there is something there.

That is what we do here. And that is why this Journal exists.
