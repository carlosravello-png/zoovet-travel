Standing rule · In-cabin transport

IATA publishes its first manual for pets in the cabin: what it really requires, and why your export still rests with the country

On 15 July 2026, IATA published Edition 1 of its guidelines for pets in the cabin. The headlines said «new rules». They are not rules, and they change nothing prior: it is the first manual, and it is made of recommendations. Verified against the official document, read in full, on 22 July 2026.

By Carlos Ravello · Veterinary review: Dr Jessica Camacho García (DVM · CMVP 12434) · Published 22 July 2026 · Primary source read in full

⚠ Three things this manual does NOT change

1. It is not a law: they are recommendations to airlines. The final say still belongs to your airline and to the destination country.

2. It is only about the cabin —a small pet, a passenger flight—. It does not touch the cargo hold or international export.

3. It sets no weight, size or breed limit: that is still defined by each airline, one by one.

The fact, in one sentence: on 15 July 2026 IATA published Edition 1 of its operational guidelines for pets in the cabin —its first standardised manual—. They are not binding rules and change no prior regulation: they are recommendations to airlines (CR1 container, booking 48–72 hours ahead with the PETC code, documents before check-in), and IATA itself defers to each state's requirements for quarantine, breed bans and import. At Zoovet Travel we have exported pets internationally since 2013, and that is why we say it plainly: this tidies the door of the cabin for small pets; your export —cargo hold, a large dog, a border crossing— still rests with the veterinarian and with each country's regulations.

What it is (and what it is not)

IATA is not a regulator: it is an association of airlines, and this document says so in its own way —it adds clarity without constraining each airline's policy decisions—. Its very structure gives it away: every section closes with a block titled «Recommendation to the airline», with checkboxes. It is a best-practice manual, not an obligation. The text even separates by colour the stages of the journey that are the airline's responsibility —booking, check-in, boarding, flight— from those managed by others: the airport and, above all, the state.

The practical consequence is direct: a passenger can lean on these guidelines to demand transparency, but cannot assume they apply the same everywhere. Two powers that IATA does not replace still rule over the pet in the cabin: the airline —its contract of carriage, which sets price, per-flight quota and weight limits— and the state of origin, transit and destination, with its health and import requirements.

What the manual establishes, in concrete terms

Read in full, the document sets out reasonable procedures for the in-cabin travel of cats and dogs that fit within Container Requirement 1 (CR1) of IATA's Live Animals Regulations (LAR) —that is, a carrier that lets the animal stand, sit erect, lie in a natural position and turn about normally while standing, and that fits entirely under the seat in front—. Operationally:

  • Advance booking, generally mandatory between 48 and 72 hours before departure, registered with the service code PETC («Pet in Cabin»). The manual itself warns that many agencies and search engines do not support that code: you must book directly with the airline.
  • Documentation before check-in: a valid health certificate, vaccination records, permits and the airline's own forms. Responsibility for complying with all of this, the document stresses, rests with the passenger.
  • Sedation discouraged: the manual recommends communicating that sedation is discouraged unless prescribed by a veterinarian and that, if used, it requires documentation (a Record of Administration).
  • At check-in, trained staff assess the pet and the container; if the animal appears unwell, distressed, excessively lethargic or shows unacceptable behaviour, boarding must be denied.
  • Onboard, the pet stays inside the carrier at all times, and the carrier stowed during taxi, take-off, landing and turbulence.
  • At destination, the passenger presents the pet at customs or the veterinary border control point, where they verify its identity, inspect the carrier and review passport, certificates, vaccinations and import permits.

Why now: demand that soars and rules that fragment

IATA did not publish this in a vacuum. Its 2025 Global Passenger Survey found that around a quarter of travellers have flown with a pet or would consider it, and a widespread confusion: 41% did not know whether their pet even qualifies to fly, 36% were unaware of airline policies and 34% did not understand the procedure. «A pet is a much-loved member of the family», summed up Brendan Sullivan, IATA's Global Head of Cargo, defending common practices that deliver a «safer and more consistent» experience.

Added to that demand is a real fragmentation of national rules. Italy is the most visible case: in May 2025, its aviation authority ENAC published guidelines to admit pets in the cabin above 8 kg, opening the door to large dogs beside their owner —something until then reserved for the cargo hold in most markets—. When each country moves at its own pace, the traveller is left with an uneven map; what IATA is trying to do is give that map a common vocabulary (CR1, PETC), not a single rule.

What matters to you, the one exporting your pet: what this manual does NOT cover

Here is the point no headline made, and it is the one that decides whether this affects you. The document deals with the cabin: a small pet, in its carrier under the seat, on a passenger flight. It does not touch the ground where international export lives. And you do not have to infer it: the manual itself says so.

In its eligibility section, IATA asks that each airline's policy be harmonised with the regulatory requirements of the states of origin, transit and destination —that is, it defers to each country's rules—. And at the arrival stage it is even more explicit: it warns that quarantine requirements vary by country and depend on origin, vaccination status, species and import requirements; and that there may be restrictions such as breed-specific entry bans and limits on the number of pets per traveller, recommending that you check the destination's current regulations before travelling.

Translated to your case, exactly the three things that define an export fall outside this document:

  • The cargo hold, where the dog that is too big for the cabin travels —the typical export scenario—.
  • Weight, size and breed limits, which are still defined airline by airline: if your dog is brachycephalic (Bulldog, Pug, Boxer) or exceeds the cabin weight, your reality is set by your carrier's policy, not by this manual.
  • The import requirements of the destination country —rabies serology (FAVN/RFFIT), health certificates, deadlines, microchip, quarantine—, which, as IATA itself acknowledges, each state sets.

Put simply: IATA harmonised the door of the cabin; the export of your pet is still the terrain of a veterinarian and of each country's rules —two things a best-practice manual for airlines does not replace—.

Zoovet Travel's reading

If you are going to fly with a small pet, in the cabin, this manual is good news: it gives you a common language to demand clarity and a reasonable checklist (CR1 container, 48–72 h booking with the PETC code, documents before check-in). Use it as a reference tool —and even so, always confirm with your airline and with the destination country, which are the ones with the final say—.

If you are going to export your pet —cargo hold, a large dog, an international border crossing—, this announcement changes little of what is yours. Your journey is decided where it always is: in the veterinary process and in country-by-country regulatory compliance. That is where expert guidance stops being a luxury and becomes what separates a trip that goes well from one that ends with the animal held at the border.
