Editorial · Australia

The history of animal quarantine in Australia (and why it is not about your dog)

Australia is the hardest destination in the world to travel to with a pet, and the reason is not rabies. It is a law from 1908, two sections of the Constitution and a virus that walked out the service door in 2007. This is the whole story, and why we end up grateful for it.

By Carlos Ravello · Veterinary review: Dr. Jessica Camacho García (DVM · CMVP 12434) · Published 26 July 2026 · Editorial: signed analysis and opinion, built on verified primary sources

The idea, in one sentence: the thirty days Australia charges a dog or a cat arriving from Peru do not measure the animal's risk, which was already managed at origin through the 180-day wait that follows the rabies antibody titre; they measure the strength of its paperwork. The reduction to ten days cannot be bought: it is earned with the signature of an official government veterinarian in the exporting country who verifies the animal's identity before the blood is drawn. Everything that follows —the 1908 Act, section 51(ix) of the Constitution, the 2007 outbreak, the Mickleham facility— explains how it got there.

When responsibility matters more than an Olympic Games

Australia is by far the hardest country in the world to take a pet to. The file starts being assembled almost a year in advance and, even doing everything right, the animal ends up confined for thirty days —ten, if absolutely every piece of paperwork came out perfect— in a government facility on the outskirts of Melbourne before its family can touch it. The question I always get is the same: why so much?

The answer has nothing to do with rabies, or with your dog, or with your veterinarian. It has to do with a law from 1908 and with a handful of episodes that cost Australia dearly. And the first of the examples I describe below was not even about dogs: it was about competition horses, of the highest commercial and genetic value, that never arrived.

Melbourne, 1956. The Olympic Games belong to Australia for the first time in history, and the equestrian events are run in Stockholm, fifteen thousand kilometres away. It was not a money problem or a logistics problem. It was the quarantine law: Australia required six months of pre-shipment quarantine for any horse wishing to enter, and no team in the world was going to lock up its horses for half a year to compete for two weeks. The decision was taken in 1953, three years before the cauldron was lit. The equestrian events were held in Stockholm in June; the rest of the Games, in Melbourne, between November and December. An entire country giving up part of its own Games rather than touching its animal import regime. That is the measure of what we are talking about.

Seventy years later that door is still in place, but it no longer swings open and shut. Now it opens by millimetres, and the millimetre depends on where you are coming from. A dog leaving Auckland enters without spending a single day in quarantine. One leaving Osaka spends ten. One leaving Lyon spends thirty, or spends ten, and what decides the difference is not in the dog: it is in whether an official of the French government ran the microchip scanner over its back, with his own hands, before the blood was drawn. Same animal, same virus, different piece of paper.

Between the horse that stayed on the ground and that official with the scanner in his hand there is a curve of more than a century that almost nobody tells. Australia began by closing the door with a hammer and today closes it with a scalpel. In between there is a disaster with an exact date.

This never started with rabies

One assumes that a country obsessed with quarantine lives in fear of rabies, and with Australia it is the other way round: quarantine existed long before, and for another reason. The department of agriculture says so itself in the rabies review it published in January 2023: Australia is free of the virus, the last suspected case in animals on record is from 1867, and the only two modern human cases —1987 and 1990— were acquired overseas. One hundred and fifty-nine years without the disease. Today it is one of the very few countries on the planet that can say that.

What built the machinery was livestock, which is what the country lived on.

Sheep scab and contagious bovine pleuropneumonia were causing such concern in the flocks and herds that, by the 1870s, the need for quarantine had become self-evident. There was foot-and-mouth disease in 1872. There was rinderpest in Western Australia in 1923, diagnosed, contained and eradicated through testing and slaughter —so well handled that the veterinarian in charge was later appointed Director of Veterinary Hygiene at the federal Department of Health, in charge of animal quarantine for the whole country. There was Newcastle disease in poultry in 1930 and again in 1932. And in 1950 a small consignment of Suffolk sheep arrived in Victoria from the United Kingdom and failed to thrive; nobody could reach a diagnosis until a CSIRO veterinarian suggested looking for scrapie, it came back positive and an eradication programme had to be mounted. A country that lived off wool and meat learned early, and the hard way, that a single careless import could cost it the entire economy.

That is why, when the colonies came together in 1901 to form the country, quarantine was among the very first things the states handed over to the central government. And they did not hand it over by decree or by custom: it is written into the Constitution. Section 51 lists the matters on which the federal Parliament may legislate, and paragraph (ix) of that list is a single word: quarantine. Section 69 goes further and requires that each state's quarantine department pass into Commonwealth hands on a date to be proclaimed by the Governor-General. On that scaffolding the Quarantine Act of 1908 —number 3 of that year— was built, receiving royal assent on 30 March 1908.

That constitutional detail is the one that never appears in the guides and explains more than anything else. In the Australian division of powers, quarantine appears twice: as a power to legislate and as an administrative apparatus that the states physically hand over. We are not talking about modern bureaucracy but about founding material, written in the very years the country was learning to be a country.

The day it failed

24 August 2007. The New South Wales Department of Primary Industries confirms equine influenza in its territory.

The virus had entered through the Eastern Creek quarantine station in Sydney, where four Japanese racing stallions had arrived at the beginning of the month. But the virus did not leave riding a horse. It left, by every indication, on the clothes, the hands or the equipment of someone who worked inside: a stablehand, a veterinarian, a farrier. Someone crossed the gate with the virus on them and did not know it.

By 30 August there were already 488 confirmed cases on 41 properties and another 1,646 suspected on a hundred more. In Australia horse racing is not a pastime but an economic sector, and the sector stopped dead: hundreds of millions of dollars lost and mare coverings falling thirteen per cent that season.

Nine days after the first confirmed case, on 2 September, the government commissioned the inquiry to Ian Callinan, a retired High Court judge. The report was released on 12 June 2008 and its conclusion is more uncomfortable than any headline: Callinan could not establish precisely how the virus reached the general horse population, nor fix responsibility or direct blame on anyone, but he did determine that the virus most probably left the Eastern Creek quarantine station on a contaminated person or piece of equipment. Thirty-eight recommendations. The government accepted them all.

Three months later, in September 2008, came the Beale review, One biosecurity: a working partnership, and with the outbreak still fresh the country set about rewriting its quarantine law from top to bottom. The result was the Biosecurity Act, which received assent on 16 June 2015. The old Quarantine Act of 1908 was repealed in full exactly one year later, on 16 June 2016, after one hundred and eight years of service.

So Australia did not harden its border out of island paranoia. It hardened it because one day a virus walked out its service door, it cost a fortune, and it decided it would not happen again. There is an almost uncomfortable honesty in that: they sent a High Court judge to investigate them and published what the judge said about them.

The days were never about your dog

Here is the knot of the whole thing, and it is where most people ask the wrong question.

When a European or American dog boards the plane for Melbourne, it has already served a 180-day wait that starts counting from the day the laboratory received its blood sample for the rabies antibody titre. Six months. Which means the bulk of the biological risk was managed over there, on the ground, before take-off, and not in Australia.

So if that is already done, what gets resolved in those thirty days in Melbourne, or in those ten? One single thing: whether the file is telling the truth.

The baseline number is not ten, it is thirty. Thirty days is what any dog or cat arriving from a Group 3 country gets by default, and that is where Peru sits. The ten days are not the rule: they are a reduction, and it has to be earned. It is earned in one way only. An official government veterinarian in the exporting country has to verify the animal's identity —scan the microchip himself— at a separate visit, before the blood is drawn for the antibody titre. Separate, not the same day, not in passing. If that visit did not happen, or did not happen in that order, it is thirty days and there is no appeal.

Australia does not distrust your dog; it distrusts the paperwork. And it says so without euphemism: when it reviewed the regime in 2023, the reasons it put in writing were the increase in import volumes, the growing commercialisation of the pet trade and the increase in detected and suspected fraudulent certification, in Australia and in many other countries. Translated: somebody, somewhere, was forging pet documents. Australia responded by tightening the only screw that really matters, which is the proof that the animal is who the paper says it is.

The dates, because in regulatory work the dates are the data. After the 2013 review, the department itself puts it in one line: post-arrival quarantine for dogs and cats from approved countries was reduced to ten days. Then came the rabies review, whose final report was published on 12 January 2023 and whose measures took effect on 1 March 2023.

Look at the movement, because it is finer than it seems at first glance. The floor came down from thirty to ten, yes, but the thirty came back. Not for everyone: only for whoever cannot prove their animal's identity. Australia did not loosen up; what it did was move the punishment from biology to paperwork.

And if anyone wants to see the logic laid bare, look at how Canberra classifies the United States. The mainland is in Group 3, the strict verification group. Hawaii and Guam, which are territory of the same country and live under the same federal laws, are in Group 2, the preferential group. One passport, two regimes.

And before anyone rushes to say the difference is islands, it is worth looking at the rest of the list: Puerto Rico is in Group 3. The Virgin Islands, both the U.S. and the British ones, too. The Northern Marianas, too. Being an island is not enough. What Australia looks at is not the map or the flag on the passport, but the rabies history of that specific jurisdiction and what it can demonstrate about it. That is why Christmas Island, which is Australian territory, appears in Group 2 and not in the full-trust group: every piece of land answers for itself.

By that criterion, the list of those who enter without a single day of quarantine fits on one line: New Zealand, Norfolk Island and the Cocos Islands, and the last of these only for cats. Australia does not call them its own territories in its table: it calls them Group 1 countries, each with its own entry. Trust is not inherited through sovereignty. It is earned.

What it costs and who pays for it

All this architecture has a price, and Australia neither hides nor disguises it. The single facility through which the quarantine of every dog and cat in the country now passes is at Mickleham, on the outskirts of Melbourne, and it was built with a commitment of 379.9 million Australian dollars over seven years. It opened on 26 October 2015 and received its first dogs and cats on 23 November that year.

Fourteen days before that, on 9 November 2015, Eastern Creek received its last arrival of animals. The same station the virus had escaped from in 2007. It closed at the end of that year and in its place a single new door remained.

For the importer the bill is made up of three parts, and these are the ones the department publishes —fees updated on 29 August 2025—:

The official justification is a dry sentence that sums up the country's entire philosophy: the system works on cost recovery, whoever uses it pays for it, and that reduces the burden on the Australian taxpayer. If you bring your dog, the State looks after it and looks after it well. The bill is yours, not that of the lady in Adelaide who will never in her life leave the continent.

Johnny Depp's dogs

21 April 2015. Johnny Depp lands in a private jet with his two Yorkshire terriers, Pistol and Boo, and nobody declares them on the passenger card.

The system did not detect them, and it is worth spelling this out: they were not caught by customs, or by a scanner, or by a detector dog. They were caught by Facebook. On Saturday 9 May the two Yorkshire terriers went through Happy Dogz Grooming, a dog grooming salon on the Gold Coast, and the salon did what every grooming salon in the world does: it posted a photo of the freshly bathed clients.

By 13 May the government had been notified and the dogs located, and on the table was a word nobody wants to read in a file: euthanasia. The next day, the 14th, the then agriculture minister Barnaby Joyce said it in front of the cameras, with some fifty hours' notice and without a single diplomatic concession: either they go, or we put them down. On 15 May the dogs were back in the air.

On 18 April 2016 Amber Heard pleaded guilty to falsifying documents and the illegal importation charges were dropped. There was no fine and no conviction recorded: the court imposed a one-month good behaviour bond of one thousand Australian dollars, money payable only in the event of a breach. And then came the most uncomfortable apology video in the history of cinema.

The anecdote is not worth telling for the celebrity. It is worth telling because the system did not make half an exception for one of the most famous men on the planet, and because in not making it, it sent a message that reached every corner of the world in forty-eight hours. It is probably the most effective biosecurity communication campaign any country has ever had, and it did not cost a cent.

What I think about all this

Those of us who make a living moving animals around the world have every right to complain about Australia, and we do. It is the hardest destination there is. An Australian file is prepared almost a year in advance, it rests on the signatures of officials who sometimes do not even know the procedure you are asking them for exists, and one miscounted date sends the whole process back to day zero. There are families who give up along the way. I have seen them.

And even so, every time I close an Australian file I am better at what I do. Because Australia forces you to do properly what in other destinations can be done quickly: it forces the microchip to be implanted before the first vaccine, it forces a government to answer with its signature for an animal's identity, it forces an antibody titre to be real and not a photocopy with a stamp. It sets the bar extremely high and, in doing so, it raises the floor of the whole trade.

Behind that bar there is no contempt for your dog. There is a continent that still does not know rabies, that lost part of its own Olympic Games rather than risk it, that investigated itself with a High Court judge the day it failed, and that charges the full bill rather than passing the cost on to its own people. That is not bureaucracy. That is a country that decided, at the very moment it was inventing itself, that its environment is worth more than anybody's convenience, including its own.

I wish more countries made our lives this difficult.
