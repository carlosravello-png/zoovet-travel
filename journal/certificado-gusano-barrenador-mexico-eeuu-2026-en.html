Standing rule · Mexico–United States crossing

Nobody asks for the screwworm certificate to enter Mexico: they ask for it to get back into the United States

It is a U.S. requirement, in force since 9 July 2025, obtained in Mexico before crossing and signed by a private veterinarian authorised by SENASICA. It also applies to U.S. dogs that merely went on a trip. SENASICA publishes the official directory of those who may sign it: we read it in full on 26 July 2026 and it lists 764 veterinarians nationwide, three of them in Ciudad Juárez. Verified against the official pages of APHIS, CBP, CDC and SENASICA.

By Carlos Ravello · Veterinary review: Dr. Jessica Camacho García (DVM · CMVP 12434) · Published 26 July 2026 · Four health authorities read at source

⚠ Four things before you cross with your dog

1. The document is obtained in Mexico, before you return. It is not a Mexican entry formality: it is a United States requirement for admission.

2. The window is five days: the inspection must take place within the five days before shipment.

3. Not just any veterinarian can sign it. SENASICA publishes the official directory of those who can: check it before you fix your travel date. There are 764 nationwide, and in some border cities barely three.

4. It is additional to, not a substitute for, the CDC Dog Import Form. The dog must also be six months old and microchipped.

The fact, in one sentence: the screwworm freedom certificate is not required to enter Mexico, but to bring a dog into the United States from Mexico. It is a U.S. requirement in force since 9 July 2025, obtained on Mexican soil from a Médico Veterinario Responsable Autorizado (authorised responsible veterinarian) accredited by SENASICA, the inspection must take place within the five days before shipment, and it also covers U.S.-origin dogs returning from a trip. In the opposite direction, to enter Mexico from the United States or Canada, SENASICA asks for no health certificate and no vaccination record: only a physical inspection at the point of entry. At Zoovet Travel we have been exporting pets internationally since 2013, and this is the kind of asymmetry that costs dearly: the owner who prepares paperwork for the wrong country arrives at the border holding the document nobody asked for, and without the one they will.

The direction of travel, which is what almost nobody has clear

Every health requirement has two elements: who demands it and at which point of the journey. When either is lost in translation, the requirement stops being useful and becomes a source of anxiety. That is exactly what has happened with the screwworm certificate since June 2026: it has circulated as "a new piece of paper for pets at the border", without saying which side of the border it applies to.

The answer, read on the official pages of both countries, is unambiguous. The certificate is demanded by the United States, for the entry of dogs coming from a region affected by screwworm — Mexico is one. Mexico does not demand it, and it is not requested on entry into Mexico. It is obtained in Mexico because that is where the animal is before crossing: the country of departure certifies, the country of arrival demands. That is the logic of any export health certificate, and it is what breaks when the requirement is reported without a direction.

The confusion has a concrete and expensive consequence: there are owners preparing Mexican paperwork to "enter Mexico" with their dog when what they need is Mexican paperwork to leave for the United States. And there are U.S. owners who cross for a weekend in Mexico convinced that, because the dog is theirs and is from there, the return is automatic. It is not.

What the United States requires, exactly

The measure sits in the import alert issued by APHIS — the animal and plant health inspection service of the U.S. Department of Agriculture — applicable to Mexico for screwworm, updated and effective as of 9 July 2025. That alert covers cattle, horses, bison, dogs and cats; bovine germplasm, sheep, goats and swine remain prohibited.

For dogs, the official text asks for a health certificate stating two things. First, that the animal was inspected within five days prior to shipment to the United States. And second, that it is free from screwworm, or was found to be infested with screwworm, held in quarantine, and treated until free from screwworm prior to the certificate being issued and the dog being exported. In other words: a previous infestation does not disqualify the animal, provided the treatment is documented and complete.

The APHIS page on bringing dogs into the United States, last modified 1 April 2026, adds the piece most people overlook: the requirement includes dogs that are returning. A dog born, vaccinated and resident in the United States that crossed into Mexico for a few days enters, on the way back, as a dog coming from an affected region. There is no exception for the animal's nationality or for the brevity of the trip.

To that is added, on the CDC side, what already applied for rabies: the Dog Import Form receipt, an animal that appears healthy and free of lesions or wounds, six months of age, and a microchip. Mexico is classified by the CDC as a low-risk country for canine rabies, so no rabies serology is required; but a dog vaccinated in the United States that has been in a high-risk country in the previous six months also needs U.S.-issued rabies vaccination certification. These are requirements that add up, not ones that replace each other.

Who may sign it: three official pages, three wordings

Here is the technical point that decides whether the paperwork works or not, and where the official sources do not say quite the same thing. It is worth seeing them side by side, because the difference is not a nuance.

Official sourceDateWho may sign
CBP national media release26 June 2026A "full-time salaried government veterinary official" from the region of origin
APHIS — dog imports, Mexico section1 April 2026A "clinical veterinarian trained and authorized to sign on behalf of the official government veterinarian"
SENASICA — if you travel to the United States24 July 2026A Médico Veterinario Responsable Autorizado (MVRA) holding a current SENASICA accreditation and official screwworm training

The three wordings reconcile, but only if they are read in order. CBP's is the general rule for any affected region in the world: the signatory must be a state veterinary official. APHIS's is the Mexico-specific exception: there, a clinical veterinarian may indeed sign, provided they are trained and authorised to do so on behalf of the official veterinarian. And SENASICA's is the one that names that figure by its real designation in the Mexican system.

SENASICA published it on its page dated 24 July 2026, and it is the operational definition that was missing: the certificate must be issued by a Médico Veterinario Responsable Autorizado in veterinary clinics and hospitals that market products for animal use or consumption, and that veterinarian must hold a current MVRA accreditation in the relevant area, issued by SENASICA itself, together with the SENASICA training course "Gusano barrenador del ganado, identificación y diagnóstico general" (cattle screwworm: identification and general diagnosis).

From which follows what no headline explained: not just any veterinarian will do. Your trusted vet, however good, cannot sign this document without those two credentials.

An official directory does exist, and it is the tool nobody is using

The question that follows — "so how do I know whether mine is authorised?" — has a public answer that has barely been circulated. SENASICA maintains an official directory titled Médicos Veterinarios Responsables Autorizados que pueden dictaminar la ausencia de GBG en perros (authorised responsible veterinarians who may certify the absence of screwworm in dogs), subtitled "Certificate for travel to the USA". It is the same list APHIS points to from its own dog import page. The current version is dated 24 July 2026, runs to 28 pages and records, for each veterinarian, state, municipality, name, notification e-mail and telephone number.

We downloaded it and read it in full on 26 July 2026. It contains 764 authorised veterinarians across the country's 32 states. The distribution across the six states bordering the United States — which is where this paperwork is actually played out — is as follows:

That is the real bottleneck in this story, and it appears in no regulation: the requirement has existed since July 2025, but the capacity to comply with it is being built now, city by city. The case of Ciudad Juárez shows it starkly. It is one of the busiest pet crossings on the continent, and in the directory dated 24 July 2026 exactly three veterinarians appear with an address in the municipality of Juárez — records 105, 106 and 107. This matches what the city's veterinary association stated on 23 July 2026 through Margarita Peña: three accredited veterinarians in the whole city, and a course planned for the end of August to certify more. According to that same source, the cost is 1,500 pesos, the document is valid for five days, and U.S. authorities ask for one per animal.

The Chihuahua figure is worth reading carefully: fifteen authorised veterinarians in the entire state, of whom three are in Juárez and the rest are spread between the state capital, Cuauhtémoc, Camargo and Hidalgo del Parral. Across the map, Nuevo León has seventy-three. The practical consequence is simple: how difficult this paperwork is depends far more on where you live than on what the rule says. Consulting the directory before fixing your travel date — and not after — is, today, the difference between a week's formality and a rescheduled trip.

What Mexico requires, exactly

The contrast is stark enough to be stated plainly. SENASICA also updated its entry-requirements page on 24 July 2026, and for a dog or cat with origin in Mexico, the United States or Canada and provenance from the United States or Canada, the official text says that on arrival the pet will be subject to a physical inspection to confirm there are no health risks, and that it is not necessary to present a Certificate of Good Health or a vaccination record. The same procedure applies for entry through the border strip.

There is no screwworm certificate on that list. There is no certificate of anything. What there is, is a formality at the point of entry — the Certificado Zoosanitario para Importación (CZI), requested on arrival at SENASICA offices in ports, airports and border crossings — and a physical inspection with rules of its own that are worth knowing, because that is where journeys get stuck:

  • The carrier must be clean, and will receive a preventive spray treatment from official staff.
  • Not permitted inside the crate: bedding, nappies, newspaper, sawdust or rags, nor toys, treats or other items made with ruminant-origin ingredients. If presented, they are removed and destroyed. The collar and lead may stay on.
  • If ectoparasites are detected, the owner must contact a veterinarian for treatment before release, at their own expense. If ticks are detected, staff additionally verify that every specimen is removed.

For pets coming from other countries — not the United States or Canada — a Certificate of Good Health is indeed required, valid for a maximum of 15 days, in original and copy, with exporter and importer details, the date and validity of the rabies vaccination (animals under three months are exempt), confirmation that the animal was found clinically healthy at the pre-travel inspection and that it was treated for internal and external parasites within the previous six months. And for those who travel to Mexico often, more than three times a year, there is the Frequent Traveller Pet Programme.

The export health certificate is a different document, and the United States does not ask for it

Two documents that are confused all the time deserve separating, because both are Mexican, both are sanitary and both serve to leave the country.

The Certificado Zoosanitario para Exportación (CZE) is SENASICA's official procedure — reference Senasica-05-001-B — for exporting a pet. According to the page dated 7 April 2026, it is free of charge, valid for eight calendar days (which may vary if the destination country sets a different period), requested at authorised offices on presentation of, among other things, a good-health certificate issued by a private veterinarian no more than five days earlier, answered within three working days, and bearing two stamps: the first on issue, without the animal needing to be present, and the second on the day of travel, at the SENASICA office at the point of departure, after a physical examination of the pet.

That document is not the one the United States asks for. SENASICA itself says so in as many words on its page of 24 July 2026: to travel with pets to the USA, you do not need to obtain an Export Health Certificate. What is required is the health certificate with the screwworm freedom declaration, signed by the MVRA. One is free and lasts eight days; the other is paid for and lasts five. They are not interchangeable, and asking for the wrong one costs you the trip.

Where the veterinarian looks, and why the examination is not a counter formality

Screwworm — Cochliomyia hominivorax — is not a parasite detected by a laboratory test: it is detected by looking. The fly lays its eggs in open wounds or on mucous membranes, and the larvae feed on living tissue. That is why the whole certification rests on a properly conducted physical examination, and why in December 2025 APHIS published specific guidance for veterinarians treating dogs and cats — document APHIS-25-038 — listing where to look:

  • Skin folds around the face, eyes and neck.
  • Mouth and ears, including the internal ear canals.
  • Perianal and genital areas.
  • Nail beds and paws.
  • Abdomen and navel.
  • Areas of excessive licking and recent surgical sites.

The guidance is explicit that the certificate must confirm the dog was inspected for screwworm within the five days before travel and found free of infestation. And here is a detail that matters to anyone travelling with cats: the clinical guidance covers dogs and cats — both species are susceptible — but the certification requirement for entry is today for dogs only. This is consistent with what the Juárez veterinary association stated on 23 July 2026: at present it is asked only for dogs, not cats. It is an asymmetry to watch, not to assume permanent.

Hence too the practical advice we give our clients: if your dog has a fresh or healing wound, a recent surgery or a persistent lick spot, deal with it beforehand, with time to spare. This is not a bureaucratic box that can be ticked by insisting at the counter; it is the very object of the inspection.

Why people spoke of "new rules" in June

The measure dates from July 2025, but the noise arrived a year later, and there is a verifiable explanation. On 3 June 2026 the first screwworm case in an animal inside the United States was confirmed. Following that announcement the USDA suspended pet dog exports to Mexico, and on 10 June it reversed the suspension: dogs came off the restricted-species list and could travel again according to previously established procedures. Twenty-four hours of closure and reopening are enough to generate a wave of coverage.

Then, on 26 June 2026, Customs and Border Protection published a national media release whose own title defines it as a reminder for travellers crossing the Mexican border with dogs. The text restates the APHIS and CDC requirements and closes by warning that its personnel will strictly enforce them. A forceful reminder of a rule already in force looks, from the outside, a great deal like a new rule; and that is how it was reported in several places. On 30 June 2026 the formula "new rules for crossing the border with pets because of screwworm" circulated in border media, naming neither an agency nor an effective date. Later coverage corrected the framing: by 23 July it was being reported, correctly, that on crossing into the United States you may be asked for the certificate and that it is issued by veterinarians accredited with SENASICA.

The distinction is not cosmetic. If it is a new rule, one expects a grace period and an effective date. If it is a rule that has been in force for a year and is now being enforced strictly, there is no grace period to wait for: you need the document.

What is genuinely new, and worth following

The requirement did not change. What changed is the epidemiological context, and that does move week by week. With dates and sources:

  • The CDC, in its situation summary reviewed on 23 June 2026, reported 16 domestically acquired animal cases as of 22 June, no domestically acquired human cases, and one travel-associated human case following a stay in El Salvador in August 2025. Its assessment: the risk to people remains very low.
  • The Texas Standard tracker, updated 16 July 2026 at 7:00 p.m. CDT with USDA data, counted 41 confirmed infestations: 40 in Texas and one in New Mexico, the most recent in a young calf in western Starr County, the first detection in the Rio Grande Valley.
  • The APHIS status page, last modified 22 July 2026, keeps all southern ports of entry closed to livestock trade, a closure announced in July 2025, with no reopening date.
  • Mexico has been declared affected by screwworm since November 2024: SENASICA refers to the health alert issued by APHIS on 24 November 2024.

Translated into decisions: the port closure affects livestock, not pets. Pets cross, with their certificate. But the trend in the counts is what determines whether tomorrow the requirement is tightened, extended to cats or widened to more states of origin. That is the part to watch, and the reason a note like this is revised, not filed away.

What we do not know

We also publish what we could not verify, because in regulatory matters a gap that is flagged is worth more than a convenient figure.

  • SENASICA's directory is a snapshot, not a live register. The 764 authorisations we counted correspond to the file dated 24 July 2026; SENASICA republishes it with a new date from time to time and does not state how often. With the Ciudad Juárez course announced for the end of August, that city's figure will change. Always consult the day's version, not ours.
  • Being in the directory is not the same as being available. The list gives state, municipality, name, e-mail and telephone, but does not say whether the veterinarian is currently handling this procedure, or with what lead time. Ask to see the current MVRA accreditation and the screwworm training certificate before paying, all the same.
  • The 1,500-peso cost is attributed to the Juárez veterinary association, which describes it as standardised nationally by SENASICA. We could not locate an official SENASICA publication setting or confirming that fee, so we give it as a sourced statement, not as official data.
  • Case counts change daily and figures differ between agencies and outlets depending on the cut-off date. Every number in this note carries its date and its source precisely for that reason; none should be read as today's number.
  • We do not know whether the requirement will extend to cats. The APHIS clinical guidance already covers them and the species is susceptible, but entry certification remains dogs only. It is a plausible change, not an announced one.

What to do, concretely

If you are crossing into the United States with your dog from Mexico, whether by road or by air, and whether your dog is Mexican or a U.S. dog going home:

  • Open SENASICA's directory and find the veterinarian before fixing your travel date, not after. Look up your state and municipality in the list dated 24 July 2026; in some cities there are very few, and the five-day window leaves no room for improvisation. At the appointment, ask to see the MVRA accreditation and the screwworm training certificate.
  • Schedule the inspection within the five days before shipment, counting back from the crossing, and no earlier: a certificate issued too far in advance is worthless.
  • Check your dog yourself a week ahead: fresh or healing wounds, recent surgery, lick spots, ears, paws and skin folds. If there is anything, treat it before booking the appointment.
  • Do not let go of the rest of the paperwork. The CDC Dog Import Form receipt remains mandatory, as do the microchip and the six months of age. The screwworm certificate is added to that.
  • Carry one certificate per animal. If you travel with two dogs, that is two certificates.
  • If the trip runs the other way — into Mexico from the United States or Canada — do not spend on paperwork nobody will ask for: prepare instead a clean, empty crate, with no bedding, newspaper or ruminant-origin toys, and expect a physical inspection on arrival.

And one final professional observation. Commercial websites have appeared offering the certificate online. Before paying any of them, verify that there is a veterinarian behind it holding both SENASICA credentials and that the animal's physical examination genuinely takes place: without an in-person examination within the five days, the document does not meet what the U.S. rule requires, however well printed it may be. At a border, a certificate that does not comply is not half a formality: it is none.
